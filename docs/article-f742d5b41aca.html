At Westminster it is normal for MPs who defect from one party to another to resist calls for a byelection, partly by arguing that they were personally elected.

But in the Senedd elections in May, MSs (members of the Senedd) were for the first time elected by the closed proportional list system, with each constituency electing six members.

Sarah Cooper-Lesadd, the lead candidate for Reform UK, Pen-y-Bont Bro Morgannwg (Bridgend and Vale of Glamorgan) was elected as a direct result of people voting for Reform UK. But she will now sit as a Plaid Cymru MS and vote for Plaid policies.

Under this election system, Cooper-Lesadd cannot resign and trigger a byelection. If she were to resign, her seat would be taken by the next person on the Reform UK list in that constituency.

In a post on social media, Joe Martin, a Reform UK MS, said her position was “completely untenable”. He went on:

No one, literally no one, voted for her in May’s election. The votes that put her in Parliament were for Reform UK. Specifically, they were to scrap the nation of sanctuary, prevent the breakup of the UK, and to deliver Reform’s manifesto.

This means she has no mandate to represent those people any longer. The people of Pen-y-bont Bro Morgannog wanted a voice in Reform. Instead, they’ve been used as a springboard for the political ambitions of Sarah Cooper-Lesadd. That simply isn’t right.

If you believe in Plaid Cymru, stand for Plaid Cymru. Don’t deceive your way through a selection process to stand where you think is most opportune to realise your own ambitions. A party with any sense would drop her like a stone at the next election.

In a separate post, Matthew MacKinnon, Reform UK’s director of external affairs, said Cooper-Lesadd should resign.

She was elected on a list system under a Reform banner with a clear intention to pursue her own agenda and not @RUKWales

She was not elected as an independent.

She has shamefully manipulated the party and the electorate.

She should resign.

Pip more prone to abuse compared to equivalent disability benefits abroad due to design flaws, report says

The government is under pressure to cut the amount of money spent on disability benefits, particularly the personal independence payment, a non-means tested benefit that helps people with the costs they incur as a result of their disability.

In a report out today, Re:State, a centrist/right-leaning thinktank focusing on public sector reform, says the amount being spent on Pip is too high. It says other, equivalent benefits in prosperous democracies have much tighter rules and, as a result, work more effectively.

In particular, it highlights the fact that, while Pip claimants have to provide evidence of their disability, they do not have to provide evidence of the extra costs they are incurring as a result. “The requirement for evidence of extra costs is common in international models, but entirely absent from Britain’s model,” it says.

Britain stands alone on a number of fundamental decisions. Pip has been designed to maximise administrative simplicity at the cost of accuracy, transparency, and perception of fairness. No other country relies solely on a functional assessment to determine extra-cost entitlement, without requiring evidence of the costs the benefit is intended to help meet. This creates a system which is vulnerable to gaming, and poorly equipped to distinguish between different levels of genuine need.

Additionally, Pip’s broad definition of extra costs means that its daily living component overlaps substantially with services that other countries address through social care and local provision. Elsewhere, clearer boundaries allow different parts of the state to perform different functions and reduce the risk of paying twice for the same need.

In incapacity benefits, versus the comparison countries, Britain appears to have a low bar for access; a less rigorous application and assessment process; and much weaker work incentives. Some of the comparator countries also make much greater use of employer responsibilities, intended to prevent people requiring incapacity benefits in the first place. It appears highly likely these design choices contribute to a greater proportion of Britain’s working-age population claiming incapacity benefits and a much greater proportion claiming incapacity benefits without any conditionality, as well as Britain’s comparably high disability employment gap.

The lesson from international experience is therefore not simply that Britain spends too much or that other countries have better systems. It is that design choices have consequences. How need is defined, where responsibility sits, what evidence is required, and what incentives are created all affect caseloads, expenditure, employment, and public confidence.

Pepper spray in young offender institutions used disproprotionately against black boys, prison charity says

Last year the Home Office announced that prison officers would be allowed to use Pava, a form of pepper spray, to incapacitate children at young offender institutions.

Today the Howard League for Penal Reform, which opposed the move, has released figures showing that Pava has been used disproportionately against black boys. In a news release, it says:

In April 2025, Shabana Mahmood, the then secretary of state for justice, gave the green light for Pava spray to be used in three prisons in England, holding boys as young as 15 – Feltham A, Werrington and Wetherby. The Howard League understands that deployment began in August for Werrington and Wetherby and in September for Feltham A.

Figures provided to the Howard League in response to a Freedom of Information Act request show that in the first few months of deployment, up to the end of March 2026, Pava spray was drawn and used against 33 children and young people in total – 19 in Feltham A and 14 in Werrington. In Wetherby, Pava spray was drawn on at least one occasion but not used.

Of the 33 children and young people who had Pava spray used against them, 18 were Black and six were white. Despite comprising only about 30% of the population in Feltham A and Werrington, Black children and young people were on the receiving end about 55% of the time.

Gemma Abbott, legal director at the Howard League, said:

Pava is a chemical irritant spray that can cause severe pain, and it is classified as a prohibited weapon under the Firearms Act 1968. It is shameful that prison staff have been given the power to use it against children.

Burnham declines to commit to impose cap on amount individuals can donate to political parties

Andy Burnham has refused to commit to imposing a cap on the amount individuals can donate to political parties.

He has also declined to say whether he thinks the laws currently being passed by parliament, which will restrict the amount Britons can gave give to parties if they have recently move to the UK after living abroad, will result in Reform UK having to give back the £72m it has had from Ben Delo and Christopher Harborne, the two cryptocurrency billionaires.

Asked specifically if these donations would become impermissible, and if he would now back a cap on individual donations, Burnham replied:

The details of those donations are a matter for Reform, and they will have to account for the detail of how those donations have been made and whether they are permissible within the law. The government set out plans in the representation of the people bill to cap overseas donations. And that bill is going through parliament as we speak.

So it’s really important that we uphold the highest standards of trust, confidence, cleanliness in British politics. And that is incumbent on all politicians and all political parties.

Burnham said he was in favour of a cap on donations before he became PM. But many Labour-supporting union leaders do not back the idea, because they fear this could limit their ability to donate to the party, and Burnham has more recently backed away from the idea.

Andy Burnham has said the budget will be “challenging”.

In his pooled TV interview, asked if the budget would be “difficult”, Burnham replied:

It’s going to be challenging because the picture around the world is challenging and particularly the situation in the Middle East. And we will look carefully at all of those things.

We won’t take risks with people’s living standards or with the economy as a whole. So we will take it all into account.

It’s why we wanted to do an earlier budget. We didn’t want to leave a long period of speculation. I think that’s been shown now to be the right decision.

It will mean taking whatever action we can to help people while also making sure we apply the highest degree of prudence to the running of the economy.

Talking about the state of the economy generally, he said he thought it was resilient.

If you look at the the detailed picture underlying today’s inflation figures, it shows resilience across the board, actually, in the British economy.

Burnham said the inflationary pressures were “driven by the situation in the Middle East”, which was a problem for countries around the world.
