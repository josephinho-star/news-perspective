Republican Jim Jordan wins party election to become House minority leader of next Congress

Congressman Jim Jordan, a Republican hardliner from Ohio, has won the election to become the next House minority leader in Congress.

Jordan beat Representative Steve Scalise, the current majority leader, by 106-73 in a closed-door vote to lead Republicans from January. He replaces the current House speaker, Mike Jonson, who said he would not run when the GOP cedes control to Democrats in the new year.

Chuck Schumer remains confident he has enough votes for majority leader, while Bernie Sanders says he won't back him

Top Democrat Chuck Schumer said he is “confident” that he has enough votes to become Senate majority leader in the next Congress.

This comes after members of his caucus have said they are not prepared to back him as Democrats prepare to take control of the upper chamber in the new year.

On Tuesday, Bernie Sanders, the independent senator from Vermont who votes with Democrats, said that he would not support Schumer as speaker. “The time is now for change,” Sanders told reporters. “We need a vigorous debate about the direction of the Democratic party.”

Senator Elissa Slotkin, the first-term Democrat from Michigan who her 2024 won election despite Donald Trump carrying her state, said that she will need to see “a concrete plan that’s definitively different” in order to secure her vote. “I’m a no until and unless I see that from anyone, and frankly I suggest that my peers withhold quorum if there’s only going to be one candidate, withhold quorum until you get that plan,” Slotkin said.

The Michigan Democrat noted that she had a “very productive” conversation yesterday with Senator Chris Van Hollen, the progressive Democrat from Maryland, who said he is in “listening mode” about the calls for him to challenge Schumer for the majority leader position.

Slotkin added today that the intra-party battle is not between progressive and moderate, but rather “team fight versus team flight”.

She added: “Do you actually want to fight hard for the things that are going to help people, or do you kind of want to just sit there and look like you’re doing something?”

Republican John Thune wins re-election as Senate minority leader

Republican John Thune has won re-election to serve as Senate minority leader in the next Congress.

The GOP senator from North Dakota didn’t face a challenger in the closed-door vote, as Democrats prepare for at least a 10-seat majority in the new year.

“I appreciated the opportunity to have an open, honest, and transparent conversation with my colleagues today, and I am honored to have their continued trust and support,” Thune said in a statement, after upper chamber Republicans met on Capitol Hill today to strategize after their drubbing in the recent midterm elections. “We remain united in our commitment to advancing conservative priorities, working with President Trump and our colleagues in the House, and holding the line against Democrats’ efforts to enact their far-left, liberal agenda.”

US Senate race in Mississippi on knife’s edge as Democrats eye latest midterm pickup

With 97% of the votes counted, Democrat Scott Colom is leading Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,575 votes in the nail-biting US Senate race in Mississippi.

Democrats are eyeing the Magnolia state as their eighth pickup in a midterms cycle where Republicans were blown out of Congress.

The state counts any ballots received by 10 November, as long as they were postmarked by election day – so we hopefully expect a call soon.

On Capitol Hill today, Hyde-Smith told reporters that she’s feeling “very optimistic” despite the lag.

“They’re still counting. We’ve got a few thousand out, so we’re feeling very optimistic,” she said, but refused to answer why the contest is so close. A reminder that Mississippi hasn’t elected a Democratic US senator since 1982. If Colom wins he’d also be the first Black man elected to that position since the Reconstruction era, 145 years ago.

House oversight Republicans say government 'failed' survivors in final report on Epstein investigation

Earlier today, Republicans on the House oversight committee released a sharply critical 136-page report summarizing the findings of its 15-month-long investigation into the federal government’s handling of investigations into the late convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein and his accomplice, Ghislaine Maxwell.

Rebuking the government’s handling of the criminal case against Epstein and its release of the files related to it, the committee recommended that the Department of Justice renew its investigation of the disgraced financier’s associates.

“The federal government has failed the survivors of Jeffrey Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell’s horrific crimes for decades,” oversight chair James Comer said in a statement.

The committee found that the crimes committed by Epstein after 2005 “could have been prevented and justice served had it not been for the failures of investigators and federal prosecutors in South Florida and Washington DC.”

It also found that Donald Trump was not implicated in Epstein’s crimes and accused Democrats of being “obsessed” with trying to implicate the president.

The committee also accused the US and foreign governments, and major financial institutions, of failing to respond to “clear indicators of human trafficking and immigration fraud.” Despite identifying suspicious transactions that “bore hallmarks of human trafficking and illicit financial activity”, institutions allowed Epstein to “use fraudulent decoy flights and refundable or dummy itineraries to transport foreign women across borders directly to his residences,” the report says.

The report calls on the justice department to interview or reinterview “key witnesses” who were part of Epstein and Maxwell’s inner circle, to investigate further allegations, and to establish a proper process for survivors to access their un-redacted records.

It also urges Congress to pass legislation sponsored by Comer that would allow witnesses and survivors of sexual abuse to speak freely about it, by non-disclosure or confidentiality agreements.
