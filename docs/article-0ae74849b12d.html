Nancy Pelosi's office latest to receive 'suspicious' letter

Nancy Pelosi’s office received “a suspicious letter” on Wednesday in what appears to be the latest incident of ill-intentioned mailings targeted at US Congress members before the midterm elections.

Earlier this week, the Guardian reported that the offices of more than three dozen members of Congress across the political spectrum received envelopes containing white powder. One lawmaker’s office said preliminary tests indicated the powder was not hazardous.

A spokesperson for Pelosi said the letter was “consistent with mailings reported by other congressional offices” and that everyone in the office was safe.

“Speaker Pelosi remains focused on serving the people of San Francisco and is concerned for the members and staff whose offices have experienced similar incidents,” Nicholas Martin, a spokesperson for the speaker emerita, said in a statement.

“She is grateful for the efforts of the US Capitol Police, the San Francisco police department and other law enforcement agencies to thoroughly investigate this series of intimidation across America.”

More than 100 letters thought to contain white powder were reportedly thought to be circulating in the US mail system. Election offices across the US have also reported receiving suspicious mail.

The mailings are under investigation by the FBI, the Capitol police and the US Postal Inspection Service. The FBI declined to confirm “specific instances or letters”, but said in a statement earlier this week: “The FBI and US Postal Inspection Service are investigating a series of suspicious mailings sent to various addresses nationwide, including secretary of state offices and their related election offices, and congressional offices.”

Aipac, the most powerful pro-Israel lobby in the US, will cease issuing endorsements and directly contributing to candidates through its political action committee, according to a report from Jewish Insider.

Since 2022, the group has been directly involved with elections through its Pac and Super Pac and spent hundreds of millions of dollars on US races, though its brand has become increasingly toxic in recent years.

“To win the fight for the future of the country and the US-Israel alliance, we must build a more strategic, consequential, and nimble political operation. We will prioritize increasing direct, organized contributions from individual Americans to candidates – expanding upon our current efforts – and maximizing the emerging political fundraising tools,” Aipac leadership said in a letter, according to Jewish Insider.

“Endorsements, direct PAC contributions, and earmarking will no longer be part of our approach so we can expand individual giving and utilize new tools to help our friends win.”

Historically the group has spent most in Democratic primaries, and donated more to Democrats, rarely backing Republicans in competitive races during general elections. It emerged this week, however, that Aipac made a significant donation to Republican Mike Rogers, who is running against Abdul El-Sayed. The group, which spent tens of millions to defeat El-Sayed in the Democratic primary, plans to donate as much as $9m to support Rogers, the New York Times reported.

With 13 days until the midterms, early voting data is rolling in nationwide. Democrats need to net four Senate seats and hold 218 House seats to reclaim a congressional majority. Here is where the numbers stand so far:

Turnout is surging in key battlegrounds: Over 16 million Americans have already voted in person or by mail. According to the University of Florida’s Election Lab, battleground states -including Michigan, Ohio, Georgia, Texas, Florida and North Carolina – have each passed 700,000 ballots cast as of 21 October. Turnout is being driven in part by widespread voter frustration, reflected in Donald Trump’s record-low approval ratings.

Democrats lead in early returns: Registered Democrats account for 47% of early ballots cast, compared with 29% from Republicans (tracking returned ballots, not candidate selection). The party gap is most pronounced in North Carolina, Kansas and Iowa. While Republicans traditionally favor election day voting, strategists worry this widened margin signals a “red undertow” – GOP voters staying home due to frustration over high gas prices, the conflict in Iran and sweeping tariffs.

Early votes are pacing well ahead of 2022: In Georgia, early votes already represent nearly 30% of the total turnout from the 2022 midterms. Florida sits at 18%, with similarly high paces recorded in North Carolina, Michigan and Ohio.

Mail-in voting remains resilient: Over 56m mail-in ballots have been requested or sent nationally – greater than the 52m requested in 2022, according to NBC News (using data from TargetSmart). Despite executive orders from the Trump administration attempting to restrict mail-in voting, which the supreme court struck down in September, voters continue to utilize the process at pre-pandemic rates.

Meta ordered to pay $25bn penalty over deceiving users about data privacy

A judge in New Mexico ordered Meta to pay $25bn in penalties, on after a jury found the company had misled consumers in a case arising from allegations that Cambridge Analytica – a political consulting firm involved in Donald Trump’s 2016 presidential campaign – obtained data from millions of Facebook users without their consent.

The state’s attorney general had sued Meta in 2021 following revelations that Cambridge Analytica, a British political consulting firm, had harvested personal data from as many as 87 million Facebook users through a third-party app.

“Facebook’s wrongdoing – over the course of years and systematically broadcast to the public – goes to the heart of Facebook’s core business practices,” Judge Francis Mathew wrote in his order following the verdict.

Mathew found that a penalty of more than $219bn was warranted, based on state law, but he reduced the figure to $25bn.
