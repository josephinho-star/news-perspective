Democrats have 11-point lead among likely voters as Trump’s approval rating craters – live

2026-10-21 18:49

New poll finds likely voters prefer Democratic candidates over Republicans running for office by 11 percentage points; Trump campaigns in South Carolina

New polling shows likely voters prefer Democratic candidates over Republicans, fewer than two weeks until midterm elections

New polling from the New York Times and Siena University shows that likely voters prefer Democratic candidates over Republicans running for office by 11 percentage points.

Meanwhile, Donald Trump’s approval rating dropped to a new low for this particular survey – 36%.

Notably, according to the Times’ polling, the perception of either party has shifted over the last two years. The number of people who consider Democrats as the party of the working class has grown by five points – from 48% to 53%, while it’s dropped by five points for Republicans – from 45% to 40%.

With 13 days until Election Day, early voting data is rolling in nationwide. Democrats need to net four Senate seats and hold 218 House seats to reclaim a congressional majority. Here is where the numbers stand so far:

Turnout is surging in key battlegrounds: Over 16 million Americans have already voted in person or by mail. According to the University of Florida’s Election Lab, battleground states -including Michigan, Ohio, Georgia, Texas, Florida, and North Carolina – have each passed 700,000 ballots cast as of 21 October. Turnout is being driven in part by widespread voter frustration, reflected in President Trump’s record-low approval ratings.

Democrats lead in early returns: Registered Democrats account for 47% of early ballots cast, compared to 29% from Republicans (tracking returned ballots, not candidate selection). The party gap is most pronounced in North Carolina, Kansas, and Iowa. While Republicans traditionally favor election day voting, strategists worry this widened margin signals a “red undertow” – GOP voters staying home due to frustration over high gas prices, the conflict in Iran, and sweeping tariffs.

Early votes are pacing well ahead of 2022: In Georgia, early votes already represent nearly 30% of the total turnout from the 2022 midterms. Florida sits at 18%, with similarly high paces recorded in North Carolina, Michigan, and Ohio.

Mail-in voting remains resilient: Over 56 million mail-in ballots have been requested or sent nationally – greater than the 52 million requested in 2022, according to NBC News (using data from TargetSmart). Despite executive orders from the Trump administration attempting to restrict mail-in voting, which the supreme court struck down in September, voters continue to utilize the process at pre-pandemic rates.

Meta ordered to pay $25bn penalty over deceiving users about data privacy

A judge in New Mexico ordered Meta to pay $25bn in penalties, on after a jury found the company had misled consumers in a case arising from allegations that Cambridge Analytica – a political consulting firm involved in Donald Trump’s 2016 presidential campaign – obtained data from millions of Facebook users without their consent.

The state’s attorney general had sued Meta in 2021 following revelations that Cambridge Analytica, a British political consulting firm, had harvested personal data from as many as 87 million Facebook users through a third-party app.

“Facebook’s wrongdoing – over the course of years and systematically broadcast to the public – goes to the heart of Facebook’s core business practices,” Judge Francis Mathew wrote in his order following the verdict.

Mathew found that a penalty of more than $219bn was warranted, based on state law, but he reduced the figure to $25bn.

Elena Kagan criticized rulings made by the US supreme court’s conservative majority this week, arguing that its recent decisions on redistricting and campaign finance had undermined America’s democracy.

“The court has kind of blinded itself to the very deleterious effects of their rulings on how the political system works,” said Kagan, one of the court’s three liberal justices, during a discussion at Catholic University’s law school in Washington DC yesterday, according to multiple reports.

She emphasized that the court, which has a 6-3 conservative majority, “has created a democracy that does not work as well as it should”.

Kagan’s remarks come just two weeks before the midterm elections and amid battles over congressional redistricting. Earlier this year, the supreme court struck down Louisiana’s congressional map, effectively gutting a major section of the Voting Rights Act. In June, the court also struck down campaign finance restrictions limiting how much political parties may spend on candidates. Both decisions were split 6-3 along ideological lines.

As a result, she said, “both political parties have been engaged in a race to the bottom in terms of doing these partisan gerrymanders, effectively depriving masses of citizens of their rights to voting equality”.

Kagan said that decision was pushing political candidates “away from the center, toward the wings, toward the extremes, because the only thing anybody has to worry about any more is being primaried”.

“It is making what I think is unfortunately a too polarized country even more so,” she continued. “In this election, and the next election, we are seeing large states with substantial numbers of people who vote for Republicans and who vote for Democrats, and the states are going to be tried to turn all red or all blue in a way that I think is very destructive to our democracy.”

Kagan also criticized the court’s decisions on campaign finance rules, and the increasing amounts of money in politics.

The people of America look around this political system and think that all this money chasing all these political candidates is a nightmare. And they’re right.

Supreme court rebuffs Trump restrictions on medical care for trans inmates for now

The supreme court has refused to allow the Trump administration to immediately deny gender-affirming care for transgender people in federal prisons.

The decision leaves in place a lower-court block on the policy while a legal challenge plays out. It was adopted after an executive order from Donald Trump, who’s taken several steps to roll back transgender rights during his second term.

Two of the supreme court’s six conservative justices, Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas, dissented, meaning they would have allowed the policy to be enforced.

Trump’s January 2025 executive order called on the Bureau of Prisons to revise its medical care policies so federal funds aren’t spent “for the purpose of conforming an inmate’s appearance to that of the opposite sex”.

The new policy would halt surgical and hormonal treatments, and end “social accommodations” like makeup and wigs. Prisons would continue to offer mental health services.

Trans prisoners sued the administration over the policy and, so far, two lower courts have blocked officials from implementing the order as the larger lawsuit over the executive order continues.

Witkoff travelled to Russia from Oslo, where he had met Zelenskyy and European leaders a day earlier in an attempt to inject momentum into stalled negotiations, with Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine now in its fifth year.

Witkoff told Putin during a televised portion of their meeting:

We’ve brought some papers. It’s really some ideas, some sort of peace deal between Russia and Ukraine, and you’ll read that at your leisure.

The visit was Witkoff’s ninth to Russia since Trump tasked him with brokering an end to the war.

Trump praises UK approval of controversial Jackdaw gasfield in North Sea

Donald Trump has welcomed the news that UK government has approved the controversial Jackdaw gasfield in the North Sea.

“Great news for the wealth and prosperity of the United Kingdom, and the wonderful region of Aberdeen. The UK has started drilling in the North Sea, one of the greatest and most prosperous areas of OIL in the World,” the US president wrote on Truth Social.

He added that the UK should go “FULL BLAST”, and the move to resume offshore extraction would “make the UK very wealthy again”.

As my colleagues reported earlier today, Andy Burnham, the UK prime minister, said the Jackdaw project will “protect hundreds of jobs and help keep millions of homes warm this winter”. This comes after fierce opposition from several Labour MPs and environmental groups throughout the country.

Trump has long pushed for Britain to expand oil and gas drilling in the North Sea, instead of relying on wind turbines. He frequently clashed with Burnham’s predecessor, Keir Starmer, who argued that energy sustainability relied on renewable sources and moving away from fossil fuel dependence.
