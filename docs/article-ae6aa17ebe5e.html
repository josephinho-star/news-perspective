The FBI has arrested a California real estate agent at Los Angeles International Airport, accusing her of surveiling the president of Taiwan’s family on behalf of the Chinese government.

Federal prosecutors allege that Wanying Zhang, a 34-year-old real estate agent from Irvine, surveiled the house of Taiwan’s president’s son, Ting-Yu Lai. Zhang allegedly sent photos, videos and license plate information to a Chinese government official. She has been charged with working for China as an unregistered agent.

The news comes amid ongoing probes into foreign influence in the United States. A California court will hold a sentencing hearing tomorrow for a California mayor who has pleaded guilty to working as an agent for China.

Earlier today, the justice department announced that it has filed denaturalization actions against 40 individuals accused of offenses ranging from acting as an agent for a foreign government to committing voluntary manslaughter.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul has declared a disaster emergency over an ongoing measles outbreak.

“No one should get seriously ill or die from a vaccine-preventable disease,” Hochul said. “That is why I have taken steps to increase the number of health professionals who can vaccinate and test. These emergency measures will protect New Yorkers and support our community partners as they work tirelessly to address the spread of this disease.”

According to the state’s measles dashboard, there have been 108 cases in the state this year, with 101 of those reported outside New York City.

Neighboring Pennsylvania is reporting the largest measles outbreak in the nation, with more than 1,000 cases this year.

Speaking to reporters ahead of leaving for his rally in Nebraska, Donald Trumpslammed the recent news that election officials in Maricopa county – Arizona’s largest – accidentally sent out more than 3,000 ballots five days ahead of the 7 October mailing date.

“Maricopa is a very, very dishonest county,” Trump said, despite the fact that the area’s recorder – Justin Heap – is a former Republican lawmaker who has repeatedly undermined the integrity of the voting system in Arizona. He defeated Stephen Richer, the GOP incumbent in 2024, who defended his county’s process and routinely pushed back on claims of rampant voter fraud.

In response to the latest news, Heap said that the ballots that were issued early remain valid and do not need to be voided or reissued as the error affected only the timing of the mailing. “The correct ballots were mailed to the correct voters,” Heap said in a statement.

Trump, who has routinely and baselessly undermined mail-in voting – and even signed an executive order to try to curb it last year – still defended his decision to tell Americans to cast their ballot via mail. A reminder that the president has voted by mail on several occasions.

“I’d rather have a vote in person, but they can vote by mail if they want, because we seem to have no choice,” he said, before claiming – again without evidence – that there is “a lot of cheating in vote by mail”.

Earlier today, the supreme court heard the first arguments of its 2026-27 term, in a case that focuses on whether states can sue oil companies for the impact of climate change on their communities. The lawsuit was filed by local officials in Boulder, Colorado, against Suncor Energy and ExxonMobil. The energy companies argue that only federal law can be used to address this issue. Boulder is one of dozens of local governments that have filed lawsuits against energy companies that demands compensation for past and future harms to the environment. Notably, only eight of the court’s nine justices will hear today’s arguments, after Samuel Alito recused himself from the case after facing calls to step aside due to stock holdings in oil companies.

Trump attacks media and defends crowd size at his events ahead of Nebraska rally

Ahead of traveling to Nebraska to rally today, Donald Trump took aim at the New York Times’ reporting which highlighted that the president’s recent rally on Saturday was not full, and certain sections of Butler high school gymnasium in Vandalia, Ohio, were “completely empty”.

Trump – who has routinely blasted the press for commenting on the size of crowds at his events – claimed that in Ohio “thousands of people were unable to get in”.

“I never have empty seats – Never have, and never will! Because of their dishonesty, I have cinematographers shooting the various Arenas where I speak,” he wrote on Truth Social. “The Rallies are “HOTTER” than ever. They are all “happenings,” and they never happened before, and will never be duplicated again. The Networks refuse, however, under any circumstances, to show the Crowds, but you can hear the thunderous applause, and know that it represents thousands and thousands of people.”

Shifting back to the supreme court, and the case before the eight justices (after Samuel Alito’s recusal) which focuses on whether states can hold energy companies liable for their role in climate change.

The justices focused their questions today primarily on the merits of the case rather than concentrating on whether the court had jurisdiction to hear it, suggesting they will make a decision on the question of whether federal law pre-empts Boulder’s state climate case.

Dennis Fan, the founder and director of the Appellate Litigation Clinic at Columbia Law School who wrote an amicus brief in the case, said it was too close to call, but he was concerned for the future of the climate cases. “They focused more on the merits,” he said. “That had me much more worried for Boulder.”

Kannon Shanmugam, a lawyer for the fossil fuel companies, told the justices that climate change was a pressing issue, but state cases – such as the one filed by Boulder – were the wrong way to resolve it, and the issue should be left to lawmakers. He argued that if the cases were heard in state courts, it would allow a high number of lawsuits to be filed. “The breadth of Boulder’s argument is astonishing,” he said.

“The hope of the industry side is that this is going to be the death blow,” said Fan. “The industry is going for the home run, they’re going for the grand slam. They’re thinking, ‘If we win this argument, there will never, ever be climate change litigation again.’”

Politico barred from traveling on Air Force One for Trump Nebraska trip – report

Politico has been barred from traveling on Air Force One for Donald Trump’s trip to Nebraska, according to a report by CNN.

This comes after the administration previously stopped CNN from similarly traveling with the president, despite a federal judge putting a temporary block on Trump’s overall ban on Politico, CNN and MS Now from the White House. That injunction is in effect until 8 October, but the newsrooms have sought for it to be extended until the matter is resolved in court. A hearing has been set for Thursday in the lawsuit filed by the three newsrooms against the administration’s ban.

The White House has previously argued that the judge’s ruling does not apply to Air Force One, the jet used to transport the president, cabinet members and the media to official events.

According to CNN, Politico was set to serve as the out-of-town print pool today – the newsroom tasked with covering the president’s movements that feeds to the wider US/global press. However, a Politico spokesperson confirmed to CNN that the outlet had been excluded from traveling with Trump.

Marco Rubio responds to removal of US bombers from UK base, says no direct ties to suspected plot

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said there was no direct link between the sudden removal of US bombers from a UK airbase and a suspected Iranian plot at the site last week.

“I wouldn’t just directly tie that to the move that was made,” Rubio told reporters, when asked about the B1 bombers.

“The Department of War is constantly reallocating resources where they need to be,“ Rubio added.

Rubio said on Monday that the US has confidence in the security of all its bases.

“It’s not unusual to see rotations happen and moving into the different parts of the world based on exercises, redeployment, changing out and new people coming in, in planes coming in, old planes going out,” Rubio said. “So, rules are changing all the time.”

Ahead of taking off for his trip to Alaska today, vice-president JD Vance spoke briefly to supporters where he stressed how competitive the state was ahead of the midterm elections.

He urged voters in the Last Frontier to not judge Mary Peltola, the moderate Democrat running to unseat the Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan – by “what she’s saying in the final six weeks of the Senate campaign”.

“You’ve got to judge her by what she actually did. And the reality is that she did support a lot of radical Democrat policies,” Vance said, referring to Peltola’s time as the state’s sole congresswoman.

When pressed by a reporter about whether the Trump administration’s so-called “public service announcements” – that have aired in recent weeks – are a good use of taxpayer dollars, Vance defended the advertising spots.

“If you look historically, you go back to the Obama administration with the Affordable Care Act. New polling shows Democrats leading governors races in red states

New polling from the New York Times/Siena college shows four key Democratic gubernatorial candidates leading in their respective races against Republicans in key red states that have become more competitive in recent weeks.

While we’ve been covering dozens of races that will shape the make-up of Congress, there are a number of races for governor across the country that will be vital – particularly when it comes to the ensuing redistricting battles we expect in the lead up to 2028.

According to the Times/Siena survey, Gina Hinojosa – the Democratic state lawmaker challenging sitting governor Greg Abbott – is leading the Republican incumbent by three points. Meanwhile in Iowa, which has become a source of hope for national Democrats, sees Rob Sand – a moderate who serves as the state auditor – ahead of GOP nominee Zach Lahn by 11 points. Staying in the midwest, in Ohio the closely watched competition between Republican Vivek Ramaswamy and Democrat Amy Acton sees the latter with an edge over the billionaire and former presidential candidate. In Alaska, where a Democrat hasn’t won the governor’s mansion in almost 30 years, Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins is up by four points over Bernadette Wilson, the Republican nominee.

Ken Paxton, the Texas attorney general chosen by Donald Trump to run as the Republican candidate for a Senate seat, admitted in private that his campaign was being controlled by outside forces in DC.

“I’ve never been in a race where I felt less control over the campaign and, like, what issues are going to matter because it’s all coming – you know – from DC,” Paxton told people at a fundraising event in September, according to an audio recording obtained by Politico.

“I don’t control message or what we’re doing,” Paxton added.

An unnamed attender told Politico that Paxton never explicitly said he was referring to Trump’s team controlling his campaign messaging, but that Paxton’s comments arose during a conversation about the national political environment.

The Guardian has asked Paxton’s campaign for comment.

Paxton’s campaign adviser Nick Maddux told Politico that, “Campaigns don’t coordinate with Super Pacs, and that’s obviously what AG Paxton was referring to.”

“AG Paxton is thankful for the support and knows how important Texas is to keeping the majority which is why he’s on the ground every day talking to Texas, raising money, and taking his message directly to the people including with his statewide television buy that started over this past weekend,” Maddux said.
