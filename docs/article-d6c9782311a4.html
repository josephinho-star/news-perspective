Too old for soft toys? Tell that to kidults booking up Harrods Jellycat cafe

2026-11-27 15:12

They’re cute, cuddly and taking over the homes of young adults.

Jellycat, a British soft toy brand founded in 1999, is experiencing a rise in interest from adult buyers as they snap up signature smiley designs in the shape of everything from croissants and disco balls to turtles and teabags.

The trend is part of a wider “kidult” movement with grownups buying items traditionally geared towards children, for example, Lego sets and Pokémon cards. On social media, videos of users talking through their collections of soft toys known as plushies can rack up millions of views.

On Monday, Harrods in London is opening “a first-of-its-kind dining destination” – a Jellycat cafe serving brunch in edible and soft toy form. Diners will be able to buy a plushie-shaped peach granola bowl, and eat it too.

According to research by Circana, “kidult” toy sales in the UK grew 22% in the first half of 2026. One in five toys and games are now bought by over-18s for themselves. At the US department chain Nordstrom, the average age of a Jellycat buyer is 27 years old. Everything from nostalgia to a rise in anxiety disorders are being credited as contributing factors to the trend.

Audrey Tang, a chartered member of the British Psychological Society, describes Jellycats as “tactile, comforting, calming and very cutesy”. She isn’t surprised to see increased demand from adults for plushies. “This year has been tough on a lot of people so if there is anything that makes us smile or makes us feel nice, we’re probably going to choose that,” she says.

Tang dismisses age-regression concerns, saying it is a type of judgment. “Try thinking about where that voice about rules is coming from,” she says. “Often those types of judgments come from parents or caregivers who have previously told us: ‘You’re too old for this or you should grow out of that.’”

Each Jellycat “character” is given a name by the brand and a brief bio. The toys hover around the £20 to £40 mark but discontinued or rare versions can fetch upwards of £500 on secondhand sites. EBay reports that Jellycat ranked fourth among the UK’s most-searched terms between January and September 2026. The Jellycat boom has also led to a rise in reports of “stealing to sell” shoplifting.

Bill Nighy has described himself as a “big, big Jellycat customer”. Sarah Jessica Parker has posted a photo of her own “Petal” bunny, while Kylie Jenner’s Jellycat of choice is a trio of green peas in a pod.

Mini bag charms, including 6cm-high pickles and marshmallows, are increasingly being spotted swinging from designer handbags.

Booking for the Harrods Jellycat brunch, which takes over a space previously occupied by the Prada cafe, opened in October and slots are sold out until February.

Rachel Dyer, 43, a business support officer, has booked a table to celebrate her 10th wedding anniversary. Dyer, who started collecting Jellycats in 2025, now has 95 plushies including a pine-cone tree, a fox, and a cat wearing a parka jacket. She describes her husband as being “bemused by the whole thing”.

Dyer says she likes how Jellycat allows her to embrace her inner child. “They bring me a little bit of comfort, joy and escapism, and I don’t think there’s anything wrong with that, whatever your age.”

The Harrods brunch follows other Jellycat in-person experiences, including a patisserie in Galeries Lafayette in Paris that sells small plush chouquettes (small choux buns) and a ranch in Texas that features a bald-eagle soft toy called Algernon. When Selfridges first launched its Fish and Chips Jellycat van last July, people queued for up to three hours to buy characters including a plush sausage and mushy peas wrapped in a bespoke newspaper.

Jellycat’s revenue jumped by 43% on the previous year to £450m, October figures show.

Tang says in-person events where people can meet fellow collectors help form wider connections. “Shared interests give people an instant talking point,” she says. “That can be a huge anxiety soother for someone who may be lonely or shy to talk to people. Why should we question anything that builds community in these divided times?”
