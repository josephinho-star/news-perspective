Burnham says he would put PR in Labour manifesto and scrap triple lock to fund social care – UK politics live

2026-09-29 17:09

Party conference speeches by a leader are rather weird and antiquated constructions. There is almost no other time in the year when a PM, or an opposition leader, gives a speech uninterrupted for an hour or so. Some are great, some are mediocre, but normally even the better speakers tend to lose the audience halfway through as they rattle through second-order issues included to keep an interest group happy.

Andy Burnham’s speech today was a rare exception. Because it was emotionally engaging, because it was laced with anger and it felt like a speech 15 years in the making, because it had a coherent argument, because it was quite colloquial, and perhaps because Burnham was not standing at a lectern, this was much more interesting and watchable than these speeches usually are. Normally, 40 minutes in, you are desperate for it to end. I would have happily listened to Burnham for another half an hour.

On substance, it was strong – but perhaps not quite as bold as Burnham maintains. His ambitions on social care are in the best traditions of “Go Big” (see 8.40am) and Burnham has shown himself to be a politician confident of his ability to turn public opinion on a big, difficult issue (getting rid of the triple lock). It was bold and inspiring. But whether it turns out to be effective remains to be seen, and if Burnham fails to win this argument, he has got time to trim, or back down.

On PR and Europe, he also staked out brave positions that will attract considerable opposition – not just out in the country, but from some in Labour too. But here he was a bit less specific than on social care and pensions. He implied PR would be in the Labour manifesto, but he just said he would fight the election on a proposal “to change the electoral system so that everyone’s vote counts”, which implied something less ambitious than PR – the alternative vote, perhaps, or PR for an elected Lords? – might end up to be the only option he thinks he can sell to the PLP.

And, on Europe, Burnham implied that he will open a conversation on rejoining. But he was not explicit about that, and then he started talking about “consensus” – which sounded naive because there no subject in British life on which consensus seems less possible than Brexit. One colleague has a theory that Burnham wrote the speech planning to end with a flourish saying rejoining would be an option (like Keir Starmer saying “nobody is ruling out remain” in his 2018 speech) – only to drop the wording at the last minute. There was definitely a bit of a hiatus at the end where the speech had been building up to a big reveal. (See 3.10pm.)

Still, this speech was daring, moving and – potentially – transformational. Burnham has had a good week, and today it got better.

Andy Burnham has promised a new “long-term” relationship with the EU in an emotional Labour conference speech in which he also confirmed the end of the pensions triple lock. As Pippa Crerar reports, in a deeply personal address, Burnham spoke through tears as he reflected on the death of his father just weeks ago, and used the experiences of his grandparents buying their own home, the cruelty of the care system and the pride of his family to set out how he wanted to take political risks to make fundamental change. In his first conference speech since becoming prime minister, Burnham promised to be unflinchingly honest, saying it was time to admit “Brexit hasn’t given us control” and that it was a handbrake on growth. In his strongest hint yet that he will look again at Britain’s membership of the bloc, he said he would use the EU summit to set out a path to a far more long-term relationship, rather than new sectoral deals.

What electoral system is Burnham going to push for?

In our briefing just now, his spokesman was keen to emphasise that the PM backs the current constituency link for MPs

So that rules out a completely PR list-style system, such as we used for the EU.

There was a 1998 commission into electoral reform, chaired by Roy Jenkins, which took into account four requirements:

2. Need for stable government

3. Extension of voter choice

4. Maintaining constituency links

It recommended a system called AV+. Basically a mix of the AV preferential system (rejected in the 2011 referendum), and the AMS/STV systems used in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Around 80% of the Commons would be filled from the AV constituency MPs, 20% from the proportional system.

I wouldn’t be remotely surprised if Burnham’s new commission ends up repeating this same finding, unless he inexplicably wants Labour to have zero chance of ever having a majority government again.

IFS explains how new version of triple lock will work, saying it's 'substantial improvement'

The Institute for Fiscal Studies has welcomed Andy Burnham’s announcement about the triple lock. (See 2.48pm) Although it sounds like he is replacing it with a double lock (pensions rising line with prices or by 2.5% every year, whichever is higher), in fact it is still a triple lock. That’s because, if the amount needed to ensure pensions have still risen in line with average earnings growth since the introduction of the new policy is higher than prices or 2.5%, they will go up by that amount.

The IFS proposed this change to the system. It explains it like this.

The key change is that the new triple lock removes the most expensive and unjustified part of the triple lock: the fact that it permanently ratchets up state pension spending over time. Under the reformed system, pensions will still rise every year and in line with average earnings in the long run, but less quickly than they otherwise would have, and in a less unpredictable way.

The reform will likely generate small savings to the Exchequer in the next parliament, but they will not be enough to fund universal social care. Over time the new triple lock will prevent the state pension being locked into an ever-increasing level of generosity compared to workers’ earnings, thereby generating significant savings in the long run.

In a briefing, the IFS has illustrated this with a rather incomprehensible chart.

Jonathan Cribb, deputy director of the IFS, said:

For years, we at the IFS have been calling for the government to find a better way to increase the state pension than the triple lock. The old triple lock is not a good way to achieve any policy goal. Most importantly, it is both very expensive and very unpredictable. The new, reformed triple lock from 2030–31 is not perfect, but it is a substantial improvement on the status quo. The prime minister should be commended for grasping the nettle and helping to put the state pension system on a more secure and sustainable footing.

Labour pro-Europeans welcome PM's commitment to new stance on EU, saying 'everything' should be on table

Lisa O’Carroll is a senior Guardian correspondent covering post-Brexit issues.

Andy Burnham’s promise to set out a new vision for the UK’s post-Brexit relationship with the EU later this year unleashes the prospects of a dramatic move away from Keir Starmer’s red lines of not rejoining the single market or customs union.

All day yesterday in Liverpool, rumours were swirling that Burnham had some big reveal on the EU in his speech.

Although he didn’t specify what the new relationship would look like, announcing he wants a consultation on options the Labour party can embrace, will be music to the ears of people like Stella Creasy, the head of the Labour Movement for Europe group. After the speech she posted this on social media.

Andy Burnham has spoken the truth that the British people are living every day: Brexit has done more harm than good.

We in @labour4europe warmly welcome the news that the Prime Minister will present to the country the full options for this country’s relationship with the EU.

Everything must be on the table. Removing the red lines on Europe is the essential first step to restoring this country’s place on the world stage.

We stand with this Prime Minister in making the changes that truly live up to the challenges of 2026, not 2016.

Burnham announced he would be looking at all options for the UK’s post Brexit relationship around the next summit with the EU, due to be held at the end of November. (See 3.10pm.)

Although ministers over the weekend, including EU relations minister Hamish Falconer, had insisted Burnham was “ambitious” for his plans with the EU, there were no details of what that looked like. Until today.

Burnham has now raised the prospects that he could blur or even rub out Keir Starmer’s red lines, not joining the single market or customs union, or even go into the next election promising another referendum to rejoin the EU.

Only yesterday Lord Liddle, a Labour grandee and Tony Blair’s former Europe adviser, was telling a panel on the EU in Liverpool that the UK had “got rather stuck” in its negotiations with Brussels.

Keir Starmer, he said, could never really make up his mind about where he wanted the UK to be. He “blew hot and cold” and “the red lines were brought out too often”, Liddle said, referring to Starmer’s insistence the UK would not rejoin the single market or customs union.

If the UK is serious about growth, Liddle said, “the first thing” it has to look at is “the relationship with the EU”.
