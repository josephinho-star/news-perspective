New California law changes balconies forever, while saving you money — but HOAs are watching

2026-11-19 19:36

A new law allows Californians to mount solar panels on their balconies, but you should probably read the fine print before thinking about going solar.

Governor Newsom signed SB 868, also known as the “Plug and Play Solar Act” into law in September, allowing Californians to install plug-in solar power systems on balconies beginning in January.

SB 868 will exempt plug-in solar devices from the state’s interconnection requirements, with the goal of making utilities cheaper for renters and homeowners.

But the law comes with strict rules about what kind of systems can be used.

The plug-in system must consist of one or two panels and a microinverter, and be mounted on railing, a patio or a ground stand. Devices also cannot exceed 1,200 watts of AC output per dwelling.

Systems also require a certification from Underwriters Laboratories or an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratory and meet the standards of the most recent version of the National Electrical Code and the California Electrical Code.

Despite the litany of rules and regulations, some Californians are considering opting in.

“I’ve been looking into this. My biggest concern is the cost versus actual savings,” one Redditor wrote.

“A 1,200W system would realistically save around $350–$400/year at LADWP rates, assuming decent sun exposure and that you’re using most of the electricity generated.”

“I’m very interested, I have a south facing deck that I could easily mount onto,” a second commenter said.

“I’m definitely getting one but at peak and max performance, it will only save about $250 per year. rent is going up but not because of that,” a third wrote.

But finding an approved system could prove difficult. Only two certified devices are currently on the market, according to Will Prowse of DIY Solar Power, who posted an informational video to YouTube.

You’ll also need to check in with your landlord or HOA before hooking up your system, as the law doesn’t override the terms of lease agreements or rules for what you can put up on your balcony or around your home.

PV Magazine suggests getting written approval from HOAs or landlords before installing a system, and questions if the devices’ 1,200W limit is powerful enough.

“A 1,200-watt plug-in kit simply can’t cover an evening peak or keep your refrigerator running during a Public Safety Power Shutoff,” the publication wrote.

The law will only be active through January 1, 2030, when when the interconnection exemption expires.
