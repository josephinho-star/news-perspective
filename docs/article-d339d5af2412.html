California faces horrifying budget hole as Xavier Becerra gets grim warning: ‘Dangerous stage’

2026-11-19 01:33

California could face years of staggering deficits running into the tens of billions of dollars within five years if urgent budget repair is not carried out – and the AI boom goes bust.

While the outlook projects California will face a $2 billion shortfall for the upcoming fiscal year, it predicted the deficits are set to soar to at least $15 billion by 2028-29.

And in a worst-case scenario, the shortfall could be potentially as high as $30 billion by fiscal year 2030-31.

“The problem in the upcoming budget year is relatively small, but it grows to be fairly significant,” legislative analyst Gabe Petek said on Wednesday.

Analysts fear an AI bubble could burst the stock market, which is currently propping up the state’s finances with its capital gains tax revenue.

Analysts said lawmakers have to take that risk seriously.

“While the stock market’s recent run has been great for many investors and the state budget, it now appears to have advanced to a dangerous stage,” analysts wrote.

Petek added that the signs of a burst are there: “Every one of these indicators that we’re looking at is the highest they’ve ever been – except for at the peak of the dot-com bubble.”

Analysts suggested that the state make $6 billion in cuts or “budget solutions” next year to build up to a $4 billion cushion.

Democratic leaders in the state legislature quickly pledged on Wednesday to prepare for it.

“We will continue to strengthen our reserves and protect health care, food aid, emergency response and our schools,” Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas said in a statement.

“That’s how we keep costs steady and make every dollar count. Whatever comes next, California will be ready.”

Democrats pointed to the recently voter-approved Proposition 2, which lawmakers proposed to double the cap of the state’s rainy day fund.

The top lawmakers said they will look at moving more of any excess revenues into those reserves to prep for any downturn.

“Otherwise, when the market eventually corrects, the consequences of today’s budget problems will catch up with us.”

Becerra, as the next governor, will have to provide an initial spending plan by Jan. 10. His spokesperson said in a statement that he “is hard at work preparing his first budget proposal.”

“He remains committed to delivering a responsible, balanced budget that protects the services Californians depend on and focuses on bringing down costs,” the spokesperson said.

Becerra, before the election, had said “everything should be on the table” when asked whether he would support a mileage-based tax to maintain California’s roads, with Republicans claiming the Democrat was intending to introduce one.

“He said, ‘everything’s on the table,’ and then he started arguing in favor of it,” Assemblymember Carl DeMaio told The Post. “He literally was making the argument for the mileage tax.”

The Becerra campaign has stated that he has not proposed a mileage tax. The Post reached out to Becerra’s spokesperson on whether any new taxes may be sought after in response to the budget outlook.

In late October, Becerra himself was asked by a reporter on the campaign trail regarding the state budget relying too heavily on the AI stock boom — and if California may need to see service cuts or tax increases.

Becerra simply responded that the government has to be “steady” even if the stock market is not.

“We’re not going to overspend. We’re going to make sure we’re fiscally prudent,” Becerra said.
