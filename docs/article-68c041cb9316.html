BBC cash reserves hit lowest level in a decade after big single-year spend

2026-09-18 14:21

The BBC has spent hundreds of millions of pounds of its cash reserves in a single year, increasing concerns at the corporation over the perilous state of its finances.

Cash reserves at the BBC’s public service arm slumped by 66% in the last financial year, from £369m in 2025 to just £125m, according to analysis by the Guardian. This is the lowest figure in the last decade of accounts.

Matt Brittin, the BBC’s director general, used an internal call with staff last week to warn that slumping cash reserves and maxed-out dividends from the corporation’s commercial operation meant there was “not a lot of elbow room for anything”.

He made the call after staff questioned how the BBC’s finances had been allowed to deteriorate so badly. The broadcaster is currently engaging in huge cuts, including up to 2,000 jobs, designed to save £500m.

BBC insiders said cash reserves were being spent to ensure the BBC could keep investing in content and make the changes needed to adapt to digital audiences.

The BBC licence fee has lost about £1.3bn in value in real terms since 2017. This has been a result of fee freezes and a faster than predicted rise in the number of people choosing not to pay it.

The broadcaster is having to make painful changes to deal with the rising costs of TV production, growing competition from the likes of Netflix and YouTube, and rapidly changing patterns of how people consume media.

The financial decline has coincided with a huge amount of staff turnover at the top of the corporation. Its chief financial officer, chief technology officer and chief operating officer have all left in the last 18 months. They will soon be joined by the chief people officer.

Tim Davie, the BBC’s previous director general, resigned last year after criticism over a Panorama episode that spliced together parts of a Donald Trump speech. The US president has since taken legal action. The BBC has apologised for the cut, but maintained it did not defame him.

The cash crunch comes at a difficult time for Brittin, who took over from Davie in May. He wants to transform the BBC to cope with the demands of digital audiences, but major changes require upfront investment.

He is also engaged in delicate negotiations with the government over the BBC’s future funding model, under which those who watch streaming services could be asked to pay the BBC licence fee. Currently, only those who watch live TV and the BBC’s iPlayer have to pay the fee.

Abi Watson, at Enders Analysis, said the fall in reserves was part of an underlying depletion of the BBC’s financial cushion that had been “building for years”.

“The BBC hasn’t run out of money, but the fall in public-service cash leaves it with considerably less room to absorb further pressures or fund change,” she said. “And this isn’t a new problem.

“The latest savings requirement predated [Brittin’s] arrival. The BBC has already reduced headcount, rationalised its estate and changed its commissioning mix. Commissioned hours, excluding news and sport, are down 20% since 2016-17. Further savings increasingly mean decisions about output.”

Alex DeGroote, an independent media analyst, said the upcoming redundancies would add extra costs in the short term: “[It] will drain further the cash as staff are paid off upfront, before the benefit emerges from a lower cost base.”

He also said a bumper £197m in dividends the BBC received from its commercial arm in the last financial year could not be relied upon. “The internal dividend amount is well above trend and it’s hard to believe it’s sustainable at that level, particularly in this trading environment.”

The BBC’s remaining reserves would also be put at risk by a £125m pension payment, he said.

The BBC reported a £121m deficit for the last financial year, more than the £33m deficit it had predicted.

A BBC spokesperson said: “Like many in the media industry, the BBC is facing considerable financial challenges, which is why we’re making significant cost savings across the organisation.

“The drawdown of cash reserves has enabled us to manage pressures on licence fee income while continuing to prioritise spending for audiences and invest in our digital transformation.”
