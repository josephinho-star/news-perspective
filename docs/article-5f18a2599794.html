Australia news live: OpenAI warns Labor about global security threats; Albanese to meet Apple’s Tim Cook in US

2026-09-17 23:49

Australia needs to slow down its artificial intelligence use and create better security laws, the companies creating the technology have told the federal government.

In a submission to an ongoing inquiry, OpenAI said national leaders had a responsibility to work with other countries to stop global security threats:

Many of the most consequential risks will not stop at national borders, making international co-ordination increasingly important.

It said Australia was in a narrow “defender’s window” during which it could use the technology to find and fix cybersecurity threats, rather than create them.

Tech giant Atlassian, which runs AI system Rovo, used its submission to urge the government to bolster its laws. International standards and work already undertaken overseas should be used to protect Australian consumers, it said.

“We need to embrace governance and regulation as a force multiplier for trust,” Atlassian’s head of regulation, Anna Jaffe, wrote, arguing that tighter privacy crackdowns would also benefit the tech sector as clients would be more likely to engage with them.

The comments come after OpenAI’s chief executive, Sam Altman, and Elon Musk displayed rare unity in backing Anthropic’s head Dario Amodei, who called for a slowdown in “reckless” AI development through a three-part essay.

AI standards: big datacentres will have to bring renewables unless alternatives have ‘clear benefits’

The Albanese government has detailed the core parts of its AI standards as it consults on the new rules to be brought in early next year.

The Commonwealth standards will require large datacentres to offset their energy usage with new renewable projects, unless using state-owned infrastructure would have “clear benefit” keeping the grid more stable and prices lower. That is the compromise reached with the Northern Territory and Queensland governments, which run parts of their energy systems.

Projects will also be required to manage their energy use during peak periods and cover their own energy and water network costs so households don’t face higher prices.

Developers will be required to engage “meaningfully” with communities and First Nations peoples, draw on local suppliers “where possible,” and build away from “sensitive community areas,” such as schools and homes.

The projects will have to support training of construction and operational workers to ensure they don’t worsen Australia’s skills shortages. Efficiency standards for water and energy use are also under consideration.

The government is considering applying those to datacentres with 30-100MW capacity, excluding smaller builds. Datacentres with even higher usage could face “more substantial requirements”.

Australia’s spy chief has said Australia’s politicians, community leaders, and media have not done enough to keep Australia’s Jewish community safe from anti-semitism.

The director-general of the Australian Security Intelligence Organisation Mike Burgess spoke at length on a podcast hosted by broadcaster Neil Mitchell.

“We still have antisemitism as a problem and that does concern me,” he said:

It’s got to a pretty high, disturbing level.

It raised its ugly head after the 7th of October. It started with threatening, intimidating behaviour and then a year later, it transitioned to direct targeting of people, places of worship, and businesses. And then we saw the death of 15 people. I reckon that’s a bad place for us to be.

Burgess said more needed to be done to prevent conflation of the state of Israel with Jewish people in public and private rhetoric.

It’s okay to be angry at the state of Israel. But how you deal with that anger, it really does matter. I’d like to think there are people who are kind of passionate and they’ve got a right to have that strong view. Are they deliberately wanting to be antisemitic? No, I don’t believe they are, but their actions may well be.

Burgess said there was a widespread failure to counter growing antisemitism.

All of us should have stood up more. Maybe I could have shouted more. Political leaders could have said this is not right. I mean, a couple of them did do that, but not many of them did. The media should have probably watched how it was reporting.

By all means be critical of the state of Israel, but actually say, ‘but we’re not talking about Australians or people of Jewish faith’.

Michele Bullock, the Reserve Bank governor, says “upside risks to inflation appear to be materialising”, setting the central bank on course to deliver a fourth interest rate hike later this month.

Bullock told a parliamentary economics committee:

Inflation is too high. We are focused on getting it back down and making sure that it does not become embedded into price and wage-setting decisions.

The governor said at the RBA board’s last meeting they were worried that inflation would come in hotter than hoped, and it looked like that was happening, despite the slowing economy.

Global cost pressures have increased – the Middle East conflict, the AI boom and extreme weather events are contributing to upward pressure on a range of energy, agricultural and technology-related prices.

There is little sign of resolution of the Middle East conflict. Oil and related prices have increased sharply again and will add directly to inflation. Through our liaison program, we are hearing that many firms are passing on higher input costs.

The RBA board will meet on 28-29 September, where the “key question” will be whether the three rate hikes earlier this year will be enough to bring inflation back to the 2.5% target “in a reasonable time”. Bullock said:

I recognise that higher interest rates are difficult for Australians with mortgages who are also facing cost-of-living pressures. But reducing inflation is essential. High inflation hurts all Australians and is especially tough on people with lower incomes and those in vulnerable financial positions.

Labor already ‘removing people’ who overstay visas: Thistlethwaite

Matt Thistlethwaite says the Labor government has already been “removing people” who overstay their visas.

I know that because I sign off on the removals. We do remove people. But what we’re committing to here is additional resources. There’ll be an additional 100 officers that will work and there’ll be an additional 250 beds in detention centres for those people who overstayed.

NSW opposition claims new drug-driving laws will make roads less safe

The NSW opposition said the state Labor government’s new drug-driving laws would “[make] NSW roads less safe and [put] more lives at risk”, claiming the science on impairment from THC levels was not settled.

Mark Coure, the shadow minister for roads, said the government had “pushed ahead with the legislation despite the absence of a scientifically agreed test for cannabis impairment and has refused to release any evidence underpinning its approach”.

Drivers deserve laws that are fair, clear and based on science. Police deserve laws that are workable and properly drafted. Patients deserve clarity about how their prescribed medication may affect their legal position.

Drivers in NSW with a prescription for medicinal cannabis will be legally allowed drive with traces of the drug in their system after laws passed state parliament.

The legislation alters laws that saw drivers with traces of THC in their system immediately lose their licence for three months, instead allowing drivers who register under the new scheme to drive under strict conditions.

These including that THC in their blood is below a threshold of 50 ng/ml, they hold a valid prescription, and they undergo an online education program about cannabis and safe driving.

The government said roadside drug testing would continue and there would be no changes to the roadside drug-testing process carried out by police.

Registered patients who exceeded the THC threshold would be issued with warnings for the first two instances, while a third in two years would result in penalties, including a $722 fine and a minimum three-month licence suspension.

The roads and regional transport minister, Jenny Aitchison, said:

These reforms balance road safety with the need for a new system for patients who rely on this legally prescribed medication, without the fear of being treated like a criminal …

From now on, eligible medicinal cannabis patients, who are not impaired, will have a clearer system, more opportunities for education, and a chance to adjust their behaviour before facing penalties.

I’d encourage farmers to have a look at that [PALM] scheme, because there is opportunity. But more broadly, when it comes to migration, it is about taking a response that ensures that we have integrity in the system, that we have compliance in the system.

Asia Group chairman hopes for return of globally engaged America

Kurt Campbell, chairman of the Asia Group and formerly the national security coordinator for the Indo-Pacific in the Biden administration, told a US thinktank that US willingness to work with allies “occasionally is lost” but that he hoped for a return to a more globally engaged America.

The CSIS event marked five years since the announcement of the Aukus pact between the US, UK and Australia.

I believe that this enduring quality of American diplomacy will see a brighter day again.

I would simply say that the idea that we will enhance our security by withdrawing from allies and partners is deeply, profoundly misguided. And we have many historical examples of moments of American retrenchment. In almost every case that has led to degrees of global calamity. And so I’m all for opportunities to work constructively with allies and partners.

Campbell said while the US had partnerships with other nations that were strong and durable “they come, frankly, from a tradition of a degree of paternalism”.

On the part of the United States, we’ve got to clear out some of those hurdles that make cooperation with the United States that much more difficult. And it means approaching some of these partnerships with a much higher degree of humility.

Under Pillar One of the proposed Aukus agreement – a multi-decade deal slated to cost Australia $368bn to the mid-2050s – Australia will buy three “in-service” Virginia-class nuclear-powered, conventionally armed submarines from the US, beginning in 2032.

From the early 2040s, Australia expects to deploy a new, larger Aukus-class submarine. The new class will be designed in the UK, and the first boat built for the Royal Navy. Australia will then build Aukus submarines – except for their nuclear reactors – in South Australia.

But the proposed deal has faced consistent criticism over laggard shipbuilding rates in both the US and UK, and over Australia’s sovereign control of the boats: whether Australian submarines would be expected to join any potential US conflict in Australia’s region.

'Sovereignty, prosperity and peace are not a given': Penny Wong

AnthonyAlbanese will head to New York to lead Australia’s delegation at the United Nations General Assembly’s high-level week, to promote Australia’s commitment to multilateralism and meet international partners.

The prime minister will also deliver Australia’s National Statement, which will share Australia’s values and priorities – including digital safety, climate change, security and peace - to the largest annual gathering of world leaders.

In a statement, Albanese said:

Australia’s prosperity and security depend on a strong global system, where nations work together, agreed rules are respected, and co-operation delivers shared benefit

It’s expected several bilateral meetings with world leaders will also take place on the sidelines of the summit as Australia ramps up its bid for a seat on the UN’s Security Council.

Australia is bidding to become one of the 10 non-permanent members of the 15-seat council during 2029 and 2030. A vote for the spot isn’t due to take place until 2028, but lobbying efforts will intensify during the general assembly. Australia last held a seat on the Security Council in 2013 and 2014.

In a statement, the foreign affairs minister, PennyWong, said “sovereignty, prosperity and peace are not a gift or a given.”

From building coalitions for the protection of aid workers and the rules countries like Australia rely on for our security and prosperity, to advocating for a better UN system fit for these uncertain times.

Albanese to meet Apple boss after social media reform

Anthony Albanese will fly to the United States to visit the Apple headquarters in California just weeks after the government released sweeping reform designed to keep children safe online.

The prime minister will meet with TimCook, Apple’s executive chair of the board of directors, to discuss Apple’s new parental controls for children – which the government characterised as “in part inspired by Australia’s groundbreaking reform”.

The tech executive issued an open invitation to the prime minister to visit Apple’s California headquarters after a phone call between the pair in June.

It comes after Labor introduced its digital duty of care reform, including new laws that require platforms to protect children from online harms including content that glorifies eating disorders, misogyny, crime or stunts, and abuse or bullying. It’s also almost a year on from Australia’s world-first ban on under 16s from using social media.

In a statement, Albanese said he was “proud that Australia is setting the world standard for digital safety”.

Britain’s Aukus chief says adversaries’ hatred of deal shows its success

One of the markers of the success of the Aukus nuclear submarine deal is how much it is opposed by potential adversaries, Britain’s Aukus chief has told a Washington DC thinktank.

Sir Stephen Lovegrove, the UK prime minister’s special representative on Aukus, told an event at the Center for Strategic and International Studies: “our adversaries hate Aukus”.

They really hate it. And in a sense, the way in which you can judge whether or not it is doing the things that you want it to do is how much people who don’t necessarily share our value systems and our objectives are objecting to it.

Australia’s ambassador to the United States, Greg Moriarty – secretary of the defence department when the Aukus deal was struck in 2021 – concurred, saying China had “made clear it’s very unhappy with Aukus”.

It believes that – or it’s said as much, I’m not sure if it believes it – that it’s destabilising the region. I think when you look at the way in which China is putting additional nuclear-powered submarines, additional nuclear armed submarines, it’s building its submarine capability and its nuclear missile capability at such a pace, these Australian boats are not destabilising the region. We are seeking to balance in the region and … the growth of PLA nuclear powered submarine capabilities is very profound.

Labor 'worked through all the policy issues' on immigration, Burke says

The home affairs minister, Tony Burke, has defended the time Labor has taken to release its immigration plan and blamed the Coalition who “might have more of an appetite for legislation”.

Burke on ABC’s 7.30 was asked “what took you so long” – given he was meant to speak at the National Press Club six weeks ago.

Every part of the economy is affected by immigration these days for Australia, and so … dealing with the challenges we’ve got with housing supply, which is not immigration’s fault, but immigration needs to be part of the answer, to do that in a way that has the softest landing for the economy is something you’ve got to get right.

The opposition leader, Angus Taylor, told 7.30 that the Coalition had “laid out what we needed as preconditions in order to be able to work with the government and they couldn’t meet the preconditions”.

The numbers are too high and what we’ve seen from the government today is no cut to the numbers … We are a great immigrant nation. Make no mistake about it. But immigration needs to be capped based on the housing supply, the infrastructure, the services we have, and that has not been the case in recent years.
