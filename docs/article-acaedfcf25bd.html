‘Turncoat’: Liberal senator Leah Blyth announces defection to One Nation, blindsiding Coalition

2026-11-26 00:58

Leah Blyth, federal Liberal senator for South Australia, has defected to One Nation, blindsiding former Coalition colleagues who branded her a “turncoat” and raising further scrutiny of chaos inside the opposition.

Blyth, a shadow assistant minister on Angus Taylor’s frontbench, said in a statement announcing her move that she did not believe the Coalition was capable of opposing the Albanese government.

“I can no longer in good conscience tell Australians that the Liberal Party can save this country,” Blyth said in a statement.

“Australia is a country worth fighting for … I want our children to grow up proud of their country, with the freedom and opportunity to build a better life.

“One Nation has never shied away from confronting the uncomfortable truths facing our nation. They are the only party with the bold ideas needed to turn this country around.”

Despite praising Pauline Hanson’s party as a better option for the country, Blyth admitted she was “not completely across” One Nation’s policies.

She also cited the Liberals’ support of the controversial defence bases bill as a reason for her disappointment at her former colleagues – despite One Nation also backing that bill.

“I didn’t pay attention to who else was in the chamber supporting that bill,” Blyth told News 24.

Blyth was named by the Liberal Party to replace former Liberal cabinet minister Simon Birmingham in the Senate in 2025, and was not elected in her own right. She was also the immediate past state president of the Liberal party in South Australia, but was replaced by conservative powerbroker Alex Antic.

Guardian Australia understands the pair, who were previously allies in the conservative faction, had experienced a falling out. Blyth will face re-election at the next poll and was expected to lose her winnable spot on the Senate ticket.

Coalition MPs said they were blindsided by the news and that Blyth had not informed them ahead of time.

When asked at a press conference about why she’d waited until the last day of parliament to announce her move and kept the news from her colleagues, Blyth said: “I’m not thinking about the Liberal Party, that’s in the past.”

“I think the Liberals have left a lot of voters, including those in South Australia, and I would argue that perhaps those people that voted for a Liberal are now feeling as disaffected as I am,” she said.

Blyth said she didn’t believe the Liberals could bring about change, and criticised what she claimed were policy “compromises”.

Later asked for a compromise which the Coalition took “that you were not prepared to take,” Blyth raised the “accelerating capability and protecting bases” bill, which was rammed through parliament with little scrutiny by the Labor and Coalition parties.

“That was not up for debate, and I think that when we are handing government sweeping powers, there should be very robust debate on issues like that,” Blyth said.

One Nation also voted for that bill. When this was pointed out, Blyth quickly ended the press conference.

Hanson welcomed the South Australian senator and said she would “build on One Nation’s expanding support within the state”.

On Thursday morning, the Nationals leader, Matt Canavan, called Blyth’s defection “disappointing on a number of levels” and condemned the move as part of a broader pattern of behaviour from One Nation.

The deputy Liberal leader, Jane Hume, similarly described the move as a “disappointing decision” but said Blyth “wasn’t a big contributor to the party room”.

Speaking to Radio National, Canavan said the move took him by surprise.

“I try and be friends with everyone … and I thought I was friends with Leah, but there was no warning from her.”

Speaking to Blyth’s reasoning that her values aligned closer to One Nation, Cavanan said the move “smacks of self-interest to me”.

“Good luck to her. Leah went to the election last year supporting net zero. She didn’t oppose the hate speech laws earlier this year, like we did – the Nationals.

“To turn around now a few months later and say, ‘My principles and policies are more aligned with One Nation’ – I mean, give me a break.”
