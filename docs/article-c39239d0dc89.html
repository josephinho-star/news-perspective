EU Governments Sign Off on Previously-agreed Trade Deal With Indonesia

2026-11-19 23:21

European Union governments have authorized the signing of a previously agreed trade deal between the EU and Indonesia, which is intended to unlock unrealized trade potential and offset the impact of new U.S. tariffs.

Under the Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA), which was concluded in September 2025, the two sides have pledged to remove import duties on more than 95 percent of products, the European Council said in a statement on Wednesday. Around 80 percent of the tariff reductions will take place when the agreement goes into effect; the rest will be phased out over a five-year period, including Indonesia’s duty on cars manufactured within the EU.

The CEPA “will substantially improve market access between the EU and Indonesia by progressively removing tariffs on the vast majority of goods traded between the two sides,” the Council stated. It is also expected to generate annual savings of around €600 million ($658.3 million) in customs duties.

EU member states also approved an Investment Protection Agreement (IPA) between the EU and Indonesia that is intended to “encourage long-term investment and strengthen confidence among businesses on both sides.”

The IPA “establishes guarantees for investors and their investments, including protection against unlawful expropriation and guarantees of fair and equitable treatment, non-discrimination and the free transfer of investment-related payments,” the Council said.

The Council added that the two agreements were “part of the EU’s broader efforts to diversify its trade relations, expand its network of international partnerships and strengthen the resilience of its economy.”

The two agreements will now be signed by the two sides, after which they will be submitted to the European Parliament for another stage of approval. As the Council noted, Indonesia is set to graduate from the EU’s generalized scheme of preferences on January 1, 2027, after which its exports will no longer benefit from preferential tariffs. For this reason, “the EU and Indonesia are committed to bringing the agreements into application as swiftly as possible,” it said.

The CEPA marks an important milestone in the economic relationship between the EU and Southeast Asia’s most populous nation. Bilateral trade in goods and services between the EU and Indonesia amounted to €28.9 billion ($31.7 billion) in 2025, but growth has been modest over the past decade, and “trade between the two sides has remained below its potential,” as the Council noted. While the EU is Indonesia’s fourth-largest trading partner last year, Indonesia was just the EU’s 30th-largest and the fifth within ASEAN.

The trade pact, which covers 21 areas of cooperation, including trade in goods and services, customs procedures, digital trade, and sustainable growth, has been under negotiation since July 2016. However, the talks were slowed by a number of disagreements relating to Indonesian protectionism and the European bloc’s policies toward palm oil, a key Indonesian export, including a deforestation regulation adopted in 2023. There have also been disputes over Jakarta’s nickel downstreaming policy and the EU’s imposition of anti-dumping and countervailing duties on certain types of Indonesian stainless steel products.

As my colleague James Guild noted last year, the swift conclusion of the CEPA “cannot be divorced from the broader churn in the global economy,” in particular, the sweeping tariffs that the United States imposed on friends and adversaries alike in April of last year. Indonesia was hit with a 32 percent tariff during Trump’s initial “liberation day” tariff announcement, a figure that was lowered to 19 percent in a trade agreement signed in February of this year.

The ongoing volatility of U.S. trade policy has no doubt given both sides a strong incentive to set aside areas of disagreement and push the deal toward completion.

“At a time of economic uncertainty, this is a clear signal that the EU remains committed to open markets, sustainable growth and strong international partnerships that deliver real benefits for businesses, workers, and consumers,” Irish Minister for Foreign Affairs and Trade Helen McEntee said in Wednesday’s statement.
