Shabana Mahmood to launch fast-track training to improve police leadership

2026-11-18 00:01

Hundreds of people will be trained to boost “frankly inadequate” leadership of law enforcement, the home secretary is to say.

Shabana Mahmood will tell police chiefs and leaders at their central London annual conference on Wednesday that standards must improve as she unveils a scheme to train 200 people a year, after a report in June into police leadership found it was at times “staggeringly” poor.

“The conclusions of the Police Leadership Commission were candid and eye-opening. Police leadership is inconsistent and, at times, it is frankly inadequate,” Mahmood will say, according to extracts released by her aides.

“This is the consequence of an earlier failure to invest in the development of future leaders.

“This government will address it … through the launch of a new fast-stream programme which will place hundreds of talented recruits on a path towards superintendent.”

Between 2023 and 2025, the police inspectorate graded two forces as inadequate, 13 as requiring improving and no forces as outstanding for leadership.

Also moving the home secretary to act was that eight serving or former chief constables were under investigation for misconduct across the 43 forces in England and Wales.

Mahmood will also mention that Devon and Cornwall was at one stage paying for three chief constables, with two of them at one point suspended.

The new scheme to recruit future police leaders will be open to people from any job and includes those who are not working in law enforcement.

The aim is to put recruits on a five to 10-year path to the rank of superintendent and “build a deeper and broader pipeline” of future leaders, according to government sources.

New future leaders will not need a degree and Mahmood’s advisers believe the scheme brings a “new focus and scale” to reversing the lack of emerging high-calibre police leaders.

Nick Herbert, co-chair of the leadership commission and a former Tory policing minister, said: “The introduction of a leadership fast stream is a significant step towards putting those recommendations into practice, and it is hugely encouraging to see this progress.

Lord Herbert added: “Effective leadership is fundamental to improving police performance, cutting crime and keeping the public safe. The challenges facing policing demand ambitious reform, and we must build on this momentum to deliver lasting improvements to leadership at every level of the service.”

Former Labour home secretary David Blunkett, co-chair of the Police Leadership Commission, said more changes were needed: “It is important that the introduction of fast-stream progression is not presented in isolation from the commission’s wider recommendations for a comprehensive overhaul of leadership competencies and career progression.

“Fast-track must not be seen as an elite mechanism, but rather as an opportunity, much like in the civil service, for those with the ambition and ability to accelerate their progression through the leadership and management ranks.”

The Home Office said the 27 recommendations from the leadership commission are being considered. They include a new national academy of police leadership, and more money for leadership training, with a current spend of about £4m in a service costing £19bn a year.

The main question on chiefs’ minds is how many of the 43 police forces in England and Wales will be culled as part of the government’s planned radical changes.

Mahmood will not reveal how many big regional forces will be set up. The Guardian understands the still-unpublished review she ordered by Bernard Hogan-Howe recommended as few as nine.

But the home secretary’s speech will stress that local policing units will deal with everyday crimes if the changes go through, according to sources.

Police leaders have claimed a lack of money has put forces under strain.

Figures show that the early release of prisoners to ease overcrowding since March led to a 13% increase in arrests for breach of bail and an 8% increase in arrests so people can be returned to jail, compared with last year.

Police estimate this will cost forces £500m by March next year and fear the figure may rise.
