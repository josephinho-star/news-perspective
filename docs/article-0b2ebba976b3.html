Clarence Thomas calls foul on California ruling he says could turn citizens into government mouthpieces

2026-11-17 15:35

On Monday, the Supreme Court declined a petition to hear a case from a California physician who argued that the state government was forcing her to make statements she disagreed with while teaching a mandatory course for doctors. Thomas was joined by Justice Samuel Alito in dissenting from the decision, arguing that allowing the law to stand could have implications reaching far beyond the medical field.

The case stems from California’s requirement that all its licensed physicians complete 50 hours of state-approved continuing education courses every two years. California requires continuing medical education courses to include instruction on implicit bias, either through examples of how it affects physicians’ treatment decisions and health outcomes or strategies for addressing how those biases may contribute to health care disparities.

“When, as here, the government does not issue its own messages but instead requires private speakers to communicate the government’s viewpoint, the First Amendment applies,” Thomas wrote. “A State cannot regulate its way out of First Amendment scrutiny. We should not permit the government-speech doctrine to be wielded as ‘a cover for censorship’ or a cloak for compelled speech.”

Dr. Azadeh Khatibi, who teaches a continuing education course in California, sued the state alongside the right-of-center nonprofit Do No Harm, arguing, as Thomas did, that it compels private instructors to convey a government-favored message in violation of the First Amendment.

“She does not want to include discussion of implicit bias in her courses. It is not relevant to her topics, and it would eat away at the limited time she could use to discuss topics that are relevant,” her legal team wrote. “Dr. Khatibi also disagrees that implicit bias is the primary factor driving disparities in health care.”

California countered that while instructors must include discussion of implicit bias in their courses, they are not required to personally endorse the theory and may openly disagree with it.

“Under the second option, instructors who do not believe that implicit bias exists are free to say so,” California wrote in the brief defending its law. “Petitioners are also free to inform CME attendees that any discussion of implicit bias is included solely to comply with state requirements.”

California further argued that states must be given broad authority to determine what doctors must do to retain their licenses.

The Ninth Circuit ruled last year that continuing medical education courses constitute government speech because of California’s extensive regulation of the medical profession, meaning the state’s requirements are not subject to First Amendment free-speech scrutiny.

Thomas, however, disputed California’s characterization of whether it was compelling instructors to take certain positions.

“California’s implicit-bias-training requirement leaves petitioners no choice but to discuss the topic in a way that affirms the State’s view that implicit bias causes ‘disparities in health outcomes,'” he wrote.

The justice went on to argue that allowing California’s law to go unchallenged could have implications for Americans working in many fields beyond medicine.

“What of other professions whose members California licenses and regulates? At the next statewide legal conference at which lectures are offered for continuing legal education, could California require a keynote speaker to oppose racial preferences in law firm hiring?” Thomas wrote. “Applying the Ninth Circuit’s logic, enough government regulation of the field would convert the private speaker’s message in each of these hypotheticals into government speech unprotected by the First Amendment. That result does not follow from this Court’s precedents, and it certainly does not follow from the Constitution.”

Justice Neil Gorsuch also dissented from the court’s decision not to hear the case, though he did not sign on to Alito and Thomas’ argument. His rationale for wanting to grant certiorari is not publicly known.

The Supreme Court’s decision leaves the Ninth Circuit’s ruling intact but does not settle whether California’s requirement violates the First Amendment.

“Medical professionals should not be compelled by the government to participate in evidence-free implicit bias trainings. It is disappointing the Supreme Court declined to take up our case, but we remain encouraged that some Justices acknowledged the importance of the First Amendment issues at stake in our case, issues that will undoubtedly continue to surface in courts across the country. We will keep fighting for constitutionally-protected free speech to ensure that health care providers are never conscripted to deliver the government’s message,” a spokesperson for Do No Harm told Fox News Digital after publication.
