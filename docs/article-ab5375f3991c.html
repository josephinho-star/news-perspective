Scientists create terrifying ‘ARkStorm’ showing high-magnitude megastorm could destroy California

2026-11-17 23:50

California could be staring down a weather catastrophe of historic proportions, with scientists warning that a hypothetical megastorm could unleash biblical flooding, hurricane-force winds and an astonishing $725 billion in economic damage.

The nightmare scenario, known as an ARkStorm, envisions weeks of relentless atmospheric rivers battering the Golden State, overwhelming flood defenses, swallowing entire communities and potentially forcing millions of residents to evacuate.

The frightening scenario, presented by the US Geological Survey, comes as back-to-back atmospheric rivers pound the West Coast, with another powerful Pacific storm targeting California and threatening heavy rainfall and several feet of snow in the Sierra Nevada, according to Fox Weather.

Scientists warn that the kind of megastorm envisioned in the ARkStorm model could make even a severe winter storm look tame.

The hypothetical disaster is based on historical and geological evidence showing that California has experienced extraordinarily powerful megastorms in the past.

The name “ARk” stands for Atmospheric River 1,000, referring to a scenario with precipitation levels in some locations associated with events expected only once every 1,000 years.

Researchers modeled their projections using historical storms, including the Great Flood of 1861-62, which devastated California and left the Central Valley impassable, along with major storms in 1969 and 1986.

And the 1861-62 disaster wasn’t even the worst Mother Nature has delivered.

Geological records show six megastorms more severe than that historic flood struck the Golden State over the past 1,800 years.

The ARkStorm scenario imagines atmospheric rivers drawing enormous amounts of heat and moisture from the tropical Pacific before repeatedly slamming into the West Coast over several weeks.

The consequences could be devastating statewide.

Perhaps most concerning, California’s existing flood-protection infrastructure was not designed to withstand the extreme conditions envisioned by researchers.

Depending on the location, flood defenses are designed to protect against events occurring once every 75 to 200 years on average.

But the ARkStorm scenario involves 500-year streamflows across substantial portions of the state, potentially overwhelming levees and other protective systems.

The Central Valley could experience flooding across an area stretching 300 miles long and at least 20 miles wide, according to the model.

San Francisco Bay communities, Los Angeles, Orange County and San Diego could also face serious inundation, along with numerous coastal and river communities.

The storm could bring winds reaching 125 mph in some locations, while broader areas experience winds of up to 60 mph. Hundreds of landslides could damage homes, roads and highways.

Power, water and sewer systems could also suffer extensive damage, with repairs potentially taking weeks or months.

The financial fallout could reach approximately $725 billion, according to the USGS scenario.

Direct property losses alone could approach $400 billion, including damage to buildings, agriculture and critical infrastructure.

Business interruptions could add another $325 billion.

Homeowners could face particularly steep losses.

The USGS estimates that only about 12% of California property is insured, while approximately 25% of the state’s buildings could experience some degree of flooding during a single severe storm.

Of the nearly $400 billion in projected direct property damage, just $20 billion to $30 billion could be recovered through public and commercial insurance, according to the agency’s 2011 report.

The human toll could be equally staggering, with flooding potentially forcing millions of residents in inland and Delta counties to flee their homes.

The ARkStorm project was developed by 117 scientists, engineers, public-policy experts, insurance specialists and critical infrastructure employees to help emergency officials and government agencies prepare for a disaster that could affect every California county and economic sector.
