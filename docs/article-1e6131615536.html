Fed’s preferred inflation gauge jumps to 3.8% as Wall Street braces for rate hike

2026-11-25 16:34

More troubling for Fed policymakers, prices excluding volatile food and energy costs climbed 3.3% — up from 3.1% the previous month and above economists’ expectations.

Stocks plunged Wednesday morning as an Nvidia-led sell-off in artificial intelligence shares intensified.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average shed 872 points, or 1.7%, while the S&P 500 sank 2.8% and the tech-heavy Nasdaq tumbled 4% as of 10:24 a.m. ET.

“Tech was already under pressure before the inflation report,” Scott D. Martin, chief investment officer at Kingsview Wealth Management, told The Post.

“The rate outlook matters, but investors are also asking how much profit all that AI spending will generate.”

Kingsview reported holding a position in Nvidia as of Sept. 30.

The inflation figures came as a separate government report showed the US economy expanded at a robust 4.3% annualized rate in the third quarter, raising concerns that borrowing costs aren’t high enough to cool demand.

“The economy is still growing strongly, but that’s becoming a double-edged sword,” John Poole of JPTD Partners told The Post.

“The numbers make a quarter-point hike more likely, but I think it would be a mistake,” Martin said.

The investment chief noted that the yield on the 10-year Treasury fell despite the hotter inflation reading.

“If hiring or spending weakens further, that gives the Fed a reason to pause,” Martin said. “A hike would put more pressure on households carrying variable-rate debt and businesses that borrow at floating rates.”

The renewed surge in consumer prices marks a setback in the central bank’s yearslong battle to tame inflation, which peaked at 7.2% in June 2022 before falling to 2.6% by the end of 2024.

By December 2025, that figure had crept back up to 2.8%.

Inflation has since been fueled by soaring energy costs following the US- and Israel-led attacks on Iran in late February, as well as tariffs and strong demand stemming from the AI investment boom.

Fed Governor Michelle Bowman earlier this month attributed roughly three-quarters of the overshoot in core inflation through September to those three factors.

“The biggest concern is that core inflation also rose to 3.3%, meaning the problem goes beyond gasoline and energy,” Poole said. “In other words, inflation is becoming more persistent.”

The Fed’s July monetary policy report showed that prices for goods excluding food and energy had climbed 2.4% in the 12 months through May — four times the pace of a year earlier.

Core services prices excluding housing rose 3.9% over the same stretch, with medical care, hotel stays and airfares among the contributors.

Americans’ personal income jumped 0.7% in October, well above the 0.4% economists had forecast, while consumer spending rose 0.3% — effectively flat after adjusting for inflation.

The personal saving rate climbed to 4.1%.

“This report alone can’t tell us how much of the inflation came from tariffs versus demand,” Martin noted.

There were also signs of continued strength in the labor market Wednesday, as the Labor Department reported that first-time applications for unemployment benefits fell by 13,000 to 189,000 last week — near the lowest levels since the late 1960s.

Nevertheless, traders dialed back their bets on how aggressively the Fed will raise rates next year.

While investors continued to price in two additional hikes in 2027, the odds of a third fell to roughly one in three from 50% on Tuesday, according to CME Group’s FedWatch.

“The Fed has less reason to lower rates, and the biggest risk for investors is that interest rates stay higher for longer just as expectations for AI earnings become harder to meet,” Poole said.

The next reading of the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge is due Dec. 23 — two weeks after policymakers wrap up their final meeting of the year.
