What Dark Spell Brought ‘Practical Magic’ Back to the Big Screen? And Should You See ‘Practical Magic 2’ Opening Weekend?

2026-09-11 12:00

Though most of its demonstrations of the dark arts involve actions about as exciting as literally turning on blenders, Practical Magic 2 has one undeniable bit of movie-world sorcery up its sleeve. In 2026, a movie starring Sandra Bullock and Nicole Kidman is creating a portal back to the days of genuine star power, when big-name performers could bring people into theaters by sheer charisma. There are still some actors with drawing power — has Zendaya ever starred in a genuine flop? — but the number that could be effectively teamed up to generate mass-audience excitement feels like it’s both diminished, with much of it effectively absorbed by the Dune movies. (Again, Zendaya: People seem understandably interested in which youngish actor will next receive her admonishing looks.) Bullock and Kidman, though, qualify together, sans sandworms — perhaps now more than ever. In fact, one big reason that Practical Magic 2 is poised to create an actual spooky-season box office event is that both Bullock and Kidman are arguably bigger stars than they were in 1998, when the original Practical Magic was not a wild box office success.

To be clear, Practical Magic did good-enough business back in October of ’98. It opened at number one (take that, Bride of Chucky!) and made around $47 million in North America. For the full year, it outgrossed the well-liked Pleasantville and The Faculty, and fell a little short of Halloween H20 or the Mighty Joe Young remake. It posted grosses similar to the similarly fantasy-tinged Brad Pitt starrer Meet Joe Black, though it wasn’t tagged as the same level of flop, because that movie’s exorbitant budget was even greater than Practical Magic (which was pretty high for 1998).

Something you may have noticed about the movies mentioned as attaining popularity levels similar to the original Practical Magic is that only the slasher sequels among them have spawned further installments. Though its story of a family of witches including sisters Sally (Bullock) and Gillian (Kidman), cursed such that any man who loves them will eventually die, is certainly horror-adjacent, Practical Magic is more of a cozy, folksy sister-bonding movie with a touch of romance than true seasonal horror. So why is this particular movie getting a decades-later sequel while other $47 million grossers with mixed reviews remain among the un-franchised?

That aforementioned coziness is one big reason. Practical Magic feels like a sleepover classic to a certain age range of millennial women who were tweens or tweens in the late ’90s. That group might have, before or after beholding the darker machinations of The Craft, discovered this purportedly more grown-up companion film which comes complete with a famous “midnight margarita” scene that seems custom-built for late-night giggle-fests, whether laughing with it, at it, or both.

Still, it’s impossible to separate the sequelizing of Practical Magic from the ongoing popularity of Bullock and Kidman. At the time of the first film, Kidman was certainly a figure of great attention; she was one of the most beautiful actresses on screen, she was married to Tom Cruise, and she had been in her share of hit movies, like Batman Forever and Malice, and impressive artistic swings, like To Die For. Her bigger breakthrough would come in the early 2000s, with the one-two punch of Moulin Rouge! and The Others, but Practical Magic was a mainstream movie where she wasn’t cast in a love-interest or femme-fatale role. (Ridiculously, it was one of her first, at least on that wide-release level.) Bullock, meanwhile, still had some genuine America’s Sweetheart status in the wake of Speed, While You Were Sleeping, and A Time to Kill. In 1998, putting the two together, above any male co-stars, vaguely resembled liberation. No wonder that it stuck out on video store shelves and on cable listings for some years afterward. Not every Bullock pairing from this era has aged as well. Consider that around the same time, she made a movie called Two If By Sea, co-starring Denis Leary.

Practical Magic 2 Review:

In 2026, Practical Magic 2 feels in particular like a movie built around Bullock’s star persona. Kidman has plenty of presence in the movie, too; at times, she feels like the only cast member who has correctly and knowingly assessed a proper tone for this material, and there are moments, however fleeting, where she injects a kind of screwball daffiness into her performance through an enthusiastically off-kilter line-reading. Kidman isn’t really known for comedy, but she has a certain ease with witchiness. She was similarly funny in the underrated 2005 comedy Bewitched, adapting the old magic-centric sitcom.

But Practical Magic 2 takes its rhythms from Bullock, for better or worse (mostly the latter). She can be a terrific comic actress — take another look at The Heat, where she’s the uptight straight woman to Melissa McCarthy’s raging id — but left to her own devices, Bullock seems to default to a kind of put-upon voice of fake reason that involves a lot of hemming, hawing, and repeating her lines. Repeating so, so many of her lines. It feels like fully half the dialogue in Practical Magic consists of repeated lines. The script isn’t even reiterating jokes, but simulating hustle-and-bustle overlapping chatter by having characters say things two or three times. It also recalls the tell-don’t-show emphasis of a Netflix movie, as does the movie’s extra 25 minutes of runtime. But those aren’t Bullock-specific problems. It’s the repetitive patter, where verbal stalls replace genuine wit, that feels like Bullock slipping on a comfy sweater.

The movie wants so desperately to provide comfort, in fact, that it simply shrugs off the previous movie’s attempt at resolution. Years later, the curse that seemed to be broken remains in effect, having Sally’s husband off-screen, inspiring her to erase memories of magic from the heads of her now-grown daughters Kylie (Joey King) and Antonia (Maisie Williams). (Don’t bother checking the movie’s time-math; it doesn’t work.) When the curse nonetheless threatens to take the life of Kylie’s boyfriend (why wouldn’t it?!), she sets out to break it once an for all, with her mother and aunt hot on her trail. There really isn’t much more to the movie than that. It’s just so choked with exposition, catch-up, and dithering banter that it somehow manages to last a full 130 minutes.

It’s arguable that Beetlejuice Beetlejuice, whose blockbuster success in this early-fall release date likely helped inspire Warner Bros. to spend money on Practical Magic 2, has a similar unwieldiness. But that movie also had Tim Burton on hand, visibly both delighted to be back working in a largely handmade milieu and, through the Winona Ryder character, questioning his legacy. No such artistic curiosity animates this movie. Bullock and Kidman have recruited their mutual director Susanne Bier (Bird Box for Bullock, The Perfect Couple for Kidman, both for Netflix) to nominally supervise their antics. As ever, the horror is pretty lite (and newly superhero-ish, which is to say, crummy-looking) and the rom-com effervescence falls flat even with Lee Pace on hand to yearn. It’s hard to begrudge the existence of Practical Magic 2 as a flex for Bullock and Kidman, two Oscar winners who have now spent decades at or near the top of Hollywood. But neither a theatrical release nor even a straight-to-streaming release seems quite right. Can a movie be released direct to sleepovers? Or back in time?

See It Or Skip It? Should You See Practical Magical 2 in Theaters Opening Weekend?

Like the 1998 original, Practical Magic 2 is a film best suited for at home watching. Save your pennies and wait for Practical Magic 2 to hit SVOD and, eventually, HBO Max. You can have your Midnight Margarita and Practical Magic 2, too!
