California school board defies state by passing policy banning trans athletes from girls sports

2026-11-21 16:59

A California school district has adopted a sweeping new policy barring biological males from girls sports, locker rooms and showers, setting up a potential legal showdown with state officials who warned the district against approving the restrictions.

The Chino Valley Unified School District Board of Education voted 4-1 Thursday to approve the policy authored by outgoing board President Sonja Shaw, despite opposition from California Attorney General Rob Bonta's office and the district's teachers union.

Shaw, who has previously clashed with California officials over parental rights and transgender student policies, said the vote marked a significant escalation in her efforts to protect female athletes and called on other school districts across the state to follow.

"Yesterday we passed a policy I authored that went beyond statements and resolutions," Shaw told OutKick on Friday. "Last night we took it further. We passed actual board policy that establishes clear protections for girls in sports, locker rooms, showers, and overnight school travel."

"These are common-sense measures: no boys competing in girls’ sports, no boys sharing girls’ locker rooms or showers, and no boys and girls sharing hotel rooms on school trips. They protect girls’ privacy and safety in the most basic ways."

Shaw claimed Chino Valley Unified is the first California school district to formally adopt such restrictions, following a resolution she authored in 2025 that was subsequently adopted by other districts.

"We are the first district in California to put these protections into formal board policy," she said. "We have millions of children in public schools across this state, and parents should not have to wonder whether their daughters’ privacy and safety are being protected."

The policy, titled "Fairness in Girls' Athletics and Privacy in Sex-Based Facilities," establishes eligibility for girls athletic teams based on biological sex at birth rather than gender identity.

Under the accompanying administrative regulations, eligibility will generally be determined using existing student enrollment records reflecting sex at or near birth. When those records are unavailable or have been amended, the district may require a birth certificate issued around the time of birth or a statement from a licensed healthcare provider based on a routine athletic physical.

The regulations also explicitly prohibit requiring students to undergo additional examinations, tests or inspections beyond the standard physical required of all athletes.

Shaw said the changes go beyond symbolic opposition to California's existing transgender student policies.

"I authored the original resolution because someone needed to stand up and say enough. Yesterday I took that fight to the next level and gratefully our board passed it with a majority," she said. "I am sick and tired of adults pointing fingers about who is responsible and who can fix it. More districts should follow."

"The only controversy here should be that there was any opposition at all."

The vote came one day after Bonta's office sent the district a letter warning that the proposed restrictions conflicted with California law.

Deputy Attorney General Edward Nugent urged the district to reject or postpone the policy.

"Because the Proposed Policies conflict with Education Code ... the Board should reject the proposal, or at the very least, delay the vote to allow for discussion of the District’s understanding regarding the applicability of state law to the Proposed Policies," Nugent wrote in a Wednesday letter to the school system.

The law, enacted through Assembly Bill 1266 in 2013, has been a central point of conflict between California officials and the Trump administration over transgender participation in girls sports.

Shaw rejected the state's warning and publicly challenged Bonta, arguing that federal law takes precedence over conflicting state requirements.

The district's policy specifically invokes the U.S. Supreme Court's June 30 decision in West Virginia v. B.P.J. and Little v. Hecox , which upheld laws in West Virginia and Idaho restricting participation in female sports to biological females. However that ruling only protects the state laws that currently exist to protect women's sports.

In California, the state continues to maintain laws allowing transgender students to compete according to their gender identity.

In August, a federal district court dismissed the Trump administration's lawsuit against the California Department of Education and CIF over the state's transgender athlete policies. The court did not resolve the broader question of whether transgender participation in girls sports violates Title IX, instead finding insufficient legal notice to justify imposing the federal government's interpretation as a condition of funding.

The Chino Valley policy directs the superintendent to consult legal counsel and take lawful steps to protect students and federal funding if state requirements are applied in a manner that conflicts with the district's new restrictions.

The vote came during Shaw's final meeting on the Chino Valley school board after she unsuccessfully ran for California superintendent of public instruction earlier this month.

Shaw has emerged as one of California's most outspoken school board officials on parental rights and transgender student policies, previously leading efforts to require schools to notify parents about certain changes in their children's gender identification.

That policy became the subject of litigation involving Bonta's office, part of an ongoing dispute over parental notification requirements and state protections for transgender students.

In her latest statement, Shaw directly challenged California's political and educational leadership, including Gov. Gavin Newsom, Bonta, lawmakers, teachers unions and the California Department of Education.

"And let me say it once again to the teachers unions, the Attorney General, our Governor, the state legislators, and the California Department of Education, the same ones who came after me when I proposed a landmark policy to protect parents’ constitutional rights," Shaw said.

"They lost that battle. And they will lose this one too if they choose to challenge federal Title IX and the basic rights of our daughters."

Despite leaving the board, Shaw vowed to continue advocating for sex-based eligibility requirements in girls sports statewide.

"This should be a wake-up call to every school board in California. You have a responsibility to protect all students and that includes protecting our daughters," she said.

"I made those girls a promise, just like I made my daughters a promise. I’m not backing down now or ever. This will be fixed."

OutKick has reached out to Bonta's office, the CIF and California Department of Education for comment.
