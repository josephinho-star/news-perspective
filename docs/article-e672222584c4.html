JONATHAN TURLEY: Baltimore Raven learns truth is no defense in the NFL

2026-11-21 17:48

Baltimore Ravens cornerback Marlon Humphrey will not be playing this Sunday against the Carolina Panthers. He is not on the IL list, but on the FS list. Humphrey was flagged for free speech; uttering four words about the crime problem in Baltimore. The cornerback quipped that the city is "best at making criminals." While Ravens players were allowed to protest the United States at a London game, a player is not allowed to make a political comment on his personal podcast about the crime epidemic in his city. The day Humphrey was suspended, a 30-year-old woman was shot and killed near the M&T Bank Stadium. However, players would be wise not to acknowledge the crime. However, they could presumably condemn the country.

Head Coach Jesse Minter announced that Humphrey had been suspended after the 10-year veteran spoke about crime in the city on his podcast, The Marlon Humphrey Show. He was asked what Baltimore is best at making. Notably, Humphrey first offered The Star-Spangled Banner and National Bohemian beer. He then added as a joke that the city is also "best at making criminals."

Crime in Baltimore makes the city one of the most dangerous cities in the United States. Despite a drop in crime levels, it remains in the top five cities for violent crime.

Notably, Humphrey's comment was not only tongue-in-cheek, but was not racist or prejudiced toward any group. It was not encouraging violence or other bad conduct. On the contrary, it implied criticism of those who continue to endanger others in the city.

Yet, Minter went public with the virtue-signaling suspension.

One can only wonder whether the suspension would have happened if the Ravens were not on a dismal five-game losing streak, ranked fourth in the AFC North, and 11th in the AFC playoff rankings.

We have previously discussed how players are not entitled to protest on the field in relation to the kneeling demonstrations. The same rule applies to other employees from Starbucks to Whole Foods. Employers are allowed to limit political expression and protests in the workplace.

Companies also may contractually subject employees to discipline for public comments or actions that bring controversy or disrepute to the company.

However, that does not make this right. I was critical of kneeling demonstrations during games, but I fully supported players' right to voice their views outside the game.

The NFL is notorious for hypocrisy and inconsistency in punishing free speech. The Humphrey controversy is reminiscent of the Commanders hammering a coach for expressing a different view of January 6.

The NFL has spent years virtue-signaling with statements on helmets and in the end zones. However, when players or coaches express opposing views, they are silenced or suspended.

The NBA is little better. LeBron James can show disrespect to the National Anthem, but Jaden Ivy was fired for expressing his opposition to Pride Month. Players are expected to engage in mandatory speech while remaining silent on their own views.

Nevertheless, Baltimore Mayor Brandon Scott rejected an apology by Humphrey: "While we acknowledge Marlon’s apology, a verbal apology is not enough." He added that Baltimore is "a city full of young Black boys who look to the Ravens as a beacon of light in their lives."

That is a curious objection, since Humphrey was highlighting the failure of Scott to make the city safe to protect those lives, given its top-five ranking for criminal violence.

The message is clear. Players are expected to wear and repeat approved views of the team. For the NFL, speech codes reflect whatever values are popular or approved by NFL commissioner Roger Goodell and team owners.

Now players are on notice that they cannot comment on blue cities being breeding grounds for crime. It is a curious sensitivity for a sport that often sounds like a gangbanger's delight from shotgun and pistol formations.

The NFL and teams should get out of the speech regulation business and focus on football. While they are private companies and not directly subject to First Amendment limits, that does not mean that there are no free speech values at issue in these controversies.

The First Amendment is not the full or exclusive embodiment of free speech. It addresses just one of the dangers to free speech posed by government regulation. Many of us view free speech as a human right. Corporate censorship of social media clearly impacts free speech, and replacing Big Brother with a cadre of Little Brothers actually allows for far greater control of free expression.

Humphrey has now issued the perfunctory apology and is publicly covering himself with sackcloth and ashes. While Baltimore can no longer be called "best at making criminals," the NFL certainly remains the best in coercing silence.

It is fitting that the team was named in reference to Edgar Allan Poe’s poem "The Raven." Any effort by a player to express himself freely in Baltimore will be met by the same answer from Humphrey's attempt at free speech: "Quoth the Raven[s], ‘Nevermore.’"
