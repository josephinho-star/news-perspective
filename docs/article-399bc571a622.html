Play about 12-year-old ecojustice influencer wins Shakespeare’s Globe prize

2026-11-27 15:30

Tiny, a 12-year-old schoolgirl, is an ecojustice influencer, whose funny and disconcerting takes on the climate crisis go viral. She is also an alien, sent to warn humankind of its impending doom – but her mission is far more difficult than she anticipated.

This is the plot of Tiny Alarms, the first winner of Shakespeare’s Globe’s new climate playwriting prize. It was inspired by the real beliefs of schoolchildren for whom its author, Timothy X Atack, ran creative writing workshops exploring ideas around the far future.

“These young people, they were aged 11 to 15, roughly, and the whole group were pretty much united in the idea that theirs would be the last generation of humans. They were really matter of fact about it … in a way that kind of broke my heart and gave me immense fire at the same time,” said Atack.

When the Bristol-based writer, composer and sound designer saw the callout for the prize in February, he believed it was a “truly hopeful exercise”, and he knew this was the story he wanted to tell.

“As a sci-fi writer, I’m used to thinking on these big cosmic scales, and it felt like one of the best things I could do in relation to the climate crisis was to take this planetary threat that we have and weave it into a story that was essentially about parents and children,” he said, adding that as well as addressing “this deeply scary problem”, he also “wanted it to be funny”.

The judging panel, which included Lenny Henry, chose the play because they felt it most captured how it feels to be a young person navigating the climate crisis today.

“It’s the play I want my children to see,” said the prize’s chair, Nancy Medina, artistic director of the Bristol Old Vic. “The innovation, the humour, the journey that this story takes us on is one we all need to take in order to connect back again to our humanity and why we care.”

Amber Massie-Blomfield, director of Fern Culture, which is collaborating with Shakespeare’s Globe on the prize alongside Climate Spring, said the quality of the shortlisted plays “gives the lie to the oft-repeated notions that climate plays are difficult to stage, dystopian, didactic or just plain dull”. She urged venues, producers and funders to stage more climate crisis plays, because, she said, “there is no time to lose”.

Atack will receive £15,000 and support to develop his play. It has also been optioned by the Globe, which launched the prize to reflect how almost every play Shakespeare wrote was about humans and our place in the natural world.

Atack said that although he normally writes with staging flexibility in mind, for this play he was “very definitely thinking about a space like the Globe”.

“Shakespeare is a huge influence in the way that the play is framed, in the way that it starts, and certainly in the ways that lots of the people on stage are able to break out and speak to the audience at whatever moment takes their fancy. That’s very Shakespearean, I feel,” he said.

“There’s aspects of almost kind of like a Julius Caesar-style address that happens at the start of the show, but in this case it’s done by a 12-year-old schoolgirl.”

As well as Shakespeare, Atack is inspired by fantasy and sci-fi writers such Ursula K Le Guin, who use the genres as a “the perfect vehicle for asking about what future we want”. “It’s a wonderful place to ask absolutely huge questions and for them to feel dealable with,” he said.

His favourite scene in the play is when Tiny “confronts her elders and betters with home truths”, he said. “I love the idea of a younger generation stepping up and for instance going to 10 Downing Street, as happens in the play, and saying what Tiny says.”

The play features a number of different view points, from environmental activists to climate deniers, and Atack hopes that “whatever people come away from it with, it’s their own response”.

“I don’t like using plays as a soapbox. I think there are much less expensive soapboxes that you can stand in front of,” he said.

“I’d really like people to create their own meaning from the stories I tell, but if there is a thing that I’d be really happy for this play to achieve, it’s a conversation between generations about how we move forward in the face of this existential threat.”
