Trump doesn’t rule out sending ICE to polling sites; US supreme court to review policy to detain immigrants challenging deportation – live

2026-10-01 23:37

Justices will consider whether immigrants have a right to a hearing to determine if they should remain detained while challenging their possible deportation

Supreme court says it will review whether Trump administration can detain immigrants while they challenge deportation

The supreme court today said it will review whether the Trump administration can enforce its policy of denying bond hearings for immigrants to determine whether they should remain detained while their cases are adjudicated.

The novel move to impose mandatory detention is another feature of Trump’s hardline immigration crackdown during his second term in office, and has now made its way to the top court after several appeals court judges ruled that the policy violated federal law and fails to provide immigrants with due process.

However, those decisions conflict with rulings by the fifth and eighth circuits – which ruled that immigrants accused of illegally entering and remaining in the US must be detained while their deportation cases are pending.

The case that will be part of the supreme court’s docket for the 2026-27 term focuses on Ricardo Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha, a citizen of Brazil, who authorities say he entered the US illegally two decades ago and applied for asylum in 2016 and was granted a work permit.

He sought a bond hearing after an immigration judge determined that his detention was mandatory after his September 2025 arrest. After Barbosa da Cunha sued, a federal judge ordered that be provided a hearing or released. An immigration judge found that Barbosa da Cunha did not present a flight risk or danger to persons or property, and ordered him released on bond. The administration appealed against the decision.

The number of people in immigration detention has shot up since Trump returned to office. It was more than 65,000 in July 2026, up from about 40,000 at the end of Joe Biden’s administration.

Oregon governor's race neck and neck, new poll suggests

In what is shaping up to be a good midterm for Democrats in most of the US, the governor’s race in Oregon could prove to be an exception.

Republican state legislator Christine Drazan is backed by 46% of voters, according to a new poll commissioned by Oregon Public Broadcasting, as the Democratic incumbent governor Tina Kotek hovered close behind with 44%.

A Republican has not been elected to the state’s top office in over four decades.

Kotek defeated Drazan in the 2022 governor’s race, winning 47% of the vote to Drazan’s 43.5%, and making history as one of the first two openly lesbian governors elected that year, along with Maura Healey of Massachusetts.

That race, however, featured a prominent third candidate, Betsy Johnson, a conservative Democrat, who is not on the ballot this year and took 8.6% of the vote.

As OPB reports, in 2022, Drazan led most public polls through November, only to see her lead collapse in the final days of the contest.

In the current rematch, Drazan has taken Kotek to task over her inability to curb homelessness in Oregon and the recent exodus of major corporate hubs during her tenure. Meanwhile, Kotek has accused Drazan of being more loyal to Trump than to Oregonians.

Trump repeats familiar false claims about Venezuela, the Saudi king and Biden's favorite ice cream

Donald Trump’s remarks in Texas, weeks before the midterm elections his party is in danger of losing, have just concluded with the ritual playing of YMCA.

Trump’s speech veered very far away from a discussion of the virtues of the Republican Senate candidate he was there to praise, Ken Paxton, and devolved instead into a typical rally speech, in which the president recited a number of boasts and false claims that he has made previously.

Among other things unrelated to the Texas Senate race, Trump once again claimed that the Saudi king told him, during his visit to the kingdom in May 2025, that the United States was “dead” during the presidency of Joe Biden.

“American trucking is back. Two years ago, our country was dead. You know, this was said to me by the king, by the leader, by the crown prince of Saudi Arabia,” Trump said.

The president has made this claim, that the Saudi crown prince, Mohammed bin Salman, told him the US was “a dead country” before he returned and instantly made it ”the hottest country in the world”, dozens of times since he returned from Riyadh in early 2025. In fact, a review of video from Trump’s visit to Saudi Arabia reveals that he made this remark to the Saudi crown prince in a televised speech, rather than the other way around.

Trump then repeated the complaint he has been making for at least six years: that reporters went so easy on Biden that they repeatedly asked him what his favorite flavor of ice cream was.

“They never used to ask him hard questions,” Trump told the crowd, before recreating what he claimed was a frequent interaction the press had with Biden. “You remember, they’d always say, how many times have you heard them say, ‘what’s your favorite ice cream’? That was the hardest question, ‘Mr President sir, sir, what’s your favorite ice cream’? ‘Vanilla’. ‘Thank you sir’”.

While Biden was frequently seen eating ice cream, and took criticism for dodging questions from reporters, particularly during his term in office, the idea that he was never asked uncomfortable questions by reporters is false – as is the claim Trump makes every time he tells this story, that Biden’s favorite flavor is vanilla.

During his 2020 campaign against Trump, Biden did share a letter sent to him by a young boy who asked what kind of ice cream he liked. Biden replied that the boy should enjoy some “chocolate chip ice cream, my favorite flavor”.

Trump also repeated his false claim that the former Venezuelan president, Nicolás Maduro, had “emptied his prisons into the United States of America. All of his prisoners were emptied into Texas.” There is no evidence that any such thing happened, despite Trump’s repeated claims that it did.

Late in the speech, Trump again repeated his false claim that Paxton’s opponent, Democrat James Talarico, is a vegan – which he again mispronounced as ‘vaygan’.

South Korea disputes Trump's claim it agreed to fund $54bn Alaska pipeline – report

While we wait for the start of Donald Trump’s remarks in Texas, let’s pause for a moment to note that the government of South Korea on Thursday reportedly denied that it had agreed, as the president claimed with great fanfare on Wednesday, to spend $54bn to build a liquefied natural gas pipeline in Alaska.

In an Oval Office announcement on Wednesday, flanked by Dan Sullivan, a Republican senator facing a tough re-election battle, Trump said that South Korea had agreed to pay to build an 807-mile (1,299km) pipeline to carry natural gas from Alaska’s North Slope to a liquefaction facility on Alaska’s southern coast.

The Korean investment, Trump said, was part of a $200bn package of US projects that South Korea agreed to in 2025 as part of a trade deal in exchange for lower US tariffs on South Korean cars, auto parts and other products.

The White House then released video of Trump touting the investment.

On Thursday, however, the Wall Street Journal reported, the South Koreans insisted that they did not “agree to a specific dollar figure—or even to invest at all.”

South Korea’s industry ministry said that the government had only agreed to “initiate a review” of the pipeline deal.

South Korea’s industry minister, Kim Jung-kwan, took issue with to Trump’s characterization of the country’s Alaska LNG investment. “Putting out figures, or using definitive language, goes far beyond what was agreed,” he said at a briefing for reporters.

Reuters reported that the South Korean industry minister also he had expressed regret to the US commerce secretary, Howard Lutnick, over the White House announcement, since it went beyond what had been agreed.

The Trump administration broke the law when it pushed through a last-minute change to the form overseas citizens use to register and request absentee ballots, the Democratic National Committee (DNC) said in a lawsuit filed on Thursday.

Until recently, the form, the Federal Post Card Application (FPCA), gave overseas voters four options to choose from when describing their geographic location. Voters could say they needed an overseas ballot because they were on active military service, living outside the US with an intent to return, or living outside the US and uncertain about their return. The form also allowed voters to check a box if they were a US citizen who had never lived in the country (38 states and the District of Columbia allow citizens born abroad to vote in at least some elections even if they have never lived in the country).

In August, as the deadline to send out overseas ballots approached, the Pentagon – the agency that oversees the form – submitted two emergency requests to modify the form. Among the changes, the Pentagon sought to eliminate the option allowing voters to say they were citizens who never lived in the US. The requests were quickly approved by the Office of Management and Budget (OMB). NPR first reported the changes earlier this week.

“Trump and Republicans are so desperate to hang on to power that they are trying to eliminate the way some American citizens abroad register to vote and request mail ballots. They know that Americans, both at home and abroad, are going to reject their destructive agenda, and they are scrambling to disenfranchise as much of the electorate as possible by any means necessary,” said Ken Martin, the chair of the DNC.

The Pentagon did not return a request for comment.

The changes could confuse and discourage US citizens who have never lived in the country from registering and casting a ballot, experts said.

Trump administration excludes UK, France and Baltic allies from Nato meeting in Poland - report

The Pentagon is set to host a meeting with several Nato members in Poland next month, however there are notable exclusions. Namely, the UK, France and several Baltic countries, according to Politico.

Citing six unnamed diplomats familiar with the planning, Politico reports that the gathering will be led by Elbridge Colby, the Pentagon’s policy chief, and will be a “working group focused on Nato’s future”.

Neither the White House or Pentagon responded to the Guardian’s request for confirmation or comment.

Along with the UK and France, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania are also reportedly not included in the scheduled meeting, despite all of the countries spending significantly on military and defense.

The nations that are set to attend are Norway, Sweden, Finland, Poland, Germany and the Netherlands. Colby has held two similar meetings with the invitees earlier this year.

One of the diplomats interviewed by Politico said that splintering the alliance by hand-picking which countries to include could only add to the already fractious relationship with the US.

“If they try splitting Nato into the good ones and the bad ones, it is dangerous for the US,” they told the outlet. “But for Europe, this is existential.”

Federal prosecutors in Washington DC can never again charge the former Olympic canoeist David Hearn over damage to the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool, a judge ruled on Thursday, closing a case the US attorney’s office had already conceded it should not have brought.

Judge Todd Edelman, of the DC superior court, dismissed the case with prejudice, siding with Hearn’s lawyers over the office of the US attorney Jeanine Pirro, which had wanted to keep the option of refiling.

Pirro, a Donald Trump ally, publicized the charges at a press conference in July. Later that month her office moved to drop the case, conceding that the damage resulted from flawed installation rather than anything Hearn did.

Trump, who had pressed for the prosecution, told reporters in the Oval Office on 3 August that Pirro had “choked” in abandoning the vandalism charges, and “folded like an umbrella”.

Edelman agreed to the dismissal in August but left open whether it would be with or without prejudice. Prosecutors argued for the latter, which would have allowed a future revival. Hearn’s lawyers countered that the case was politically driven and lacked factual support, and warned of a risk of “harassment and bad faith” if the door stayed open.
