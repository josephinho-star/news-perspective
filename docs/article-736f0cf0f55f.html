Badenoch expected to announce plan to cut inheritance tax and claim UK faces ‘battle of ideas’ over spending – UK politics live

2026-10-07 09:14

What policies has Badenoch announced at conference, who gains, and what it says about Tory strategy

When Ed Davey, the Lib Dem leader, gave his conference speech two weeks ago, he was clearly making a pitch for the moderate Tories who either switched to his party two years ago, or who might cross over at the next election. Since 2024, on migration and Europe, Kemi Badenoch has been sounding increasingly like Nigel Farage. As a result, for one nation Conservatives, her party has been a turn-off.

Now that is changing. The story of this conference is that – belatedly, but significantly – Badenoch is trying to win them back.

That is best illustrated by looking at the headline policies either announced at this conference or in the run-up to it. They fall roughly into three categories.

These are proposals that don’t benefit a particular demographic, but which arguably are good for the nation as a wh0le. They are, however, almost all ideas that would appeal to people with distinct, rightwing views.

The welfare proposal seems to penalise young people, although the Tories would claim that ultimately they will benefit from a system that does not consign them to a life on benefits. In fiscal terms, it is hard to gauge to what extent the young would lose out because the Tories have not provided clear costings.

Badenoch is sensitive to the claim that her party only favours wealthy, older voters, and there have been two announcements that do seem designed to appeal to younger people.

Younger voters may be sceptical about whether these proposals will do a lot for inter-generational fairness. They are both announcements that primarily benefit businesses. Builders might sell homes more cheaply because red tape has been cut, but they might just pocket the gains. And it is employers who pay employer national insurance – although the move should help young people by boosting their employment prospects.

Policies for older, wealthier people

While the policies for younger people may seem relatively marginal, the Conservatives have announced two measures that will potentially have a huge impact on a smallish number of older, wealthy people.

9) Repealing the mansion tax. Andrew Griffith, the shadow chancellor, announced this in his speech on Monday. This is how the party explained it in a note it sent out later.

The Family Homes Tax, which Labour call a High Value Council Tax Surcharge, was announced by Rachel Reeves in her 2025 budget, and is due to start in April 2028. It will add between £2,500 and £7,500 a year to the council tax bill of every home in England affected. Even the lowest band costs more than the average Band D council tax bill in England, which stands at £2,392 this year, so the surcharge alone will cost more than a typical family pays for all its council services. However, there have been recent reports that the threshold could be lowered even further …

A Conservative government will abolish the Family Homes Tax outright, and if Labour legislates before the election a Conservative government will repeal it. The Family Homes Tax is an unfair tax raid on the homes people have worked hard for. It is a purely political and spiteful tax raid by Labour. The tax is expected to raise just £400 million a year compared to the over £50 billion a year raised by the council tax system. It adds huge complexity for very little gain in terms of overall revenue.

Interestingly, asked about the pensions triple lock yesterday, Badenoch said it would be difficult to reverse Labour legislation on this if it becomes law because that money would be spent elsewhere. But she is not applying that logic here.

10) A cut in inheritance tax? If that does happen in the speech later, that will clearly be a third, significant bung to older, wealthy people.

You don’t need to be John Curtice to see what is going on here.

Shadow justice secretary Nick Timothy claims police have been 'corrupted by ideology of multiculturalism'

At the Tory conference, Nick Timothy, the shadow justice secretary, is speaking now. He is recalling the failings of the police over the Maccabi Tel Aviv fan ban, which he highlighted in parliament, and he claims this is symptomatic of wider failings with the police. He says:

As I campaigned to expose the reality of what happened, more and more emerged about life in many of our towns and cities, about police leaders so compromised, so corrupted by the ideology of multiculturalism.

They surrendered their operational independence to those they should have faced down, including many they should have locked up. That is something we should never, ever tolerate.

And I promise you, under Kemi’s Conservatives, we never will.

Voters more likely to reject Badenoch's claim Tories have changed under her leadership than to agree, poll suggests

Kemi Badenoch claims that the Conservative party has changed considerably under her leadership, and that as a result it does not deserve to be tainted by the terrible reputation of the last Conservative government. In one of her interviews yesterday, she said:

We are under new management. Your viewers will know that sometimes you have a football team that’s disappointing. There’s a new captain, there’s a new coach, there’s a new owner, effectively, which is all me.

In some respects, this is true. The Conservatives are now committed to leaving the European convention on human rights, a treaty backed by every previous Tory leader ever since it was drawn up in the 1950s. On net zero and migration, the party has also shifted sharply to the right.

In other respects, the party has not changed that much at all. Badenoch is presenting herself as a modern Margaret Thatcher, but the “battle of ideas” extract from her speech released overnight (see 8am) is mainstream Conservatism.

According to a YouGov poll published yesterday, voters are more likely to think that the Conservative party has not changed under Badenoch (46%) than it has (30%).

As Andrew McDonald and Megan McElroy point out in their London Playbook briefing, there are broadly two options if Kemi Badenoch wants to cut inheritance tax without getting rid of it completely. They report:

There were persistent rumors and some reporting before this conference that the Tories were looking to scrap that tax altogether. But since then, Badenoch’s team has been making more cautious noises. Right-leaning papers including the Telegraph, Times and Sun all report this morning that Badenoch will cut IHT, but not scrap it. That would sit well with the broad theme of the speech on the state of the public finances, given inheritance tax is expected to raise more than £10 billion a year from next year onwards. To put it another way: scrapping IHT would effectively cost the same as building a “Britannia Shield” every year.

Instead: The Times’ Oliver Wright and co. reckon Badenoch will announce a cut to the 40% inheritance tax rate, which only applies to an estate’s value over £325,000 (or £500,000 if a home is given to a direct descendant). If that is what Badenoch opts for … it’s still an expensive tax cut. Estimates from 2023, the last time the Tories were looking at this, reckoned a 10 percentage point cut to 30% would cost a few billion a year. The other rumor doing the rounds is that she could change the thresholds at which IHT kicks in.

Inheritance tax (IHT) s one of the most curious taxes in the Treasury’s armoury. It is one of the most unpopular of all taxes levied by HM Revenue and Customs, with polling showing strong support for getting rid of it entirely, even though most people never pay it. The explanation for this paradox is partly the visceral belief, widely shared, that parents should be able to pass on their assets to their children. (Another factor is that inheritance tax is also very unpopular with the sort of rich people who own newspapers, even though Kemi Badenoch claims they never have to pay.)

Speaking about IHT on the Today prograamme this morning, Helen Miller, director of the Institute for Fiscal Studies thinktank, said:

It’s a tax that is charged on estates when people die, but only around one in 20 estates will actually end up paying it.

The threshold for inheritance tax is £325,000, which means it is not charged on any estates worth less than that. If a home is being given away to children, the threshold rises to £500,000, and for couples the threshold rises to £1m. “So most people don’t go anywhere near the tax,” Miller said.

There are more details of how the tax works here. And here is a Commons library briefing on IHT including this chart showing how many people pay it.

Badenoch expected to propose inheritance tax cut in speech to Tories saying UK faces 'battle of ideas' over spending

Good morning. Kemi Badenoch is expected to announce cuts to inheritance tax (IHT) this morning in a keynote conference speech that will claim the next election will be a “battle of ideas” between a government spending ever more money, and a Conservative party determined to make the state live within its means.

The Tories have not confirmed that inheritance tax cuts will be proposed. Equally, they have done nothing at all to quash speculation that Badenoch will make an announcement along these lines, to the point where it is now hard to see how she can deliver a speech that does not mention IHT without causing huge disappointment to her supporters. IHT is set to raise £15bn a year for the Treasury by the end of the decade, and Badenoch is expected to propose cutting it rather than getting rid of it completely.

While any tax announcement is likely to dominate the headline, the Tories are also set to publish a “savings plan” today that will explain where they will find the money for tax cuts, including for the £2.3bn cut to employer national insurance for young people announced by the party last night. At their conference last year, the Conservatives said they had identified cuts to government spending worth £47bn, with £23bn coming from welfare. Yesterday Helen Whately, the shadow work and pensions secretary, said she had found “billions more” in cuts, in addition to those on the table last year.

The Conservatives have released some extracts from Badenoch’s speech in advance, and she will focus on an assertion that there is now a firm dividing line between Labour and the Tories on public spending. She will say:

There is a fight coming in British politics.

A fight that we have not seen in this country for more than half a century.

It will be a battle for the soul of this nation.

A battle of ideas.

A battle that goes to the very core of who we are and what kind of country we want to be.

On one side we have a government that wants to wind back the clock to failed ideas of the past.

They believe economic growth is created by politicians.

They think government knows best.

They think that your income, your pension and your savings are theirs to take and then hand out as they see fit.

They claim that what they are doing is compassionate but we know that these ideas will make every single person in this country poorer.

Your taxes will be higher. Your bills will go up. You will find it harder and harder to make ends meet.

Just because these ideas are delivered on TikTok doesn’t make them any less dangerous than they have always been.

On the other side of the battle is us.

A renewed Conservative party fighting again for the belief that what people earn belongs to them, not government.

That the collective wisdom of people in our country is greater than the central planning of bureaucrats and politicians.

That spending money we don’t have on things we can’t afford is not kind, it is reckless.

Here is the agenda for the day.

10am: Kevin Hollinrake, the Tory chair, opens the session. There are also speeches from Chris Philp, the shadow home secretary, and Nick Timothy, the shadow justice secretary.

Around 11.15am: Kemi Badenoch delivers her keynote speech.
