Death toll rises to 68 with dozens injured after high-speed train collision in Spain – latest updates

2026-10-28 17:22

At least 68 people have been killed after two passenger trains collided in Toledro province in central Spain, and the death toll may rise further.

A similar number of people remain hospitalised, including 15 in serious or critical state (15:44).

The two trains with 544 people on board in total (10:46) were travelling in the same direction on the high-speed line from the Spanish capital, Madrid, to Seville in the south-west of the country.

Video footage from the scene showed extensive damage to both trains (12:23, 13:52).

Spain’s prime minister Pedro Sánchez said the country was “in mourning” as he insisted the government will get to the bottom of incident and address potential failures with “utmost firmness” (15:49).

Transport minister Óscar Puente said initial review of the evidence pointed to an “inexplicable” decision to order trains to go ahead after a signalling failure (15:01), implying a human error.

The investigation is likely to focus on 17 minutes between the trains were halted and the crash.

Spanish media reported that the controller responsible was questioned by investigators this afternoon (16:40).

Today’s collision is the second high-profile train crash in Spain this year (11:00), and the third deadliest incident on its railway in the last 50 years (12:55).

Spain is expected to declare three days of mourning later today (15:54).

Both main parties suspended their campaigning activities ahead of next month’s parliamentary election, but the far-right Vox party continued with its rallies (12:11).

There are no more injured people requiring medical attention at the site of the accident involving the AVE high-speed train and the Iryo train, sources told El Pais.

Minister's comments show attempt to get ahead of growing anger over rail infrastructure - snap analysis

The speed with which Spain’s transport minister has laid out the broad circumstances of the crash appears unusually fast for the aftermath of an accident, where politicians and industry leaders are anxious not to pre-empt investigations.

That may be due to the scale of the crash and the nature of the findings – but also owe much to the growing anger and concern about the state of the nation’s railway infrastructure. And perhaps a desire to demonstrate that, for this second horrific crash this year on the Madrid-Seville line, the circumstances were apparently very different.

Although the official investigation is yet to come, Óscar Puente has pointed towards human error as the predominant cause (15:01) – an “inexplicable decision” to tell trains to continue operations when the outage of the signalling system had brought services to a standstill.

That makes the crash – on one level – move from the narrative where the trains are literally coming off the rails, and the country metaphorically so, as elections approach.

But questions will remain. The recent collision in Bedford in Britain was similar in its rarity: a train likewise hit the back of another stopped on the same line, albeit at a lower speed and with only one fatality. That crash continues to be investigated, but first findings show that while human error may have been the biggest cause, it arose from a set of unusual circumstances and a technical failure in the automated warning system which has yet to be fully explained.

Spain has identified individual culprits in past disasters: prosecuting the train driver who sped recklessly round a corner in 2013 in Galicia, killing 79 people. Police are apparently questioning the line controller today.

But the public will want to know what led to the initial outage of a signalling system that was due to be phased out – and what circumstances or daily experience could have pressed someone to urge the trains to continue regardless, however tragically wrong that decision proved to be.

Spain 'will be rightfully asking questions,' opposition leader says, as he calls for investigation, review of rail infrastructure

In the last few minutes, the opposition People’s party (PP)‘s leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo responded to the incident near Urda.

In a sombre speech, Feijóo expressed his solidarity with all affected and their families, and thanked emergency services for their response.

But he said that “our grief does not compel us to remain silent,” as he said the Spanish people will be “rightfully asking questions – and they deserve answers.”

He called for an immediate government investigation and a judicial inquiry into the crash, “utilising all available resources and free from interference.”

He also demanded a response to growing concerns about the state of infrastructure, as he called for “a comprehensive, independent review of the entire railway network.”

“This can no longer be delayed; the public has a right to full accountability for every euro.”

He also confirmed that his party has for now suspended all campaigning events ahead of next month’s parliamentary election as a sign of respect for the victims and their families (unlike the far-right Vox party).
