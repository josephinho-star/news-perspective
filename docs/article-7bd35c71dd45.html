AI company behind FAA’s SMART tool denies link to ground stop, Duffy confirms

2026-11-14 04:04

Air Space Intelligence (ASI), the company behind a recently deployed Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) AI tool, denied any link to the nationwide ground stop issued Friday afternoon.

“The current ground stop is not based on or a result of the SMART system, any product of ASI or our integration with FAA and ATC,” the company said in a 5:34 p.m. EST post to social platform X, while the stoppage was still in effect.

The nationwide grounding began Friday afternoon and lasted for around four hours. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy wrote in a 4:16 p.m. post on social platform X the FAA made the order “following a communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers.”

The airspace was opened at around 8:15 p.m. and the stop was lifted, Duffy said during a White House briefing that began 15 minutes later. The secretary said the incident resulted from an overload of data on routers.

“Our switches, our routers, actually got overloaded with data,” Duffy said. “They started to give us inconsistency in the data from those … routers. And because of that, we shut the system down.”

The FAA deployed the new Strategic Management of Airspace, Routing and Trajectories — or SMART — tool in September in Washington, D.C. The AI-powered system, which cost the Transportation Department $875 million, is meant to ingest real-time data as well as current and future weather conditions to recommend necessary flight path changes to air traffic controllers, Duffy said at the time.

During Friday’s briefing, the secretary asserted the SMART tool had nothing to do with the ground stop, saying the system “isn’t plugged in.”

“This is completely disconnected, and I love that some people have said this would be the root cause. By the way, if it was, I’ll tell you that, but this is not even plugged in,” he said. “So, there’s no possible way SMART would have impacted.”

Duffy also asked Congress for $50 billion to rebuild what he described as an “incredibly old” air traffic control system, aligning with his ongoing efforts to modernize U.S. air travel. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin echoed Duffy’s call, saying, “It has to be a complete overhaul of the system.”

In September, Duffy had asked the Congress for $30 billion to fund new technology, control towers and airport improvements across the country.
