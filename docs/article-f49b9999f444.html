Earlier in the day following Canada’s announcement, Trump also announced plans to exclude Canadian products from large US government contracts.

Shortly after the US Supreme Court decided not to intervene in the battle over Missouri’s new congressional map earlier today, a federal judge temporarily barred the state from using old districts in the November elections.

Last week Missouri’s highest state court blocked the use of a newly drawn congressional map backed by Donald Trump, ruling that voters must approve or reject the new districts. The map had already been used in August primary elections.

The decision outraged Donald Trump and Catherine Hanaway, the state’s Republican attorney general, who immediately pledged to appeal to the supreme court. The supreme court, however, turned back the emergency appeal on Tuesday, letting the state court’s ruling stand.

But almost immediately after, a federal judge in Missouri issued a temporary restraining order that bars Missouri from using anything other than the new Republican backed map. Hanaway said in a statement that Missouri would “follow and comply” with the judge’s order.

“We maintain that changing the congressional map in between the primary and general elections is simply not feasible and profoundly unjust to Missouri voters,” she said.

Missouri was the second Republican state to act in response the president’s push to redraw congressional maps to favor Republicans. Mike Kehoe, the state’s governor, called the legislature into a special session last year to create new boundaries ahead of the US midterm elections. The state utilized the new map in its primary elections in August.

But last week the state’s supreme court, which includes five Republican-appointed judges, unanimously ruled voters must approve the map in November before it can be utilized. The state’s attorney general argued the decisionthrustMissouri into a “full‑fledged constitutional crisis”.

The court of appeals for the District of Columbia upheld a block by a lower court judge that prevents the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) from sharing tax return information with Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE).

“No statute authorizes the IRS to disclose ‘return information’ for non-tax civil investigations without the taxpayer’s consent, or to disclose ‘taxpayer return information’ for any purpose,” the court writes in its ruling.

The court also noted that IRS’ argument against the preliminary injunction – namely that it “impedes and delays federal law enforcement by improperly restricting the information that ICE is entitled to receive” – is actually a problem that needs to be addressed through legislation in Congress, not the court.

The judges added that the administration’s claim is “weak sauce”.

A US appellate court has decided that the federal constitution does not guarantee a right to clean water.

That legal opinion came in the form of a decision on Friday from the fifth circuit court of appeals to uphold a lower court’s dismissal of a 2022 lawsuit from several Jackson, Mississippi, residents claiming that tainted drinking water violated their constitutional rights.

Those residents of Mississippi’s capital city contended that their local drinking water was unsafe to drink because of high lead levels and other contaminants.

The New Orleans-based fifth circuit’s majority opinion held that the residents wanted “recognition of two new constitutional rights lacking any foundation in the history and tradition of our nation” – those being “the right to be free from exposure to contaminated water and the right to accurate information from public officials”.

In the majority’s view, residents should have gone about their legal fight for clean water another way, such as pursuing “tort claims” or by “electing representatives who will better manage the public-water system, and petitioning their representatives for other remedies”.

Tort claims allege “harms caused by others” while seeking “to impose liability on parties responsible for the harm, and to deter others from committing harmful acts”, according to Cornell University’s law school.

Friday’s decision also said that state and federal officials were working hard to improve the majority-Black city’s drinking water. The residents who filed suit alleged that public authorities had for years failed to mitigate lead levels and failed to inform them about an increase.
