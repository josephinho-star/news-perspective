Fast Takes: Chuck Schumer’s thankless job, gov’t shutdowns hurt average Americans and more

2026-11-23 01:12

From the left: Chuck’s Thankless Job

As progressives push to oust Chuck Schumer as Senate Democratic leader, explains Vox’s Andrew Prokop, realize that the job “isn’t really about winning a national popularity contest,” but “a multitude of tasks” from “crafting legislative and big-picture policy strategy” to “dealing with outside interest groups” and “trying to hold the majority in the next election.” One “underappreciated” key is “volunteering to be the one political figure who’s most likely to be hated,” as were “many of the leaders considered especially effective in their role — from Mitch McConnell to Nancy Pelosi”: Much of what the “base wants can’t happen,” so “many disappointments are inevitable, and that the leader will be the fall guy for them.”

Liberal: Gov’t Shutdowns Hurt Average Americans

“Donald Trump’s decision to start a war with Iran was stupid,” but so “would be provoking a government shutdown in the name of ending the war,” roars the Washington Monthly’s Bill Scher. As the Dec. 11 government funding deadline approaches, Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen “has made refusing to fund the war a rallying cry” in his bid to be Dems’ Senate leader. Yet a shutdown would mean “unpaid furloughs for thousands of federal employees two weeks before Christmas,” and a long shutdown could leave “disgusted with both parties for playing political games.” Rather than battling with Trump, Dems can get tangible “affordability” outcomes by focusing on “passing bipartisan tariff reform” instead of being drawn “into another shutdown morass.”

Republican: Democrats’ No. 1 Risk

You can’t fault Democrats for feeling “giddy” after an “election in which they picked up at least 43 House and nine Senate seats,” but The Wall Street Journal’s Karl Rove warns that the “election wasn’t a seismic shift toward Democrats but a rejection of Donald Trump.” Voters “felt he wasn’t focused on rising prices and the economy,” compounded by his “mishandling of immigration enforcement,” “Iran and anger at his self-enrichment schemes.” But Democrats’ energetic “democratic-socialist wing” is “a loser with the broader electorate.” For firebreathing Democrats, “overplaying the oversight card could easily backfire with voters who want more action on inflation and less political infighting.” If they don’t learn from how Trump misread his 2024 win, Democrats “risk misjudging their 2026 victory.”

Eye on ’28: Blacks Key To Ending Dems’ Divides

Analyst Nate Silver “does more than broaden understanding of the intra-Democratic divide,” argues The Washington Post’s Henry Olsen, he “points to the central role that Black voters and leading candidates will play in resolving it.” Silver sees Dems divided on two axes: “ideology and inclination.” It’s the far left vs. the left, but also “fighters (people who want to battle opponents, whether within the party or outside it)” vs. “uniters (those more inclined to find common ground with adversaries).” Blacks are likely the swing bloc, so in the 2028 presidential primaries, watch out for a Black, lefty uniter like ex-Veep Kamala Harris Gov. Wes Moore (Md.) or Sen. Raphael Warnock (Ga.). GOP strategists “salivate at the prospect of” a progressive Democratic nominee; establishment Democrats prefer a “unifying moderate” — but: Don’t be surprised if the eventual nominee, drawing on strong support from Black voters, splits the difference.”

Conservative: Media Touts Fake Sanctions Expert

“The commentary of a ‘sanctions expert,’ Brett Erickson,” emerged “around the start of the Iran War” and appeared in “media outlets around the country,” widely cited “expounding on how the Iranians were likely to outgun President Donald Trump at every twist and turn of the war, and otherwise “slamming the administration,” reports The Free Beacon’s Eliana Johnson. Yet he’s a 29-year-old with “little professional expertise” in the areas of “financial crime and geopolitics” for which he claims to be an expert. He “has amassed a litany of impressive-sounding credentials” by paying for “online certifications” without “ever actually working in the area”; he claims to be the principal of a company, but its main office “is registered at a single-family home in Arizona.”
