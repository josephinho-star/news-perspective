School cell phone bans are boosting grades — and teachers are reporting even bigger classroom benefits

2026-11-18 19:28

Put down the phone, pick up the GPA.

A new Harvard study suggests school cellphone bans may be paying off in more ways than one, with students showing modest gains in both math and reading when their devices are out of reach.

The research, published by Harvard’s Center for Education Policy Research, found that students at schools with strict phone bans made academic gains equivalent to roughly one to two extra weeks of learning per year.

Researchers tracked 3.3 million students in grades three through 10 across 4,600 US schools between 2021 and 2026, comparing individual students’ academic growth at schools with phone restrictions against those without them.

The biggest boost came at schools that required students to lock their phones in pouches for the entire school day — not just stash them away during lessons.

And the benefits weren’t reserved for students struggling to keep up.

Researchers found gains across achievement levels, while fewer students saw their reading scores slide over the course of the school year.

Still, banning phones isn’t a magic fix for America’s slipping test scores.

Student performance has been declining since the mid-2010s, with researchers pointing to a range of contributing factors, from pandemic-related school disruptions to chronic absenteeism.

The findings suggest phone restrictions could be a relatively inexpensive way to give learning a lift, but researchers cautioned that their success depends on how schools put the policies into practice.

The results add fresh fuel to the debate over New York’s own bell-to-bell cellphone ban, which bars K-12 public and charter school students from using personal internet-enabled devices throughout the school day.

Gov. Kathy Hochul signed the measure into law on May 9, 2025, as part of the state budget. It took effect the following day, with districts, charter schools and BOCES required to adopt their own policies by Aug. 1.

Schools began enforcing the restrictions at the start of the 2025-2026 academic year, keeping smartphones, tablets and smartwatches out of students’ hands from the first bell to the last — including during lunch, recess and passing periods.

But not all phone bans are created equal. The strongest academic gains in the Harvard study came from schools using lockable pouches to keep devices out of reach all day.

Less restrictive policies, which relied on students simply keeping their phones put away during class, also showed some gains, though researchers were less certain of those results because the schools differed in other ways.

And while local educators haven’t exactly been handing out report cards showing a dramatic academic turnaround, some say they’ve noticed changes in the classroom.

Long Island high school teacher Randi A. told The Post that ever since the ban was enacted, her students have become more focused when tuning into her lessons and they’re also much better at interacting with their peers face-to-face.

“Students are paying more attention in class discussions and activities,” the Nassau County educator said, adding that “classroom management has been easier for most teachers in my school.”

“They are engaging in class lessons, showing an improvement in classwork and verbal communication skills,” she added.

But taking away smartphones doesn’t necessarily mean students are logging off entirely.

Victoria Vilkas, a second-grade teacher in Rego Park, Queens, said her younger students haven’t noticed much of a difference — phones were already banned at her school before the statewide restrictions took effect.

Instead, she said, school-issued devices were presenting their own distractions.

“They are so used to being on devices that constantly open other tabs on the Chromebook even during tests,” she said, adding that YouTube videos and online games are “an addiction” for her students outside of school — a habit that can follow them into the classroom.

Randi A. agreed and added that her students often “don’t know what time it is because they don’t know how to read an analog clock” without their cell phones.

Vilkas, who is also a mother of a high schooler, said she had heard from teenagers that adjusting to communicating with friends in person rather than through their phones “had been a challenge” since the ban was enacted.

John T., an English teacher at a Nassau County high school, offered a more measured assessment during the ban’s early days.

While he noticed students chatting with one another after finishing their assignments “instead of immediately reaching for their phones,” he hadn’t necessarily seen a dramatic improvement in classroom attention.

He also questioned whether the restrictions “needed to extend to lunchtime,” particularly for students who “might not have friends to sit with.”

Ultimately, the Harvard findings suggest that limiting access to smartphones during school hours may offer academic benefits — even if the full picture of how students are affected goes beyond test scores.
