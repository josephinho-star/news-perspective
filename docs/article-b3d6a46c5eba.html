Google's AI answer engine is quietly strangling open web

2026-09-06 21:05

On Aug. 20, Google unveiled a small, almost bureaucratic-sounding feature: a "Preferred Sources" button that publishers can embed on their own websites so readers can flag them as a favourite source across Search, Discover and Google News. Framed as a gesture of goodwill toward an embattled news industry, the announcement was in fact something closer to an admission. When the world's dominant gatekeeper of information starts building buttons to nudge a fraction of lost traffic back toward the websites it increasingly bypasses, it is because the underlying architecture of the web's economy has already broken.

For a quarter-century, the compact between Google and the open web was simple: publishers let Google crawl and index their content, and in exchange, Google sent them readers. That readership converted into advertising revenue, subscriptions, and the everyday sustainability of journalism, encyclopaedic knowledge, and countless small businesses built on organic search. That compact is now unwinding in real time, and the numbers are startling enough to qualify as a structural rupture rather than a passing algorithm update.

Today, that compact is unravelling before our eyes. The statistics no longer point to a routine algorithmic tweak. According to Chartbeat data spanning more than 2,500 news sites worldwide, Google search referrals fell by roughly a third over the year leading to late 2025, with American publishers hit even harder at around 38%. The independent measurement firm SparkToro estimates that "zero-click" searches – queries that end without a single visit to any external website – now account for roughly 68% of all Google searches in 2026, up from 60% in 2024 and just 49% in 2019.

Newer data exposes an even deeper crisis. According to an extensive study by Seer Interactive covering thousands of search terms through late 2025, organic and paid click-through rates (CTRs) have plummeted by 61% and 68%, respectively, since the widespread launch of AI Overviews. Even when a website is cited within an AI overview, its organic CTR now averages just 0.70% – a 49.4% drop year-over-year. For uncited websites, the rate is an abysmal 0.52%. When no AI overview appears, CTRs traditionally hit 1.45%. Google's interface is intercepting the vast majority of intent before users ever click. The damage to individual publishers has, in places, been severe. HubSpot estimated losing 70% to 80% of its organic search traffic. Education platform Chegg reported a 49% year-on-year decline in non-subscriber visits. DMG Media, owner of the Daily Mail, told the U.K.'s Competition and Markets Authority that click-through rates on its content collapsed by as much as 89% on desktop and 87% on mobile whenever an AI Overview appeared above its link – a submission serious enough that NPR described the broader phenomenon as an "extinction-level event" for online news.

Smaller and midsized outlets, lacking the brand recognition or direct-audience relationships of legacy players, appear to be absorbing the deepest cuts. This raises an uncomfortable question about media pluralism: if search is quietly re-engineered to reward only the largest, best-resourced publishers with any residual traffic, what happens to the smaller, local- language, and specialist outlets that depend on the same pipeline? It is worth being precise about why this is happening, because the mechanics matter more than the outrage. At its I/O 2026 developer conference, Google unveiled what it called the biggest overhaul of its search box in 25 years. Shortly after, CEO Sundar Pichai described a vision of Search evolving from a retrieval engine into an "agent manager" – a system that does not merely point users toward information but acts on their behalf, synthesizing answers and executing tasks without ever routing them elsewhere. With Google actively selling advertising directly inside AI Overviews, the company's revenue model and product roadmap are optimized around keeping users inside Google's ecosystem. Publishers are not collateral damage; they are the resource being strip-mined.

Physical chokepoints – straits, canals, rail junctions, transshipment ports – have historically given whoever controls them disproportionate leverage over the flows of trade and information passing through, precisely because alternatives are scarce and switching costs are high. With roughly 90% of global search volume, Google occupies an analogous position in the informational economy. What is unfolding now is what happens when the operator of that chokepoint begins converting its role from an allocative intermediary into a value-extracting one.

Regulators across multiple jurisdictions have reached a similar conclusion, and the resulting convergence is striking. The European Commission opened a formal antitrust investigation in December 2025 into whether Google's use of publisher content in AI Overviews and AI Mode breaches EU competition law. France's press bodies lodged a complaint this August, layering onto roughly $1.1 billion in fines Google has already paid French authorities over neighboring-rights disputes since 2021. Meanwhile, Brazil's competition authority and Japan's Fair Trade Commission have opened parallel inquiries.

Türkiye is not a bystander to this story. The Turkish Competition Authority (RK) fined Google roughly $19.7 million euros in 2018 over its Android bundling practices and concluded a separate general-search abuse case in mid-2024. More pivotally, in April 2026, it launched a dedicated sector inquiry into the AI ecosystem, examining how data, computing power, and platform dominance are reshaping competitive dynamics.

The inquiry explicitly flagged that major AI providers often operate in Türkiye without any local legal footprint. Ankara already has both the institutional experience and the appetite to scrutinize exactly the kind of gatekeeping power now under the microscope in Brussels, Paris and London. Given how exposed local-language and midsized publishers are proving to be elsewhere – and the dramatic 60%+ drops in CTRs across the board – that inquiry must urgently extend to how AI Overviews and AI Mode are suffocating Turkish digital media.

Google disputes the scale of the damage, pointing to mitigations like "Further Exploration" links and the new Preferred Sources button. But a company whose stated ambition is to keep users transacting with its own AI cannot simultaneously guarantee the volume of outbound traffic that sustains the ecosystem its models depend on for training data.

The question for Ankara is whether it will proactively help write those rules to protect its local publishers or simply live with whichever digital realities emerge first from Washington or Brussels.
