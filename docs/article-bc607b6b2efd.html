On Greece-Israel relations: From Achilles to Ayil

2026-09-07 21:05

Greece’s defense initiative, designated the “Achilles Shield,” was launched amid threatening rhetoric from Defense Minister Nikos Dendias. In Greek mythology, Achilles (known as “the mightiest of all warriors”) is the epic hero of the Trojan War in Homer’s "Iliad." While Achilles slew Hector in a duel, Homer’s narrative does not record Achilles’s death by Paris’s arrow, for a true hero transcends such fates. Thus, the “Achilles Shield” nomenclature carries a rhetorical framework in which anti-Trojan sentiment converges with a Turkish-centric strategic perception.

Yet there is an ironic possibility. Following the Aug. 31 agreement with Israel, Achilles risks being transformed into "Ayil" – the Hebrew term for “the sacrificed, the substitute, lacking control over its fate,” specifically referring to the ram Abraham offered in place of his son. If this trajectory materializes, the symbolism of Greek strategic autonomy would be fundamentally inverted.

With this in mind, let us examine the process and its implications with analytical rigor.

Greece crossed a threshold in its defense posture following the Aug. 31 defense agreement with Israel. The accord transfers to Athens systems of considerable strategic significance: the 300-kilometer (185-mile) range David’s Sling air defense system, the 150-kilometer BARAK MX missile platform, mid-range Spyder surface-to-air missiles, MMR radar systems, and a drone defense architecture. The weapons package, valued at approximately $3 billion, will be delivered over 35 months.

The strategic calculus behind this agreement – and Greece’s broader realignment toward Israel – entails costs that exceed the allocated defense budget. Before examining the technical dimensions, the fundamental premise warrants scrutiny.

Greek military planners likely view David’s Sling as a way to double the engagement range of existing Patriot systems, enabling the interdiction of Turkish aircraft well before they reach the Aegean Sea or Western Thrace. The BARAK MX, deployable from both naval and ground platforms and with a 150-kilometer effective range, presumably aims to supplement or replace the aging Russian-supplied S-300 and Tor-M1 systems currently in Greek service.

The drone defense architecture and Spyder missiles – capable of engaging targets to 70 kilometers – appear specifically calibrated against Turkish unmanned systems. The MMR radar constellation will provide the requisite surveillance infrastructure for David’s Sling and drone defense operations. In essence, the procurement reflects a stated intention to counter Turkish unmanned aircraft threats.

Yet quantitatively, the defensive sufficiency of these systems against a sustained or coordinated threat remains uncertain. History instructs: Iranian missiles successfully penetrated Israeli airspace despite existing Israeli defenses that Greece is already procuring. The question emerges whether this procurement solves Greece’s security dilemma or merely displaces it.

Does this acquisition constitute a genuine strategic remedy for Greece, or has Greek taxpayer revenue merely been redirected to Israel? These questions carry sufficient political weight to alter governance in Greece, for military architecture must align with identified threats and operational realities. As defensive expenditures increasingly diverge from cost-effective solutions, the psychological comfort of enhanced security often masks deeper structural vulnerabilities.

Defense commitments are fundamentally instruments of dependency and loyalty. Thus, it must be stated plainly: This weapons procurement places Greece within Israel’s strategic orbit. The acquisition transcends technical military necessity and assumes distinctly political dimensions.

A “Türkiye complex” permeates Greek strategic thinking. In 1919, Greek expansionist ambitions reached into Asia Minor, emboldened by British reassurance to advance as far as the Sakarya River. Decades later, in the 1960s, Greek governments – buoyed by perceived American sympathy – pursued the annexation of Cyprus and targeted Turkish Cypriots. With each instance of Western support, Greek policy grew increasingly assertive. In January 1996, believing itself backed by international consensus, Athens attempted to plant a flag on Kardak – rocky islets whose sovereignty remains disputed. Most recently, following the discovery of hydrocarbon reserves in the Eastern Mediterranean, Greece – encouraged by Israeli, French, EU, and American backing – engaged in harassment operations against Turkish energy vessels in 2019. Each initiative, however, drew a corresponding response.

Against this historical backdrop, Greek strategic psychology must be read with precision. A Greek intellectual tradition, shaped by grandiose historical memories yet disconnected from contemporary strategic realities, has consistently externalized its security calculations and expansionist aspirations by relying on external powers. When convinced of external backing, Greek policymaking accelerates toward assertion and action. Conversely, the major external powers supporting Greece have harbored no intrinsic interest in Greek security per se: the U.S. seeks basing privileges; France pursues defense contracts of substantial value; Germany seeks to collect the massive debts it has extended to Athens.

Israel’s weapons sales to Greece must therefore be understood not as responses to Greek security needs, but as instruments of Israeli strategic agenda.

An examination of the Israel-Greece-Greek Cypriot alignment and the logic behind Israel’s decision to arm both Athens and the Greek Cypriot community requires attention to Israel’s evolving regional position.

As Türkiye consolidates its status as a consequential regional power diplomatically, militarily, and economically, Israel’s traditional strategic doctrine is eroding. Confined to a narrow strip of contested territory, Israel has no inclination toward further displacement. Having navigated multiple conflicts against arrayed Arab coalitions since 1948, Israel has consistently preferred regional environments in which no single contiguous power can mount a coordinated challenge. Israel has therefore pursued the destabilization of Syria, the containment of Iran, and the engineering of manageable client relationships with Egypt and Jordan.

Viewing Türkiye as a categorical threat, a position articulated with increasing clarity by Israeli strategic planners, Tel Aviv has begun constructing an alternative approach. The opening gambit of this “Türkiye strategy” emerged on July 15, 2016, when coup plotters aligned with the Gülenist Terror Group (FETÖ) nearly succeeded in overthrowing the Turkish government. When that initiative failed, the project was shelved, awaiting a more opportune moment. Yet as Iranian influence in Syria diminished and Russian regional leverage atrophied, Israel’s threat assessment regarding Türkiye intensified.

The formation of the Eastern Mediterranean Energy Bloc, which brings together Israel, Greece and the Greek Cypriot administration, and the ensuing Israel-Greece-Greek Cypriot trilateral alignment reflect this revised Israeli “Türkiye strategy.” Tel Aviv is effectively recreating, in miniature, the containment architecture once directed at the Soviet Union, now recalibrated for Turkish containment. Should Israel face direct conflict with Türkiye in any “worst-case scenario” planning, Israel seeks to prevent the region from Syria to Western Thrace from becoming an extended theater of operations.

To achieve this objective, Israel has begun supplying advanced weapons systems to Greece. The benefits accruing to Israel from this arrangement are considerable and merit frank acknowledgment.

First, Israel effectively outsources the cost of technological competition with Türkiye to Athens. The Israeli systems positioned across Western Thrace will force Türkiye into bipolar force deployments and complicate Turkish strategic prioritization.

Second, and more significantly, Israeli software and hardware control mechanisms embedded in these systems introduce a vulnerability often overlooked. Greece may find itself ensnared in circumstances it neither chose nor controls, as Israeli technicians retain the capacity to manipulate these systems’ operational parameters. In the narrative of Abraham’s sacrifice – the original meaning of Ayil in Hebrew – the sacrificial animal has no agency and does not comprehend the purposes to which it is bound.

Objective analysis reveals an uncomfortable truth: Türkiye does not pose a genuine threat to Greece’s security interests. Türkiye faces challenges of such magnitude and complexity that Greece is not a meaningful priority in Turkish strategic calculus. Yet Greece, having assumed the role of Israel’s sacrificial instrument through this agreement and Israel’s positioning on Cyprus’ southern shores, faces the distinct possibility of being drawn into conflicts it would not otherwise choose.

Despite both nations' NATO membership, a reduction in regional tension might yet be achieved through a binding non-aggression compact. Should such an agreement constitute a viable diplomatic pathway, Türkiye has already signaled its openness to such discussions. Absent this trajectory, the security dilemma will prove irresolvable, destined to perpetuate itself through successive cycles of armament, suspicion, and miscalculation.
