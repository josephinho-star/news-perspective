America's women's basketball epic reaches its climax with national unity on the line in FIBA World Cup final

2026-09-13 11:53

It's the final countdown.

Caitlin Clark, Angel Reese and the U.S. women's national basketball team will defend its dynasty in Berlin on Sunday in a World Cup championship against France.

But this weekend, the pursuit of gold carries a distinctly heavier, and distinctly American, weight.

Taking the court on the 25th anniversary weekend of the September 11 terrorist attacks, head coach Kara Lawson isn't just looking to protect a historic 35-game World Cup winning streak.

"And as far as unity, I think unity is not just exclusive to the women’s basketball community," Lawson said. "I think it’s unity for our country as well. I think our team hopefully is creating unity across our country. People being proud about how we play and how we represent the red, white and blue."

The French stand in the way of that hope.

France enters Sunday's championship undefeated. They just dismantled host-nation Germany 86-64 in the semifinals and jumped the U.S. for the top spot in FIBA’s power rankings heading into the final stage.

The French are also out for revenge.

Sunday marks a direct rematch of the unforgettable 2024 Olympic gold-medal thriller—a game the Americans narrowly escaped 67-66 only because French star Gabby Williams' toe grazed the 3-point line as time expired.

The 'young and turnt' crew

American women's basketball fans have been captivated by the surreal imagery of watching Clark and Reese play together as teammates in this World Cup.

In the team’s dominant 108-56 quarterfinal blowout against Hungary, the pair checked in together and completely overwhelmed the Hungarian defense, combining for 19 points and highlighting their growing on-court connection.

They've come together after three years as the sport's biggest rivals, with legions of fans that are often at each other's throats on social media. But now, the duo and their fans are on the same side, supporting America.

"We’ve played against each other so many times, so I’m sure we know each other’s game like the back of each other’s hand," Reese told TNT Sports on Thursday after a win over Hungary. "She dishes it off to me, I got one to her today, so, it’s fun. We just continue to build chemistry because we know we’re going to be USA teammates for a while."

Clark told The Associated Press before the quarterfinals, "We're both fiery, fiery competitors and we want to win and it's one of the things I really admire about [Reese's] game. She's competitive and she has a tremendous motor, like she has one of the best motors in the league. She works just as hard as anybody on the court."

Their emotional investment has been on display in a pair of clips captured by X user Michal Caro.

During their blowout win over Hungary, Reese and Clark were seen chatting and laughing while sitting on the bench.

Then, during a tense dogfight against Spain in the semifinal, the duo was seen sharing frustration and concerned expressions on the bench.

Clark and Reese highlight the group nicknamed the "young and turnt" crew, which has become a fan favorite term for Americans this World Cup. The nickname originated from fellow young teammate Paige Bueckers, who coined the term during a USA Basketball training camp in December 2025.

But their youth also earned them a spot outside of the team's starting lineup, as Reese, Clark and Bueckers, have all come off the bench in the U.S.'s five games so far. Lawson has remained committed to starting the veterans for the U.S.'s World Cup run, sparking debate among fans among social media.

However, Lawson has also suggested she would be open to adjusting the lineup.

"I think that our starting group's done a pretty good job of getting us off to good starts, and rotationally from game to game we could change things, but I like where we are right now," Lawson said Wednesday before the quarterfinal.

The French's hopeful revolution

France's key players view Team USA as summer teammates, workout partners and daily WNBA rivals.

Marine Johannès, a New York Liberty player, has spent years sharing a locker room and playing alongside Team USA veteran leader and Liberty teammate Breanna Stewart.

Johannès was ready and expecting to take on the U.S. in the championship before the Americans had even punched their ticket to the final.

"If I'm honest, we have nothing to lose. We actually know they're a great team. They won every World Cup for years and years. They are really talented. We all know that. We are playing with these players all season in the W, we know how good they are," Johannès told reporters after her team defeated Germany in the semifinal, hours before the U.S. took the court against Spain. "It's one game. Nothing to lose. We are going to play hard, and we'll see what happens."

France's star, Williams enters Sunday's final playing some of the most dominant basketball of her career. In the semifinals, Williams helped orchestrate France’s commanding 86-64 blowout of host-nation Germany, completely silencing a raucous, high-energy home crowd in Berlin.

"I've been through quite a few moments like that. I know what that kind of pressure is like," Williams said. "I know what's needed in those moments."

Born in Nevada, Williams came up through America's premier women's basketball institution, UConn, where she won two national championships and shared the floor for years with current U.S. star Napheesa Collier.

Her time in the WNBA has given her hands-on experience defending and scoring against virtually every player on the American roster. For Williams, facing Team USA isn't a showdown against an unfamiliar juggernaut, but an opportunity to settle a score with players she has grown up alongside after Olympic heartbreak in 2024.

A nation and sport on edge

For all the tactical matchups on the court, Sunday's championship carries a distinctly heavier cultural weight in a highly polarized calendar year for women’s basketball—and for the United States in a heated midterm year.

The WNBA season that preceded this World Cup break was defined by tribalism. Phoenix Mercury star Alyssa Thomas punched Clark in the throat, prompting a suspension and overwhelming backlash from Clark's large passionate fan base. Toronto Tempo coach Sandy Brondello was suspended after calling Reese a "protected species" igniting even more race-related discourse on social media.

Then came the Sophie Cunningham rallies and a national phenomenon over the incursion of the "Save Women's Sports" movement into the WNBA.

Tensions escalated when Chicago Sky player DiJonai Carrington was ejected for hitting Cunningham and subsequently sparked a racial controversy by posting about "white privilege" on social media.

The friction spilled directly into the stands when former NBA player Enes Kanter Freedom, who was attending a game to publicly support Cunningham, was ejected from the arena following a heated courtside confrontation with Chicago guard Natasha Cloud.

And then it was announced that WNBA commissioner Cathy Engelbert would be retiring at the end of the year, signaling a need more change in the WNBA moving forward.

But now as the tournament has progressed, Americans who may have found themselves on opposite sides of the Clark-Reese debate are now jointly lobbying for the two stars to start together, and the intrigue has led to record-breaking TV ratings for the FIBA World Cup.

According to TNT Sports U.S., the USA-Italy game averaged 1.3 million viewers and peaked at 1.7 million. The USA-Czechia game averaged 1.2 million viewers, peaking at 1.4 million, while the USA-China opener averaged 577,000 and peaked at 694,000.

And at the heart of Team USA's mission is unapologetic patriotism.

Stewart, who has been representing the country internationally since she was 14 years old, emphasized that setting aside rivalries to put on the American jersey remains the ultimate honor.

"It feels great. I think that any time I can represent Team USA I want to," Stewart said ahead of the knockout rounds. "I've had the incredible opportunity of being able to do it for a long time and since I was really young, and every time I get the call I want to show up."

OutKick can confirm that the entire Team USA roster has stood proudly for the national anthem ahead of the quarterfinal and semifinal games.

The timing of the final, taking place the same weekend as the 25th anniversary of 9/11, was felt by Lawson and her players.

Lawson noted that she heavily felt the somber weight of the moment all the way in Berlin, and hopes a gold-medal finish can offer a moment of healing for Americans back home.

"I think, you know, being a part of USA Basketball for so long, there’s a great pride in it," Lawson said. "I mean, from when I was 17 years old until now, being a part of it in different capacities, you know, I think—I don’t know if there’s anyone prouder to represent the U.S. than I am in this capacity.

"And so we think about that. And I felt the presence of the U.S. here, even though we are in Europe. I felt it, and certainly felt it yesterday in 9/11 and, you know, a somber anniversary."

When the ball tips off in Berlin against France, Team USA will be fighting to protect a 35-game World Cup winning streak and a multi-decade basketball empire. But more importantly, they will be playing for a divided nation tuning in back home, hoping to offer an undisputed moment of American greatness.
