Pssst, what are you injecting? It’s the hot new question in Beverly Hills

2026-09-23 23:47

She looked at me like I’d just admitted to washing my face with hand soap. Apparently, my wellness routine was missing at least one syringe. What struck me was the assumption: She hadn’t asked whether I was using injectables; she wanted to know which ones.

Among women I speak with, beauty secrets are becoming less secret. The coveted recommendation now comes with a protocol. It is no longer enough to admire someone’s complexion. Apparently, we need the ingredient list.

Somewhere between dinner plans and a video of someone’s dog, there must be a shirtless man explaining cellular repair. I miss when unsolicited advice was about bangs.

Enter peptides. They are short chains of amino acids, but the promises attached to them are considerably longer: younger-looking skin, less fat, more muscle, faster recovery. Their names sound like Wi-Fi passwords, and their appeal is understandable.

Men are in on it, too. UCLA researchers report recreational gymgoers turning to untested peptides for performance and injury recovery. Call it beauty, and someone might accuse you of vanity. Call it “optimization,” and suddenly your abs have a research department. “What are you injecting?” has become “Why aren’t you?”

I understand the appeal: I want to look younger and have the energy to enjoy it. I’ve used moisturizer, serums and sunscreen since I was 10. My bathroom shelf isn’t a monument to doing nothing.

But I’m struck by how neatly wellness gives our insecurities a respectable vocabulary. Wanting a better body becomes investing in longevity. Wanting to look less tired becomes taking charge of your health. Sometimes we are simply dressing up the same old fear of falling behind.

But why compare injections at all?

My suspicion is that the question asks for a shortcut, checks whether someone else has an advantage and announces that you know enough to ask. In Beverly Hills, where looking effortlessly maintained can seem like a full-time occupation, the right protocol becomes another form of social currency.

A suspiciously rested face lets you imply that life is treating you exceptionally well.

The pressure deepens when the recommendation comes from a friend. An advertisement has something to sell. Your friend appears to have something to share. Trading protocols can make an uncertain medical decision feel like a sensible group purchase. If enough apparently healthy people are doing something, it starts to feel vetted. But the longterm effects of many experimental injections remain poorly understood.

A friend’s glowing complexion is not a clinical trial. And “peptide” is not a safety rating. Some peptide medicines are approved for conditions including diabetes and obesity. Their success does not validate every injection marketed for rejuvenation or gym gains.

A recent UCLA review of six compounds marketed as peptide supplements found that most research involved animals. Human studies were scarce and generally weak, leaving the claimed benefits for musculoskeletal recovery and performance unproven.

The FDA has flagged potential immune reactions and impurities in several compounded peptides. For certain growth-hormone-releasing peptides, it identifies concerns including reduced insulin sensitivity and higher blood sugar. That is considerably more consequential than an expensive serum failing to deliver a glow.

Other concerns include contaminated vials, inadequate sterility and allergic reactions. For many experimental compounds, limited human research leaves longterm safety and appropriate dosing unresolved.

Combining products adds questions. Calling the mixture a “stack” makes it sound organized, but does not establish that the combination has been adequately studied.

So, is it worth it? For someone treating a diagnosed condition with a proven medicine, the benefits may justify the risks. For an otherwise healthy person buying an inadequately studied injection to keep pace with someone else’s waistline or biceps, I find the bargain much harder to defend.

Before joining in, I would want to know what human research supports the exact product and purpose, where it came from and what a qualified physician thinks could go wrong. A convincing answer should survive outside an Instagram reel.

I question a culture that makes doing nothing to a healthy body feel like neglect. We should be able to admire someone without feeling the need to adopt their medical routine.

I’ll do almost anything to stay looking young. I’d just prefer the research to mature before I sign up.

And the next time someone asks what I’m injecting, I’d like “nothing” to count as a complete answer.

Adelle Nazarian is a journalist, entrepreneur and commentator covering politics, culture and technology.
