The ICJ’s Myanmar Genocide Ruling: What Comes Next?

2026-11-20 05:15

Yesterday, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) in The Hague ruled that Myanmar is responsible for having committed a genocide against the Rohingya Muslim minority in 2017.

The case was a landmark – the first time that a state was held responsible for genocide, and only the fourth time since the Holocaust that an international court ruled that the legal threshold to prove genocide had been met.

Seven years after the case was brought by The Gambia, the ICJ’s judges ruled, by a margin of nine votes to six, that thousands of killings and widespread sexual violence by Myanmar’s security forces against the Rohingya population in 2017 amounted to state-sponsored genocide.

“The court finds that Myanmar’s security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, perpetrated widespread acts of sexual violence as well as acts of extreme violence against children and attacked fleeing Rohingya,” presiding Judge Yuji Iwasawa said in a reading of the decision.

The verdict has been widely praised by survivors and legal advocates alike. Arsalan Suleman, the lead counsel for The Gambia, described the guilty verdict as “a landmark victory for the Rohingya people and for countless individuals in Myanmar who have endured violence at the hands of the military.” He added, “It represents an important step toward accountability and honors the courage and perseverance of the victims and survivors who have spent years seeking justice.”

As of press time, Myanmar’s military-backed government, which took office in April, has not yet made any public comment about the verdict. Nor has it received much mention in leading regime media outlets. The front page of the Global New Light of Myanmar was instead taken up with reports of a meeting between Min Aung Hlaing and the head of the BIMSTEC economic bloc, Min Aung Hlaing’s donation of alms for the Buddhist Kathin festival, and “news” of engagements involving diplomats from Sri Lanka and Belarus.

Nonetheless, regime propaganda accounts have offered an indication of how Naypyidaw intends to respond to the landmark verdict. In the run-up to the ruling, many pro-military Telegram channels changed their profile pictures to an image of a lighted torch imprinted with the colors of the Myanmar flag, emblazoned with the words “A Light For Justice.”

In addition to attempting to spin the verdict as a qualified victory for Myanmar, one prominent pro-military account on X responded to the guilty verdict by posting AI-generated images highlighting great powers’ lack of compliance with past ICJ rulings, one of which declared that “cooperation with the ICJ is no longer needed.” Another post stated, “Even though ICJ rulings are legally binding, the court has no police or military to enforce them, so major powers and politically sensitive cases sometimes ignore them.”

All of this suggests that Myanmar’s new government intends to ignore the verdict, as well as the ICJ’s determination that the Rohingya are a legitimate protected ethnic group. (Both the post-coup junta and the civilian National League of Democracy administration have viewed them as Bengali immigrants from Bangladesh.)

While Myanmar’s position is unsurprising, it raises questions about whether yesterday’s ruling, for all its symbolic importance to survivors, will have much material effect. In its ruling, the ICJ judges declared that Myanmar now has an obligation to compensate the Rohingya for the violations it had committed, to be determined at a later date. They said that Myanmar must also allow the “safe and dignified return to their place of residence and the reunification of families for the Rohingya displaced as a result of Myanmar’s wrongful acts.” (Around 1 million Rohingya are currently living in refugee camps in southeastern Bangladesh.)

Myanmar has also been ordered to facilitate the search for the disappeared and ensure their proper burial; provide for the physical and psychological rehabilitation of victims; and ensure their protection from further persecution. The court has given Myanmar two years to comply with the judgement.

Many of the responses to the verdict have stressed its compulsory nature. In a statement yesterday, the European Union said that Myanmar must fully implement the ICJ’s ruling “without delay” and that “compliance with ICJ’s decisions is not optional; it is an obligation under international law.” Canada said much the same thing, as did U.N. Secretary-General Antonio Guterres. In effect, a state that has been found guilty of committing genocide against a particular ethnic group is now being ordered to protect and offer reparations to that same group.

This is the point where the case grinds onto the reefs of political reality. Pro-military propagandists are not wrong to point out that the ICJ has no direct means of compelling the country’s acquiescence. Under Article 94 of the U.N. Charter, The Gambia has the right to bring the case to the United Nations Security Council (UNSC) and request further action. The UNSC can in turn “make recommendations or decide upon measures to be taken to give effect to the judgment.”

However, as one analyst noted, the Council has never previously exercised this power, and Myanmar can probably count on the support of China and Russia, which have routinely vetoed resolutions critical of its actions. While neither Beijing nor Moscow is particularly keen to be seen to condone genocide, it is unclear whether these concerns override the strategic considerations that led them to engage with post-coup Myanmar in the first place. In China’s case, geographic proximity makes disengagement, let alone sanctions, very hard to envision.

At the same time, the United States, whose cooperation is probably necessary for any attempt to hold Myanmar meaningfully to account, is both ill-disposed toward international justice in general and the ICJ in particular. Indeed, the Trump administration appears less concerned about the Rohingya case than about the possible precedent it could set for other ICJ cases, including the genocide case brought by South Africa against Israel in 2024.

Mike Waltz, the U.S. ambassador to the United Nations, recently warned the ICJ not to use its Myanmar case “to lower the legal threshold for proving genocide so that weakened and false standard can later be weaponized against American and allied soldiers whenever they raise a finger against terrorists that hide behind civilians.” Meanwhile, after his trilateral summit with Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping this week, Trump described the International Criminal Court (ICC), another international court based in The Hague, as a “rogue globalist organization” that has exceeded its authority. The three leaders also collectively warned the ICC against abusing its jurisdiction.

The U.S. State Department said yesterday that it was examining the ICJ’s judgment but also stressed the need to avoid the “politicization” of the court. These are not the words of a government that is committed to pursuing accountability in Myanmar.

Yesterday’s genocide conviction could potentially impact Myanmar’s attempts to normalize its relations with other partners, including the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN). Since taking office as president in April, Min Aung Hlaing has made normalization with the 11-nation bloc a priority, and five ASEAN member states – Laos, Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam, and Malaysia – have been supportive enough to welcome him on official state visits during that time. The bloc as a whole has also just adopted the Thai government’s policy of “calibrated engagement” with Myanmar.

Whether the momentum toward normalization slows or reverses in the wake of the genocide conviction also remains unclear. One observer argued today that any ASEAN country trying to normalize its ties with Myanmar “will face significant pressure from the European Union to disengage.” But this in turn depends on the EU (or any other Western nation) committing political will to holding Myanmar to account.

In recent years, Brussels has moved to advance its relationships with ASEAN member states, particularly in terms of trade and investment, as a hedge against the uncertain trajectory of U.S. foreign and trade policy. Would Brussels threaten meaningful sanctions on, say, Vietnam or Malaysia, two important trade partners, if they should grow closer to Myanmar? Again, it is hard to say so with confidence.

As Richard Horsey of the International Crisis Group has argued, with the ICJ lacking the ability to impose the ruling, “what matters now is whether the governments engaging Naypyidaw are prepared to attach any cost to ignoring it.”

It is clear from the reaction to yesterday’s ICJ verdict that a genocide conviction carries considerable moral weight, rooted in the postwar catchcry of “never again.” At the same time, it is undeniable that the concept of genocide has become, at least in its casual and rhetorical usage, politically contested, diluted by overuse, and entwined with the machinations of powerful international actors. In the current world, it can no longer be assumed to carry a global consensus, nor to act as a spur to global action.
