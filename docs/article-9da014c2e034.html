LIRR riders sharing phone location get $8 ticket warnings — despite claims of no tracking

2026-11-23 11:00

Long Island Rail Road riders sharing their location are getting ticket activation warnings — despite officials’ claims that they wouldn’t monitor geodata to police commuters.

The LIRR hiked prices in January and started issuing a new $8 fine if riders didn’t activate their tickets on the TrainTime app prior to boarding, with the transit agency saying it would only use a “timekeeping function” to check if the rules were being broken.

But The Post found phones sharing their locations received warnings while phones not sharing didn’t receive any warnings — which cybersecurity experts said shows the app may be “abusing user data.”

“The tests [The Post ran] are pretty black and white — they’re clearly using some sort of location data when they say they’re not,” said Harry Maugans, a cybersecurity expert and privacy advocate.

“People should care about this, because if they don’t, one day we’re going to wake up in an Orwellian state,” said Maugans, who is CEO of data privacy firm Privacy Bee.

The Post used two phones with different location settings over multiple trips spanning various dates, with both phones having unactivated tickets at the start of a trip.

One phone, with its location set to “always” sharing, was consistently hit with pop-up late-activation warnings early in the ride that tell riders they’ll be surcharged for repeatedly violating the policy.

The other phone, with its location set to “never” sharing, was able to activate tickets without penalties or warnings — even after two or three stops into a trip after boarding.

The LIRR, which operates as one of the Metropolitan Transportation Authority’s commuter railroads, didn’t offer an explanation for the discrepancy.

The MTA only pointed to the TrainTime app’s terms of services when confronted with The Post’s findings.

The terms of service and conditions claim that location data is stored entirely separate from each customer’s TrainTime account.

The agency also didn’t respond to questions about what the stored data is used for.

LIRR tickets aren’t associated with a specific train, which experts said showed the app shouldn’t “know” if a user is on board a moving train except if the phone’s location is being used as a giveaway.

The TrainTime app’s vendor, Masabi, a London-based company whose ticketing software runs inside the app, said in its own privacy policy that it handles that rider data only as a processor, leaving the MTA in charge of how it’s used.

The Post has filed Freedom of Information Law requests regarding the TrainTime app and location data and is awaiting a response from the MTA.
