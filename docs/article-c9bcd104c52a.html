I watched Trump, Xi and Putin gather in China. Here are my top takeaways

2026-11-18 19:47

SHENZHEN, China — It took a trip to China for me, an American, to see U.S. President Donald Trump in person for the first time.

Trump emerged from a group of black-clad photographers and other personnel surrounding him at a welcome banquet in Shenzhen for the Asia-Pacific Economic Leaders' Meeting held Nov. 17 to 19.

Seated just a few spots to his left was Russian President Vladimir Putin who picked up a glass of red wine to join a toast led by Chinese President Xi Jinping.

"At tomorrow's Leaders' Meeting, we will discuss measures to expand mutual opening up, explore pathways to innovation-driven growth, and identify opportunities to expand practical cooperation across various fields, so as to translate more initiatives into tangible results," Xi said in Mandarin, according to a CNBC translation.

Xi was speaking broadly, but Thursday's agenda includes a bilateral meeting with Trump, and their highly anticipated trilateral meeting with Putin.

But looking at the mood in the room, it seemed that their trilateral conversation had already started Wednesday night at their long banquet table. Much of what happens at APEC, including bilateral meetings and other exchanges between leaders, takes place away from the media.

But what struck me was how little sign there was of the tension surrounding Nvidia's CEO Jensen Huang resignation, a controversy that has gripped markets for the past 10 days.

Ahead of Trump's entrance into the banquet hall, Chinese Commerce Minister Wang Wentao shared a friendly moment with U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and other members of the American delegation.

Last week, the U.S. officials said that Trump did not plan to raise allegations of Huang's affair with an alleged Chinese spy during his meeting with Xi.

It was a striking contrast to just two months ago, when I saw photographs of Huang and Tesla CEO Elon Musk sharing a table with Trump and Xi in Washington, D.C.

And when Trump visited Beijing in May, Huang took a few minutes to speak with reporters, including me, inside the Great Hall of the People. Musk was there too, accompanied by one of his sons.

This time, neither were visible in a room that was closely watched by the world. Musk was expected to join Trump's delegation in Shenzhen, but I did not see him Wednesday evening.

Their absence from the government-heavy banquet was striking. The world's most powerful leaders may be grappling with the implications of artificial intelligence, but they certainly didn't need the industry's biggest names at the table to do so.

Over the months I've spent following APEC's "China Year," one thing has become clear: The forum has given Beijing an opportunity to host the U.S., Japan and other member economies at a time when unresolved tensions might otherwise have made such gatherings difficult.

But even in a year defined by talks of U.S.-China "strategic stability," the APEC events have exposed the rifts and the shifting dynamics in global power. Those dynamics were on display for the opening of the APEC CEO Summit.

Several hundred attendees, from Xiaomi CEO Lei Jun to U.S. Ambassador to China David Perdue, arrived early for the opening of the APEC CEO Summit.

After waiting in silence for at least 10 minutes, the audience welcomed Xi in a distinctly modern way: by raising their smartphones to capture the moment. The applause was rather muted.

It was much the same at the more government-heavy welcome banquet that evening. So many attendees rushed to take their own photographs of Xi and other leaders entering the room that reporters' views were largely blocked.

Following Xi's morning speech, there was a lengthy intermission before Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim took the stage.

"The entire multilateral framework needs to change," he said, praising China on its "rare" recognition of smaller countries.

During the tea break that followed, attendees quickly resumed mingling. Some even brought coffee and snacks back into the room as Putin prepared to speak.

They listened as the Russian president talked about his country's economic resilience and claimed Europe was losing ground. As attendees headed to lunch, one thing stood out: Neither Trump nor any other G7 leader had taken the stage at the opening event. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney spoke later in the afternoon.

The most visible American presence during the morning session came from business leaders appearing in a promotional video ahead of Xi's speech.

And the absence of an American voice was perhaps even more noticeable at Wednesday evening's banquet.

Trump did not follow Xi with a toast of his own. This was the same famously outspoken president, who in a winding speech in Davos, had declared that the U.S. must get Greenland.

Yet on Wednesday night in China, Trump wasn't the one holding the microphone.
