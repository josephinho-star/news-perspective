How far will Pakistan go to defend Saudi Arabia?

2026-09-24 21:05

As the Houthis in Yemen escalate their attacks on Saudi Arabia, Pakistan is facing an increasingly important question: How will Islamabad respond? Saudi Arabia and Pakistan have maintained close relations for decades, with their partnership spanning defense, diplomacy, trade and energy, while millions of Pakistanis work and live in the Kingdom.

The timing could not be more important. On Sept. 19, Saudi Arabia said the Houthis had attempted to strike Riyadh with a ballistic missile, while further attacks were reported against other parts of the Kingdom. Saudi authorities said the missile targeting the capital was intercepted, with no reported casualties or damage.

For Islamabad, these developments have placed its relationship with Riyadh under a spotlight.

Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Türkiye signed the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement on Aug. 7. Pakistan's Foreign and Defense Ministry described the agreement as a defensive arrangement aimed at strengthening regional peace and stability, improving defense cooperation, developing military capabilities and collectively deterring actions that could destabilize the region. Islamabad also stressed that the agreement is not directed against any country.

Since then, Pakistan's position has combined a strong commitment to Saudi security with an emphasis on diplomacy and regional stability. On Sept. 10, Islamabad said there had been no discussion of a military response under the agreement regarding the Houthi attacks, but highlighted that it would be ready to defend its ally if the situation persisted.

More recently, high-ranking Pakistani officials, including the country’s defense minister, have again emphasized the country's commitment to Saudi Arabia's security, while the exact operational details of the agreement still have not been made public. This leaves room for different forms of cooperation, from intelligence sharing, technical assistance and air defense support to military deployments if the hour demands it.

A defense partnership like this one does not necessarily mean that every attack must be met with an identical military response. Security cooperation in today’s world can involve many layers, with the response depending on the nature, scale and location of a threat.

For Pakistan, this is particularly important because its relationship with Saudi Arabia extends far beyond defense.

The two countries have maintained close ties for nearly eight decades. Saudi Arabia is one of the most important economic partners for Pakistan. The bilateral trade between the two countries reached approximately $4.75 billion in the 2025 to 2026 fiscal year, according to official figures, with Saudi Arabia becoming Pakistan's largest source of remittances.

Pakistan received a record $41.6 billion in remittances overall during that fiscal year, with Saudi Arabia contributing the largest share.

The military relationship is equally well established. Pakistani and Saudi forces have conducted joint military exercises for years, including the Al Battar II exercise in November 2025, which focused on counterterrorism operations, urban combat, explosive threats and operational coordination.

The Mecca agreement therefore builds upon an existing foundation rather than creating cooperation from nothing.

That was evident on Aug. 31, when senior officials from Pakistan, Türkiye and Saudi Arabia met in Istanbul for the first Strategic Political and Defense Committee meeting established under the agreement. The three countries agreed to institutionalize cooperation, strengthen collective deterrence and defense, establish a secretariat in Saudi Arabia and deepen defense industry cooperation, including joint technology development and production.

Türkiye's role adds another important dimension. On Sept. 19, Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said Türkiye was ready to help meet potential Saudi military and technical needs under the trilateral arrangement, while describing the attacks on Saudi territory as a serious issue concerning the Kingdom's sovereignty and territorial integrity.

For Pakistan, the most immediate challenge is therefore not simply whether to demonstrate solidarity with Saudi Arabia. Islamabad has already made that commitment very clear. The question that remains is how that commitment can be translated into practical security cooperation while preserving the broader objective repeatedly emphasized by the three partners: regional peace and stability.

Its position is particularly significant because Islamabad maintains relationships across a region where alliances and rivalries continue to overlap on multiple occasions. Its longstanding ties with Saudi Arabia give it influence in Riyadh, while its wider diplomatic engagement gives Islamabad an interest in preventing another conflict from expanding. This creates space for Pakistan to play more than one role.

Pakistan can remain a firm security partner of Saudi Arabia while also supporting diplomatic efforts aimed at containing escalation. The Mecca agreement itself calls for de-escalation and restraint alongside stronger collective defense capabilities. That balance may define Pakistan's role in the present crisis.

How far Pakistan ultimately goes will depend on the nature of the threat and the decisions taken by the three partners collectively. But one point is already clear: for Islamabad, defending Saudi Arabia is not a new question. What is new is the scale of the security environment in which that commitment now has to be translated into action.
