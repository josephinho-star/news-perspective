Voyager 1, SpaceX Starship and BepiColombo mark major space milestones

2026-11-23 11:00

Rachel Feltman: Happy Monday, listeners! For Scientific American’s Science Quickly, I’m Rachel Feltman.

As usual, we’re starting the week off with a roundup of recent news. But because there’s been so much exciting space-related stuff happening in the last few weeks, we decided to spend today focused on space stories.

Here to fill us in is Lee Billings, senior editor for physical science at Scientific American.

Lee, thanks so much for coming on to talk to us about all the exciting space news we’ve been having.

Lee Billings: Rachel, it’s always my pleasure to be here. There’s so much to talk about. We’re barely gonna scratch the surface, but uh, it’s gonna be a lot of fun.

Feltman: Yeah, absolutely. Well, I guess, you know, is there a story that you would like to start with that’s felt particularly big the last couple weeks?

Billings: You know, it’s hard not to go with NASA’s Voyager 1 probe, which is now, uh, on its way out of the solar system into interstellar space, reaching the one light-day milestone. So people talk about light-years a lot, which, you know, “Oh, a light year, how far is that?” Well, really, really, really far. But it turns out even a light-day is very, very far because Voyager 1 was launched almost a half century ago, in September 1977, on a tour of the outer solar system. It swung by Jupiter and Saturn. Here we are, almost a half-century later. It’s been traveling at about 38,000 miles per hour, and just last week, Voyager 1 finally hit that milestone of one light-day, the point at which a signal from Earth to the spacecraft or a signal from the spacecraft to Earth takes a full 24 hours traveling through space to get to its destination. And that, to me, is just wild. It’s a relatively small thing, but it’s still so cool.

Feltman: Yeah. No, it really puts the scale of it all into perspective. You know, I feel like Voyager is such a fun mission, and we don’t talk about it that much. I feel like it comes up when we hit a big milestone for how far out it is. So could you remind our listeners what the point of Voyager is and what it’s doing out there?

Billings: So Voyager 1 was one of two probes. There was a sister probe called Voyager 2 that was actually, somewhat counterintuitively, launched earlier than Voyager 1. But the pair of them went out into the outer solar system on what some people called a grand tour. And Voyager 1 looked at Jupiter and at Saturn, and Voyager 2 looked at those as well and also looked at Uranus and Neptune.

And now they’re both on their way out of the solar system. They, in fact, have already breached the so-called heliopause, this boundary in space where the sun’s influence wanes, and instead they’re essentially in interstellar space. And today it seems kind of basic. Oh, they went and saw these outer planets. Well, back then we didn’t really know much about these planets, and even now some of the observations from Voyager 1 or Voyager 2 are the best we’ve got from some of these locations, like Neptune or Uranus, for instance.

Feltman: Well, and speaking of, you know, exactly how far out they are, it’s kind of a semantic discussion of if and when they’ve reached the solar system, right?

Billings: That’s right. It depends on, kind of, what you define as the end of the solar system. For instance, beyond this heliopause I mentioned, this boundary where the sun’s influence wanes in terms of its solar wind and stuff like that, there’s also something called the Oort Cloud, which is much, much farther out, and that’s what we think is a big spherical shell of comets that are very, very, very far out around the solar system, but they’re still gravitationally bound to the sun. They’re still influenced by the sun’s gravity and held in thrall by it. And so some people would say that that is the true boundary of the solar system. So you’re right, it is a somewhat semantic consideration. But the whole point is that this is the farthest anything that we’ve ever sent out there has gotten in terms of a physical artifact, and it may stay that way for quite some time.

Feltman: Also, just as an aside: [I] love the Oort cloud. In my opinion, it’s one of the most Star Trek names of a thing that we have in our immediate cosmic vicinity. It’s just like, “That cloud full of Oorts,” you know?

Billings: Yeah, and the weirdest thing is, you know, we’ve never actually seen it directly. The only way we know it’s there, basically, is that we can look at the trajectories of these long-period comets and surmise that they must be coming from this reservoir that’s very, very far away, with a boundary that’s perhaps around one light-year away. And unfortunately, it will take Voyager 1 much longer to get past that.

Feltman: Well, great segue to my last question about Voyager, which is just: What can we expect next from this, you know, very long-lived mission? What would you say are some of the milestones or pings back that we’re most excited about?

Billings: So I don’t wanna be a downer. I do think that what’s happened with Voyager 1 and also Voyager 2 is amazing and beautiful, and they’re phenomenal achievements of humanity that will go down in history. At the same time, nothing lasts forever, and Voyager 1, and I think also Voyager 2, are both slated to essentially go dark no later than about 2036, about 10 years from now. And that’s because their radioactive, nuclear power sources that give them heat and electricity way out there, where things like solar power won’t work ’cause they’re so far away, uh, will diminish to essentially nothing due to radioactive decay. They will no longer be able to send signals back. They’ll no longer be able to stay warm, and so they’ll freeze and go dark. Obviously that’s at most 10 years away. I think perhaps it’s optimistic to think that they will reach that point because they’re already hanging on by a thread.

Some people at NASA were very concerned that Voyager 1 wouldn’t even be awake, so to speak, to see this light-day milestone because they have been having a lot of trouble with keeping its instruments on. They have been shutting down instruments and components on these spacecraft to continue to use them on the small trickle of electricity, of power that still comes from these radioactive power sources. And so we do have some, some time still left in their life, so enjoy them while you can.

Feltman: Always good advice. Well, let’s move on to another piece of space news that people may have seen recently, that has to do with reusable rockets and some chopsticks, some big ol’ space chopsticks.

Billings: Oh, goodness, goodness, goodness. Yes, yes, of course. Well, I hope everyone’s heard about this because it is a very big deal. SpaceX’s experimental Starship, a fully reusable megarocket that’s just giant, it’s hard to envision the scale of this thing, went up into orbit, it deployed some Starlink satellites, and then it came back to Earth, and it flew back to its launch site, and it precision flew and slid in kinda like a glove into its launch tower, which then deployed these, yes, these mechanical arms that are called chopsticks and plucked it out of midair. I guess you could say that if they’re chopsticks, then the Starship upper stage that did this is like the world’s biggest egg roll, and it plucked it right out of midair.

And the reason why this is a big deal is because that’s never, ever, ever been done before. This is something out of science fiction. The engineering behind this is staggering. Only SpaceX has managed to do it, and it matters because if they can catch these things routinely and then fly them again very quickly—that’s the whole point of catching them like this—then you are in a new era of spaceflight. You are in a place where, all of a sudden, instead of launching this thing, you know, maybe at best once every few days or once a week or once a month, you might be launching it a dozen times a day. And that is a very wild prospect, and I don’t think anyone’s really ready for what the world literally looks like if that happens. I can’t emphasize enough how much of a change and a shift that would be in what we can put into space, and also, frankly, some of the environmental effects we might have to deal with from such a rapid uptick in launches. So it is a very, very big deal, and people looking back 100 years from now will probably look at that moment, and they’ll forget about the light-day, and they’ll say, “Oh, that was when, uh, SpaceX managed to catch the upper stage of Starship with the chopsticks.”

Feltman: Yeah. Before we move on, one last question on that. Could you tell us briefly what it is about catching the rocket in air that makes it so much more practically, you know, reusable than having it land back on the ground?

Billings: So I think a lot of it has to do with, in some sense, with the tower rather than the rocket. Because people think about these rockets going off into space and shooting into orbit, and they focus on the rocket, and they don’t really pay a lot of attention to the launchpad or the launch tower. But those are critical pieces of infrastructure in this equation as well. And we’ve seen in the past what happens when a rocket as mighty as Starship interacts with its big plume of exhaust, its fiery plume of gas coming out of its rocket engines. What happens when that stuff hits that, sort of, infrastructure? A direct hit can be pretty nasty. And you can reinforce that infrastructure, but it’s probably easier just to minimize the sandblasting effects and the flambéing that happens with a more direct exposure. So you catch it in midair, you ideally preserve more of the readiness of the tower itself and also, frankly, the rocket. The rocket is gonna have to go through a significant, kind of, g-force experience when it shudders to a stop on the ground. And if you can minimize that stuff you notionally can launch much faster and do a faster turnaround.

Feltman: Yeah. Well, okay, there’s one more big space story we’re gonna discuss today. It’s a mission that—I was wondering, why does this sound like the name of a restaurant in Midtown or perhaps an uncle of mine?—and it’s because it’s named after a man named Giuseppe. So tell us about BepiColombo. That’s why that name sounds like that.

Billings: BepiColombo, yes. It honestly is my favorite space mission name to say. Can I just, just give me a minute here? BepiColombo. BepiColombo. BepiColombo. I love saying it. It’s so good.

As you said, it’s named in honor of a man named Giuseppe. Actually, Giuseppe, nickname “Bepi,” Colombo. And he died in 1984. He was an Italian mathematician and engineer, and he is most well known for being the first person to really figure out that you could use what’s called a gravity assist flyby to get to Mercury.

And NASA actually did that with the Mariner 10 spacecraft. The point is, though, is that he’s somewhat of a legend in the study of Mercury, and so this spacecraft is named in honor of him. And it’s extremely fitting because it turns out BepiColombo had kind of a wild ride to Mercury that took a little, you know, took a lot of these gravitational assists and, I think, even more than originally were planned.

Feltman: So tell us when it launched and what the big milestone is that we’re talking about now.

Billings: So it launched, actually, back in October 2018, and it was originally supposed to get to Mercury, if I remember correctly, last year, like last December. But what happened was that, in 2024, some electric thrusters on the spacecraft lost power, and so what needed to happen after that was spacecraft engineers, mission planners needed to find a new trajectory to Mercury and rejigger the mission while it’s in motion, while it’s in space, to find a different way to get to Mercury. And that required a lower-thrust trajectory, and in the end, what we saw was BepiColombo—God, I love saying the name—BepiColombo doing one flyby of Earth, two flybys of Venus and six flybys of Mercury.

And you think to yourself, “Wait, six flybys of Mercury? Come on, it’s going to Mercury. You’re telling me it’s already been to Mercury?” Well it gets complicated because it turns out that we started this conversation talking about Voyager 1, the most distant object that we’ve ever sent out there. In some ways, it’s actually harder to get closer into the sun for a spacecraft, and what is the closest planet to the sun, Rachel?

Feltman: Mercury.

Billings: Mercury. So that is one of the hardest places to get to in the solar system. The reason why is because you actually have to bleed off a lot of speed to get down there. And so the normal way you’d do that is by firing your engines to bleed off that speed, but if your engines are malfunctioning and not giving you as much thrust as you need, you’ve gotta bleed it off in a different way. That’s where all these extra flybys came in, and the reason why it’s bleeding off that speed is because it doesn’t want to just fly by. It wants to go into orbit. We’re trying to orbit Mercury.

Feltman: Right. Not just whiz right past.

Billings: That’s right.

Feltman: So we’re recording this on the 19th [of November], but, by the time this airs, hopefully something very exciting will have happened. Could you tell us more?

Billings: Of course. Yes, yes. So I believe on November 21st, BepiColombo is going to go for orbit insertion. This is the point when it actually will officially be captured into Mercury’s orbit, and it’s an elaborate sequence. There’s like 16 or more different maneuvers. Some folks on the mission have called it six months of continuous activities.

It also has multiple different components to deploy. In some respects, BepiColombo, kind of like the name, you know, there’s the Bepi and there’s the Colombo part, is two spacecraft in one. There is something from the European Space Agency called the Mercury Planetary Orbiter that’s gonna be studying the planet’s surface and its interior.

And then the Japanese space agency [the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency], I like to just call it JAXA for short, has contributed a second spacecraft, a second orbiter called Mio, which is going to be looking at Mercury’s magnetosphere, basically, how the solar wind and space weather interacts with the natural geodynamo, the big, planetary-scale magnetic field that exists around Mercury.

And deploying all those in sequence, making sure that they all separate and go into their separate orbits takes some time. And so, you know, even though a lot of excitement is due with the initial capture into orbit, the real fireworks are hopefully going to start next year. We’re looking actually at, like, I think it’s April, early April of 2027 for routine science to begin for these two spacecraft around Mercury.

Feltman: Very cool. Well, we’ll definitely have you back to talk about some of those questions and hopefully answers as the science comes in. But thank you so much for sharing some space news with us today.

Billings: It’s my pleasure, as always, to talk about space news. And once more with feeling: BepiColombo.

Feltman: BepiColombo. [Laughs].
