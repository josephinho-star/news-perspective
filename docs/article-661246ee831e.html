Australia’s Indigenous population estimated at 2.2m before ‘demographic catastrophe’ of colonisation

2026-09-15 09:00

The number of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in Australia before colonisation was dramatically underestimated, according to new research that suggests many more of them were killed.

Modelling by a national research group puts the population levels before the 1788 first fleet arrived at 2.2 million, compared with early estimates of 250,000 people.

The research, by the Australian Research Council Centre of Excellence for Indigenous and Environmental Histories and Futures (CIEHF), has been published in the peer-reviewed Nature Human Behaviour.

The authors wrote that, historically, population estimates have been poor “due to lethal diseases, frontier and structural violence, and absence of systematic censuses” and that it’s “invaluable to truth-telling and reconciliation” to try to rectify that.

They warn readers that the content of the study is confronting and possibly distressing.

The modelling showed there had to be millions of excess deaths from smallpox and other diseases, massacres, and the other results of colonisation such as dispossession and malnutrition, according to co-author Prof Corey Bradshaw, a global ecology professor at Flinders University.

His previous research found that an 1789 smallpox outbreak alone killed as many as 220,000 Aboriginal people. There were also other smallpox epidemics, as well as outbreaks of measles, influenza, tuberculosis, typhoid and other diseases.

Bradshaw said he would bet his career that massacre databases were underestimated by an “order of magnitude”.

“If you’re a frontier farmer, a colonialist that came out to a new area, and you were trying to remove people from ‘your’ land, you’re not going to write extensively about how many people you killed to do that,” he said.

“There’s another component here that’s essentially greed and racism – the entire concept of terra nullius was based on not many people being here. If there’s a systematic desire to take land [fewer people means a] better justification for imperialism.”

Australia was colonised on the basis of terra nullius, that it belonged to no one. That concept was overthrown with the high court’s Mabo decision, when it was recognised that First Nations people had been here for tens of thousands of years.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people were not properly counted in the census until 1971, after the 1967 referendum. A that point, 115,953 people identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander.

In 2021, 812,728 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people were counted in the census, a figure revised upwards to 983,257 with post-census recounts.

The 2.2 million figure, as well as a new estimate of 2.06 million excess deaths between 1788 and 1861, means the population was reduced to about 7% of its original size.

“We are not talking about a marginal consequence of colonisation but a demographic catastrophe at the heart of the Australian story,” distinguished professor Larissa Behrendt said. A Euahleyai/Gamillaroi woman, she is also the chair of the CIEHF’s advisory committee.

Early on after colonisation, various people estimated how many Aboriginal people were in their region. For example, governor Arthur Phillip estimated Gadigal people of the Eora nation came to about 1,500.

The first continent-wide estimate by social anthropologist Alfred Radcliffe-Brown put the number between 250,000 and 300,000, but mainly used observations from after the impacts of colonisation had been felt.

Radcliffe-Brown was aware that was an underestimate, but many researchers since have relied on that count as a baseline. Again, often recognising that it would be an undercount because of historical reasons and because changes in who identified/was identified as Aboriginal.

In 1983, economic historian Noel Butlin estimated the population at about 900,000. Later he increased that to between 1.1 million and 1.3 million. In 1987, archaeologists John Mulvaney and J. Peter White argued it was more likely 750,000.

There have been many and varied estimates since, usually based on historical and ethnographic information.

The Australian team used different approaches to model the population. They looked at archaeological items to reconstruct how many people would have been there at a particular time, reconstructed the climate and the productivity of the land to infer how many people it could sustain, and looked at genetic data to work back in time based on known DNA mutation rates.

They also reviewed, but did not rely on, ethnographic estimates.

“The findings highlight the scale of demographic catastrophe experienced by Indigenous Australians due to colonisation, with the implications being a heightened understanding of intergenerational trauma and to inform future policy that assists Indigenous people,” said co-author and distinguished professor Lynette Russell of the Monash University Indigenous Studies Centre and CIEHF.

The authors wrote that Australia must prioritise reconciliation, engagement and policymaking with Indigenous people who still suffer intergenerational trauma and that the broader community should embrace the rich culture that survived these events.

The lead author, James Cook University associate professor Alan Williams, said the “devastating findings” highlight the impacts of invasion. But they also demonstrate the “remarkable survival, resilience and recovery over the past century of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Island people”.

Behrendt said the research “tells us something extraordinary”.

“Our survival is one of Australian history’s most remarkable stories,” she said.
