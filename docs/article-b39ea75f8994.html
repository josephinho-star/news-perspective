FBI warns AI hackers can breach systems in under 3 hours

2026-11-23 11:25

Your bank could spend millions protecting its computer systems. Your employer might have an entire cybersecurity team watching for suspicious activity. Yet hackers now have an AI-powered tool that can help them break into some organizations in less than three hours.

The FBI is warning that criminals are using the ARTEX AI hacking tool to automate cyberattacks against businesses around the world. Some attacks have moved from initial targeting to powerful administrator-level access in under three hours. That leaves companies very little time to recognize an attack and stop it.

If hackers reach a business that stores your personal information, you could end up dealing with the consequences. Here's how ARTEX works, why the FBI is concerned and what you can do to protect yourself.

What is the ARTEX AI hacking tool?

ARTEX started as a legitimate penetration testing framework. Cybersecurity professionals use these tools to find weaknesses in computer systems before criminals discover them. However, hackers have found ways to turn ARTEX against organizations without permission. The framework uses artificial intelligence to handle tasks that previously required significant hacking expertise. It can search for security weaknesses and attempt to exploit them. Once inside a system, ARTEX can work toward gaining higher levels of access and extracting sensitive information. What makes ARTEX particularly concerning is its ability to operate with limited human involvement. Attackers can give the software instructions, then let AI agents carry out much of the work automatically.

ARTEX also connects to external AI models, including OpenAI's GPT and Anthropic's Claude. It supports DeepSeek and several other models. These integrations allow the framework to process instructions and automate complicated hacking operations. Their availability does not mean the AI companies are directing the attacks. According to the FBI, the technology allows criminals with relatively little technical knowledge to launch sophisticated attacks against multiple organizations simultaneously.

FBI warns ARTEX hackers can gain access in under 3 hours

In a November 13, 2026, security advisory, the FBI warned that criminals have used ARTEX in cyberattacks since at least August 2026. The agency identified campaigns targeting financial institutions and healthcare organizations. Government agencies and technology companies have also appeared among the targets. The speed of these attacks is especially troubling. According to the FBI, some intrusions progressed from initial targeting to privileged access in less than three hours. That means hackers obtained powerful permissions that could allow them to make changes inside compromised systems.

ARTEX can also identify weaknesses in systems that companies sometimes overlook. These include employee portals and older business applications. Criminals may exploit vulnerable third-party connections to reach organizations with stronger security. Although automated attacks can trigger security alerts, their speed creates another challenge. A company's security team might detect suspicious activity only after criminals have already gained access.

How hackers used ARTEX to attack Microsoft 365

One of the clearest examples involves Microsoft 365, the service millions of employees rely on for work email and company files. In October 2026, hackers exploited compromised application credentials associated with ConnectWise Cloud Backup. The service, formerly known as SkyKick Cloud Backup, helps businesses back up Microsoft 365 information. Here's why that attack is so concerning. The compromised applications already had permission to access customer environments. Hackers took advantage of those existing permissions to authenticate without needing an employee to sign in. In an October 21 investigation, Microsoft attributed the campaign to a China-state-aligned hacking group it tracks as Storm-0940.

The group used ARTEX to speed up its operations and attempt access to more than 2,250 downstream Microsoft 365 customer environments. Cybersecurity company Huntress reported unauthorized sign-ins across at least 162 customer environments during its initial investigation. However, the number of environments targeted does not represent the number where criminals stole information. Microsoft specifically warned that successful authentication does not automatically mean customer data was accessed or taken. The attack also did not result from a vulnerability in Microsoft's own systems or services. Hackers exploited permissions associated with a trusted third-party application. For businesses, that's a serious concern. Employees could have strong passwords and multifactor authentication enabled, yet criminals might still gain access through a compromised application the company already trusts.

ARTEX cyberattacks have already exposed personal information

The Microsoft 365 incident represents only part of the FBI's warning. In South Korea, hackers used ARTEX in attacks against financial institutions. Those attacks exposed personal information belonging to approximately 67,000 people, according to the FBI.

In a separate investigation, South Korean authorities arrested seven suspects connected to attacks against more than 90 organizations. Police said the group stole approximately 8.2 million data records, with ARTEX reportedly used in many of the attacks. Researchers have also identified a separate international cyberespionage campaign.

Cybersecurity company CrowdStrike reported that a China-linked hacking group known as Vapor Panda used ARTEX in attacks targeting more than 200 organizations across 14 countries. The campaign compromised at least nine organizations. The AI agents helped criminals identify vulnerabilities and obtain credentials. When one attack method failed, the software could attempt another approach. These incidents show how quickly AI-assisted hacking has expanded from individual targets to coordinated attacks against organizations around the world.

Why the ARTEX warning should concern you

You probably have never heard of ARTEX before now. But the businesses you depend on could become targets. Think about the information your employer keeps on file. It may include your Social Security number and banking details for direct deposit. Your healthcare provider holds sensitive medical information, while financial institutions maintain records connected to your identity. If criminals steal information from those organizations, they could use it to attempt identity theft or target you with convincing scams. A hacker who obtains your email address and details about your employer could create a phishing message that looks legitimate. A message mentioning a company you actually do business with may be harder to recognize as fraudulent.

There's another concern. Your personal information could be exposed through a company you barely remember giving it to. The FBI has not warned that ARTEX automatically compromises personal phones or home computers. Its advisory focuses on attacks against organizations and the systems they operate. Still, you don't have to be the direct target of a cyberattack to feel its effects.

Why removing ARTEX from GitHub hasn't stopped the attacks

The original ARTEX developer released the framework publicly in July 2026. After reports emerged that criminals were abusing it, the developer removed the original GitHub repository on October 8. However, the software remains available through archived copies and modified versions. The FBI also identified a commercial version called ARTEX PRO. As of early November, researchers had identified more than 1,600 publicly exposed ARTEX consoles. That figure refers to accessible framework interfaces, rather than 1,600 confirmed criminal operations. Removing the original project did little to eliminate copies already circulating online.

How to protect yourself from AI-powered cyberattacks

While companies are responsible for securing their systems, you can still take steps to protect your personal information and limit the damage if a business you trust gets hacked.

Use strong passwords and a password manager

If criminals steal login credentials from one organization, they may test those credentials against other accounts. Reusing passwords makes that easier. Use a strong, unique password for every important account. A reputable password manager can generate secure passwords and store them for you. Start with your primary email account because criminals who gain access to it may be able to reset passwords for other services.

Turn on multifactor authentication or passkeys

Multifactor authentication adds another security check when someone tries to sign in to your account. Enable it for your email and financial services whenever available. Choose a passkey or authenticator app when supported. Passkeys can provide stronger protection against phishing than traditional passwords. Keep in mind that these protections cannot prevent every attack. The ConnectWise incident involved compromised application credentials rather than someone entering an employee's password.

Check which apps have access to your accounts

You may have granted third-party applications permission to access your Google or Microsoft account without thinking much about it. Review those connections periodically. Remove permissions for unfamiliar services or applications you no longer need. For personal Microsoft accounts, visit account.microsoft.com. For Google accounts, review your connected apps through myaccount.google.com. Look for unfamiliar sign-ins while you're there. If something seems suspicious, secure the account and revoke any sessions you don't recognize. For work accounts, contact your IT department before changing business application permissions.

Watch for suspicious breach notifications

Cybercriminals often use major security incidents to create convincing phishing messages. You might receive an email claiming your bank was hacked and asking you to verify your password. Another message could direct you to a fake account recovery page. Avoid clicking unexpected links or opening unfamiliar attachments. Instead, visit the company's official website or open its app directly to check for alerts. Never share verification codes with someone who contacts you unexpectedly, even if the person claims to work for your bank.

Keep your devices updated and use antivirus protection

Install security updates on your computer and phone. These updates frequently address vulnerabilities criminals could exploit. Strong antivirus software with real-time protection can also help identify malicious downloads and suspicious programs. Some security tools offer additional protection against phishing websites. However, antivirus software on your personal computer cannot prevent criminals from compromising a company's cloud systems. Monitor your accounts for unusual activity

Check your financial accounts regularly and enable transaction alerts. These notifications can help you spot unauthorized activity quickly. Review your credit reports for unfamiliar accounts or credit inquiries. You can request free reports through AnnualCreditReport.com. If your Social Security number has been exposed, consider freezing your credit with Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. A credit freeze is free and can make it harder for criminals to open new credit accounts using your identity. A freeze will not prevent fraud involving accounts that are already open, so continue monitoring those accounts.

Know what to do if your information is exposed

If a company notifies you about a breach, find out exactly what information was compromised. Then take action based on the type of data involved.

- Change exposed passwords immediately. Update any other accounts where you reused the same password.

- Secure your email account. Check for unfamiliar forwarding rules or recovery information. Sign out of suspicious sessions.

- Contact your bank if financial information was exposed. Ask whether your accounts need additional protection.

- Consider a credit freeze. This can help protect against fraudulent new credit accounts if sensitive identity information was stolen.

- Watch for follow-up scams. Criminals may use stolen details to make fake security alerts more convincing.

- Report identity theft. Visit IdentityTheft.gov for a recovery plan if someone has misused your personal information.

You can also report suspected internet crime to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov/.

A backup service is supposed to help protect a company's information. In the ConnectWise attack, hackers abused that trusted connection to gain access to customer systems. That's especially concerning because employees could have followed every security recommendation and still been exposed through a service their company depended on. The speed of these AI-powered attacks also worries me. Three hours gives security teams very little time to stop criminals who know exactly where they're going. I think companies need to take a much harder look at the outside applications they trust and how quickly they can detect unauthorized access. For you, the best approach is to secure your own accounts and pay attention when a company reports a breach. You shouldn't have to become a cybersecurity expert to feel confident that businesses are protecting your information.
