Canada’s top court upholds Quebec law barring public sector workers from wearing religious symbols

2026-11-27 16:13

Canada’s top court has largely upheld a controversial secularism law in the province of Quebec barring public sector workers from wearing conspicuous religious symbols, in a closely watched decision that exposes the tensions between minority rights and the power of government to override those rights.

In a 6-1 ruling released on Friday morning, the supreme court found Quebec was permitted to shield the law, Bill 21, from most court challenges on the grounds that it violated charter rights.

“Bill 21 is rooted in Quebec’s legitimate and historical commitment to ensuring the laicity of the state in a free and democratic society,” chief justice Richard Wagner wrote in the majority opinion.

Canada’s top court says the four days of hearings from earlier in the year marked one of the longest in its 151-year history. Fifty-one interveners participated, including six attorneys general – also a record number. The court’s website crashed on Friday morning when the judgment was released.

While the judgment was celebrated by some in Quebec who considered it a vindication of the province’s fight, it was swiftly criticised by religious civil liberties groups.

“We cannot exist in a country as free people, where governments can literally go to the electorate and say, ‘I’m gonna take away the rights of a certain group of minorities’, because it’s popular and to play wedge politics with the charter rights of Canadians,” Stephen Brown, chief executive of the National Council of Canadian Muslims, told reporters at a press conference. “If that is the case, we don’t have a charter of rights and freedoms. We have a charter of suggestions and permissions.”

Passed in 2019, Quebec’s secularism law, Bill 21, bars workers including lawyers, judges, police and teachers from wearing religious symbols such as a hijab, turban, kippah or a cross while on the job.

Central to the case – and the reason provincial governments across the country are closely watching the result – is the “notwithstanding clause”, a constitutional mechanism that allows elected governments to override certain fundamental rights and freedoms guaranteed by Canada’s Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

The clause is the result of political compromise from the 1980s, when the federal government set out to replace the existing British North America Act with a new constitution. Provinces feared that under a new constitution, courts would become too powerful, undermining the authority of the elected government. A compromise was hashed out: in cases when a government – provincial or federal – felt that the courts had overstepped, they could invoke the clause to override certain charter rights of citizens.

Some fundamental rights, including protections of religious freedom, equality and speech, could be suspended. But voting, as well as language rights, were untouchable, ensuring Canadians could remove a government if they wanted. A government could only invoke the clause for five years. Before they could renew it, they would first have to face voters.

In its decision, the supreme court rejected most arguments that Bill 21 violated portions of the charter that are not subject to the notwithstanding clause, including guarantees relating to the equality of men and women and the education rights of Quebec’s English-speaking minority.

Quebec’s use of the clause with Bill 21 was notable – and controversial – in that it was the first time the notwithstanding clause had been invoked pre-emptively in an attempt to shield the law from court challenges. Quebec also passed Bill 96, which sought to strengthen French language protections – again pre-emptively invoking the notwithstanding clause.

But groups affected by those laws can still challenge them and many teachers, religious and civil liberties groups argued it was a clear violation of constitutional rights and reflected government overreach. Quebec’s government has defended the law, saying it is constitutionally valid.

In April 2021, a Quebec superior court judge described the effects of the law as “cruel”, saying it “dehumanises” the people it targets, but conceded the statute could not be struck down because it was protected by the notwithstanding clause. Quebec’s court of appeal also upheld the law in February 2024 on the same grounds.

In its 520-page judgment, the court did find that element of the law infringed on democratic rights protected by the charter, including preventing elected members of Quebec’s National Assembly who wear face coverings for religious reasons from participating in the legislative process.

In a 4-3 decision, the court also endorsed the ability of lower courts to issue “judicial declarations” on laws protected by the notwithstanding clause, giving judges the power to declare a law in violation of the charter, even though the law would be permitted to stand.

The court’s decision was celebrated in Quebec, where the governing Parti Québécois has looked for wedges with the federal government and institutions like the supreme court.

The premier, Paul St-Pierre Plamondon, had previously said his province’s right to decide its own laws was at risk.

Canada only recently fended off a threat to national unity after voters in Alberta rejected the initial stages of a separatist push. But Quebec’s newly elected government has shown an eagerness to clash with the prime minister, Mark Carney.

The supreme court’s judgment comes on the same day an appeal will be heard regarding a superior court decision that forces Quebec’s members of the national assembly to swear allegiance to King Charles to sit in the Salon Bleu – the chamber of the assembly. Lawmakers from a number of parties in the province recently refused to swear the oath of allegiance.
