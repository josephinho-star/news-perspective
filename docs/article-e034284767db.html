COP31 begins in Antalya in year of climate disasters

2026-11-08 08:30

Three stories about climate change are playing out like popular movie genres as COP31 begins in Antalya, Türkiye.

There's the horror story. Many scientists believe global warming is accelerating. Two months ago, the U.N. readjusted its decade-old goal of limiting global temperature rise to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) because the world is blowing past that threshold. The fallout from rising temperatures is felt globally, with Europe's deadly summer heat waves and Nepal's devastating flooding as stark examples.

There's the love triangle. For more than a century, the world has been powered by coal, oil and gas. This love affair spurred economic development, as well as the emissions warming the planet. But renewable energy is capturing some of the world's attention because it is cheaper and cleaner than fossil fuels. Instead of switching from one to the other, the world is using both to meet demand from power-hungry artificial intelligence and ever-growing economic development. This is not helping curb climate change.

Then there's the war movie, where geopolitical conflicts disrupt energy supplies, send fossil fuel prices soaring and force nations to rethink their energy systems.

These stories are colliding as the 31st Conference of the Parties, COP31, starts Monday with speeches from world leaders and will later dig into how to follow through on past climate pledges.

A key issue this year is the implementation of promises to transition away from fossil fuels. As host of COP31, Türkiye is also pushing a separate plan that would help do that by shifting more global energy use – including transportation – to electricity generated from solar and wind power. Negotiators will also hash out how to provide promised funds to poor countries to switch to renewables, adapt to a warmer world and recover from climate-induced damage.

"It’s really the moment that we place a magnifying lens on what’s happening in the world on climate change, both good and bad,” David Waskow, international climate chief at the World Resources Institute, said.

While the U.S. is the world’s largest historic carbon polluter, it skipped last year's COP and is not expected to participate this year.

"There’s always two topics in the climate negotiations that are relevant and difficult,” said Niklas Höhne, co-founder of Germany’s NewClimate Institute. "The first one is who produces emissions (and) how much. That is difficult enough. But what is even more complicated is the other issue, and that is who pays. And on this one, we are particularly in a difficult situation because the U.S., as the largest contributor, has dropped out. And there’s no other player who can jump in.”

Eleven years ago, the world was on track to hit 4 degrees Celsius of warming above preindustrial levels by the end of the century. In 2015, nations set the goal of keeping warming to below 2 degrees Celsius, but with a preferred limit of 1.5 degrees Celsius, as measured over a multi-year period.

Now, the world is at 1.4 degrees Celsius of warming and is hurtling toward 2.6 degrees Celsius, the U.N. found.

October 2026 was the hottest October on record, and 2026 is virtually certain to end up as the second year to exceed the 1.5-degree Celsius threshold, behind 2024, the European climate agency Copernicus announced Friday.

"We have failed so far,” said Johan Rockström, director of Germany’s Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research.

Rockström said Earth will likely experience more extreme weather, such as floods, droughts and storms, and may soon reach "tipping points,” such as ocean currents changing and the Amazon rainforest collapsing.

Even though burning fossil fuels is the main cause of warming, it has rarely been targeted in final documents agreed upon at the conference, mostly because those decisions require consensus.

"The reason we are seeing the breach of 1.5 degrees Celsius of warming is because we avoided the absolutely fundamental question of how climate change happened and what’s responsible, the source of the problem and that’s fossil fuels,” said Harjeet Singh, founding director of Satat Sampada Climate Foundation. "Unless and until we resolve the issue of fossil fuel phase-out, we will not be able to deal with the problem.”

Brazil, which hosted COP30 last year, this month released a detailed voluntary roadmap for how countries could transition away from fossil fuels.

The roadmap is meant to turn the "big problem” of reducing fossil fuels into "smaller actionable solutions,” Ana Toni, who served as CEO of last year's climate talks, said. One key thing nations must do is stop subsidizing fossil fuels, she said. The U.N. estimates fossil fuel subsidies globally hit $1.1 trillion this year.

"What’s standing in the way is not so much technology as governments and incumbent industries,” said Bill Hare, CEO of Climate Analytics. "One cannot underestimate the power and influence of the fossil fuel industry in slowing down action.”

Barbados youth activist Ashley Lashley's first COP was five years ago. She was frustrated by the lack of progress then, but hopeful because there was urgency to keep the 1.5-degree goal alive.

Lashley still attends negotiations with some hope, but she is more frustrated than ever. She said, "The science has become more alarming, but yet politically we seem to be negotiating how much failure we are prepared to tolerate.”

Qiyun Woo, a Singaporean youth environmentalist, said her generation is tired of "seeing their present and futures blanketed in wildfire smoke, in devastating floods (and) landslides."

What gives most experts hope is how cheap solar and wind energy are, along with electric car growth, strengthening the economic argument for transitioning away from fossil fuels.

"It has never been as easy to reduce emissions as it is today because, again, this year, renewable energy has been developing faster than before,” Höhne said.

Much of the work at climate negotiations happens on the sidelines, where not everyone has to agree and some nations do more than others.

One important sideline initiative this year is Türkiye's goal of increasing electricity's share of global energy use from 21% to 35% by 2035, preferably by using renewable power.

Mohamed Adow, a veteran COP observer and director of Power Shift Africa, said the conference is taking place at a time when the world is deeply fragmented.

"The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has intensified economic and energy security pressures," he said. "We don’t expect this particular conference to be able to sufficiently respond to the present-day challenge ... that is affecting livelihoods and economies.”

The climate talks are hindered by the continued absence of the U.S., Adow said, adding that it wasn't fair to nations like Nepal, which have to face catastrophic destruction alone while the world's wealthiest country and biggest polluter sits on the fence.

"A time will come when we will have to hold the U.S. accountable for the climate harms that it’s caused,” he said.
