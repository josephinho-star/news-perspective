Thousands march in Paris and other French cities as protests take place across Europe – Europe live

2026-10-24 17:36

Demonstrations kick off across France's major cities amid heavy security

People have also begun marching in the port city of Marseille, France’s second-most populous city. Thousands had gathered for the procession down the Canabière main street.

In Lyon, crowds have set off from Place Jean-Macé, chanting slogans and singing, flanked by rows of police officers. Crowds are also marching in Toulouse and Lille.

Meanwhile protest restrictions remain in place in other cities, where last-minute legal appeals have failed.

In Bordeaux, demonstrators have shown up at the Place de la Bourse despite a city-wide ban and tram cancellations.

“Whether the protest is banned or not, we will support the high school students’ movement and we won’t abandon them. We are parents, we were young once, and we are also workers,” said Brigitte Couderc, general secretary of the CGT-Commerce and Services union in the Gironde region told Le Monde.

In Nice, police said they had ordered the dispersal of a crowd of several hundred people who had gathered at Place Masséna despite all gatherings being banned.

France’s interior minister, Laurent Nuñez, earlier said that there was a turnout of about 35,000 people in marches across the country, compared with 60,000 last Saturday. He said authorities had counted 136 public street actions, down from 264 the previous weekend.

Jean-Luc Mélenchon, the radical-left presidential candidate who marched in the Paris rally today, said in a social media post that the French police “ruined the day” by “shooting indiscriminately” at Paris protesters.

“In Paris, as a way to ruin the day and blow off some steam, the so-called ‘forces of law and order’ are firing tear gas and shooting indiscriminately into the crowd at the tail end of the march, all to secure footage for domestic television channels, which have spent the entire week broadcasting the very images that incited the killer in Trappes,” he wrote. “A non-stop display of shame.”

In addition to the earlier footage of police using tear gas against protestors in Lyon, we are now seeing tear gas being deployed in the cities of Paris, Marseille, Nantes, and Bordeaux.

Though the protests have been relatively contained so far, the use of tear gas has become an increasingly common tactic for police to disperse crowds at the end of organized rallies, even without an immediate threat. And taking past demonstrations into consideration, these situations often become more volatile as the sun begins setting and the organized gatherings are ending.

The Interior Ministry has put today’s nationwide turnout at 410,000 total protestors across France.

Clashes began to erupt late on Saturday, following a pattern of otherwise peaceful protests growing more tempestuous as the day draws to a close.

The Paris march began calmly, but near its endpoint at Porte de Vincennes, some protesters threw projectiles while police fired volleys of tear gas and used water cannons to disperse people.

Authorities had confined the capital’s march to the city’s southeastern edge, keeping protesters away from central neighborhoods hit by last weekend’s destruction.

Police in Paris say that about 110,000 people have marched across France’s capital today, but that the “situation remains calm” according to reporting from AFP. Authorities have now begun dispersing the crowds.

The 110,000 figure is roughly the same amount as the number of protestors in Paris last weekend. Considering the fears many people had that the protests could turn violent, as well as the rainy weather across the area, the number is an impressive showing.

The police also said more than 3,000 checks have been carried out and 65 people were arrested during the demonstrations in Paris.

Meanwhile in Albania, more than 2000 police have been deployed around Tirana on the 12th day of student-led protests amid reports of concerted efforts to stop demonstrators entering the capital.

Protests initially spurred by fury over a planned Trump family-backed resort in Albania have turned increasingly violent as students have joined what growing numbers are now calling a revolution to oust prime minister Edi Rama. Today, rally numbers swelled with demonstrators traveling to Tirana from cities across the Balkan state and as teachers, doctors and labour unions also weighed in.

But police also stand increasingly accused of heavy-handed tactics amid reports of civilians being plucked from the streets by masked officers and riot police increasingly resorting to using rubber bullets, batons, tear gas and water cannons to disperse crowds.

Speaking to the Guardian after spending several hours at Tirana’s National Trauma hospital earlier in the day, Kei Brisku, who heads the Abania Rehabilitation Centre for Trauma and Torture, said there were growing concerns about “the mass and random use” of rubber bullets.

“Nine protestors, five women and four men, were badly wounded by rubber bullets today and are now in hospital,” he said.

“The use of rubber bullets against violent protesters may be justified under applicable regulations ... however, their indiscriminate use against crowds is unacceptable, as it risks harming peaceful protesters and bystanders who pose no threat.”

In power for 13 years, Rama has resolutely refused to resign. While he has offered to discuss the “ legitimate concerns” of students, he has ruled out talking with hooligans who resort to violence.

Last week, the socialist leader told the Guardian the “violence in Tirana is very real,” as he dismissed accusations that his government was employing underhand tactics to fast-track the controversial resort championed by Jared Kushner, the US president’s son-in-law.

The NFL (American football league) closed its “NFL House” in Paris as the city braced for street protests with a heavy police presence and storefronts boarded up.

The league announced the closure to fans on Saturday morning through its “OnePass” app, according to the AP, citing “potential accessibility issues.”

Security concerns have overshadowed the NFL’s planned debut in Paris. The New Orleans Saints will play the Pittsburgh Steelers on Sunday at Stade de France in the league’s first-ever regular-season game in France.

Major museums including the Louvre and Orsay Museum are also closed Saturday, though tourists were seen lined up to enter Notre Dame Cathedral.

25,000 people rally in Madrid for pension guarantees

Spanish authorities say about 25,000 demonstrators have marched through the country’s capital demanding protections to the state pension scheme.

Spain is voting in a snap parliamentary election in a few weeks and this has been a key topic of concern. Spain’s Socalist prime minister Pedro Sanchez, who called the snap election after struggling to get pension reforms through parliament, has accused conservative parties of holding back inflation-linked increases.

Protesters marched from the Atocha train station to the Gran Via shopping avenue in the Spanish capital.

Some expressed concern about portions of their state pensions being privatised in the future. About 6.8 million Spaniards receive a state pension and fiscally conservative, right-wing politicians have raised questions over how sustainable the current system is.

Spain has also seem major protests in recent months over increasingly unaffordable housing and renters’ rights.

Are French high school buildings dilapidated and rat-infested with collapsing ceilings?

Some are, namely in certain cities and low-income suburbs. Videos filmed by students and teachers have shown leaks, scurrying rats, holes in walls and ceilings, broken toilets and not enough chairs or tables, meaning teachers have to carry them from classroom to classroom. Some children said they had to learn in class while leaks were pouring into buckets, or move desks to avoid getting wet. Many school classrooms experienced temperatures over 35C (95F) in the heatwaves of last May and June.

Estimates in recent years have pointed to thousands of French schools at all levels, both primary and secondary, in need of repair. These include many 1960s school buildings. French regions are responsible for upkeep of high school buildings, but have asked for higher budgets from the government. A senate report has advised that billions need to be spent in the coming years to renovate and repair schools, as well as to address the impact of the climate crisis.

Tens of thousands of people have turned out to the heavily policed demonstrations across Franceand they have been, on the whole, quite contained so far.

Some sections of the crowd could be seen singing and dancing in Paris as they walked along the route, which was pushed to the city’s fringes at the last minute as police sought to move the march away from the fortified city centre.

In Lyon, police used teargas early on in the banned demonstration to disperse some of the crowd but people were still able to walk on.

In the last half hour there’s also a been of bit of rough activity in Marseille between police and a few dozen people. The French broadcaster BFMTV has shown live footage of masked attendees throwing bins, flares going off and spot fires in the street. There are cordons of riot police around.

Overall though, the heavy policing has appeared to be successful so far in staving off the type of looting, ransacking and violent activity we saw on the fringes of the marches last week. The government has added on 20% more officers, for a 70,000 strong force across the country.

Earlier today, the French interior minister had said protest numbers – at about 36,000 in the morning- were about half of what they were last Saturday.

At the Paris march, Nadège, a communications worker, said she was at Place de la République last Saturday when teargas hit. But that hadn’t deterred her.

I’m out again peacefully today to support everyone who is brave enough to demonstrate. It does take courage. People are afraid now.

We have to keep our rights alive. We’ve seen what happens in the US when a bad president is in power. In France, it’s our legal right to protest in the street and I’m here to defend that.

The French president had hosted student representatives for a highly anticipated meeting yesterday afternoon.

Going in, the student leaders had already expressed cynicism over whether the French president would adequately address their concerns.

Pupils in Paris began protests over the quality of their education four weeks ago – and their spirit has since inspired demonstrations elsewhere, across the nation and also into other countries, such as Italy and Greece.

French high schoolers say they’re fed up with the deteriorating state of their school buildings, overworked and underpaid teachers, and packed classrooms.

They’re demanding school renovations and upgrades, better study conditions and the scrapping of the Parcoursup univeristy admissions platform which was introduced in 2018 and has long been criticised for lack of transparency.

Macron held a two-and-a-half hours-long meeting with 20 students.

Nathanael Combary, president of campaign group High School Renewal, said the president had been “attentive” and they would continue talks with Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu on Monday on proposed changes like reform of Parcoursup.

But he and other student leaders called on their peers to continue protesting this weekend.

The radical-left presidential candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon, leader of the La France Insoumise party, is also marching in the Parisian procession.

Speaking to the press earlier he said the protests would continue every week until Emmanuel Macron and his government addressed the public’s demands.

He said Macron “is going to understand that this is going to repeat itself every week” and urged the president, “there is still time for him to concede” to the students’ wishes.
