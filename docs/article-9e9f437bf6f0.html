Trump says he would ‘love’ to see unified Ireland and ‘it may as well happen now’ as he visits Dublin – as it happened

2026-09-12 14:42

Trump has weighed in on the question of Irish unity, saying he would “love to see it unified” and predicting that a united Ireland is “going to happen eventually”.

He suggested it “may as well happen now” and appeared to cast Micheál Martin as the person to help make it happen.

Well, I don’t want to cause any problems, but I will tell you I’d love to see it unified.

I have friends on both sides, great people. They’re Irish people. How can they be bad, right?

I’d love to see it unified. I think it would be a great feather in everybody’s cap if that happened. It’s going to happen eventually.

You know what? The way I look at it, it’s going to happen eventually. It may as well happen now.

We’re talking about it and you got the right man over here to get it moving.

But, yeah, I think it would be a fantastic thing.

Now the UK will have something to say about it, obviously. But I think it would be a great thing. I mean, how can it be bad?”

That’s all from us on the US politics blog, thanks for following along. Here is a recap of the day’s developments:

While in Ireland for a two-day visit that combined political meetings with a stop at his Doonbeg golf resort, Donald Trump said he backed Irish reunification. He said he would “love to see” a united Ireland and that it “may as well happen now”. He added that unification would happen eventually and suggested it would be “fantastic”.

His comments prompted a backlash from unionists and UK politicians. Andy Burnham’s position on Irish unification remains unchanged, a spokesperson for the prime minister said. Earlier this week, Burnham said he would stick to the Good Friday agreement “100%” and that there would be no referendum without majority public support.

Similarly, Micheál Martin’s government said Trump’s comments would not change its position, with a spokesperson saying it was committed to the Good Friday agreement and programme for government.

Irishpresident Catherine Connolly used her meeting with Trump to stress Ireland’s opposition to war and genocide, saying the normalisation of either “can never be accepted”.

Sinn Féin leader Mary Lou McDonald called on Trump to apply the lessons of the Northern Ireland peace process to Palestine, urging the US to end arms sales to Israel and “stop facilitating the genocide of Palestinian people”.

The US president also weighed in on the Falkland Islands row, questioning whether Britain would be willing to defend the islands and saying he could potentially help settle the dispute between the UK and Argentina.

Trump said the war with Iran would end “very soon”, possibly after the US midterm elections in November, adding that he expected oil prices to “come tumbling down” afterwards.

When asked about the crisis engulfing the strait of Hormuz, he said: “Everything will work out fine.”

Trump ended his Dublin engagements before heading to Doonbeg, where he arrived at his golf resort for an afternoon of golf and an overnight stay, with the Irish Open taking place on the course.

Our Ireland correspondent Rory Carrollhas the full report here:

On Trump’s comments on Irish unification, McDonald said they should prompt the Irish government to start preparing for a united Ireland.

Sinn Féin has long campaigned for Irish unification and supports holding a referendum on the issue when the conditions set out in the Good Friday Agreement are met. Under the 1998 peace deal, the Northern Ireland secretary of state should call a referendum if it appears likely that a majority would vote in favour.

“President Trump’s comments on Irish reunification are timely. The taoiseach must now begin planning for a united Ireland,” McDonald said.

'US must stop facilitating genocide of Palestinian people,' says Sinn Féin leader

In a message to Donald Trump, Sinn Féin leader Mary Lou McDonald has called on the US to end arms supplies to Israel and “stop facilitating the genocide of Palestinian people”.

Her party has been a strong advocate for Palestinian rights and has urged for Israel to face greater accountability over its actions in Gaza.

“The US plays a critical role in supporting peace in Ireland. That same commitment to diplomacy and peace must be applied to Palestine,” McDonald said in a post on X.

Donald Trump and his entourage have arrived at Shannon Airport. The US president disembarked Air Force One, shook hands with Irish ministers and waved to the cameras before hopping aboard Marine One, the presidential helicopter, for the journey to Doonbeg.

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch has waded into the row over Trump’s Ireland unity comments, accusing the US president of “shooting from the hip” and describing his remarks as “deeply unhelpful”.

In a post on X, she said:

President Trump is shooting from the hip as usual, just like he did with Greenland and Canada becoming the 51st state. This sort of loose talk about the future of democratic countries is deeply unhelpful and creates unnecessary tension.

Northern Ireland is part of the United Kingdom. At a time when separatists are in power in Scotland, Wales and NI, we need a Government that will defend the Union clearly and confidently.

The Conservative and Unionist Party as is our true name will always defend the union. I would like to see the same clarity from Andy Burnham, rather than entertaining the idea of more independence referenda as he did on Wednesday at PMQs.

'War and genocide can never be accepted,' says Connolly

Irish president Catherine Connolly has issued a statement about her meeting with Donald Trump, saying “war and genocide can never be accepted”.

As President of Ireland, I am very conscious that Ireland and the United States of America are bound by strong ties. This is a deep connection, built over centuries, which allows us to speak openly to one another.

We are in a world facing the existential threat posed by war and climate change, both of which are inextricably interlinked.

I conveyed the strong view of the Irish people that the normalisation of war and genocide can never be accepted.

As a country which has suffered famine, colonisation and the violence of the Troubles, but also the importance of the long and challenging road to peace, I emphasised the unique insight which Irish people can bring to the resolution of conflict.”

Northern Ireland’s leading unionist parties, the DUP and UUP, have hit back at Trump’s call for a unified Ireland.

DUP leader Gavin Robinson posted on X:

President Trump knows better than most that elections matter.

Northern Ireland’s constitutional future will be decided by the people of Northern Ireland, not by commentary in London, Dublin or Washington.

Northern Ireland is part of the United Kingdom because that is the settled democratic will of its people.

I am confident NI will continue to choose the UK and the security it offers. As unionists we are not interested in the destruction of our country by joining with the Irish Republic.

TUV leader Jim Allister said Trump “is not the first US president to meddle in the affairs which don’t concern him”.

Northern Ireland is not a pawn in Trump’s fall-out with the UK. Our constitutional future does not depend on him or any other foreign dignitary.

Ulster Unionist Party Leader Jon Burrows MLA said the US president “like anyone else in the world, is entitled to express his view on the constitutional future of another country”, before adding:

But the only people whose decision counts are the people of Northern Ireland.

That is the principle of consent, now enshrined in law through the Belfast Agreement, negotiated and delivered by the Ulster Unionist Party.

America has been, and remains, an important ally, from the Second World War and the global fight against terrorism to the defence of democracy around the world. Their businesses increasingly see that our workforce here is the best in the world.

But when all is said and done, Northern Ireland’s future will be decided by the people of Northern Ireland.

I am confident that we are safer, more secure and more prosperous within the United Kingdom, and that is where we will remain.

Speaking of the Irish people, there have been protests in Dublin against Trump’s visit.

When he was asked earlier about the demonstrations, the US president said: “I didn’t know there was a protest. It can’t be a very big one because we’ve been all over Ireland today already, it’s early in the morning… but I haven’t seen any protest, It’s a friendly protest.”

Back to Trump’s speech, where he has offered his assessment of the Irish people, praising them for their warmth and friendliness before warning they can be “vicious bastards”.

You know they say you can’t generalise about groups of people, countries, but you can. People love coming to Ireland. You know why? Because they love you. They love the people. There’s a friendliness.

There’s also a viciousness and a toughness. Let me tell you, you are some of the most vicious bastards... but when you want to be nice, there is nobody nicer.”

PA reports that among the gifts Donald Trump received from Irish president Catherine Connolly was a St Brigid’s Cross, traditionally associated with peace, along with a collection of poetry that included Michael Longley’s sonnet Ceasefire.

The symbolism of the gifts is rather hard to miss. As our Ireland correspondent, Rory Carroll, writes in his report ahead of Trump’s visit to Dublin, Connolly was a vocal critic of Trump before becoming president:

Before winning the Irish presidency – a ceremonial post – last year, Connolly was an independent leftwing member of parliament for Galway who protested against Trump’s 2019 visit and more recently assailed him over his positions and policies on Gaza, Iran and other issues. “If we are going to act as an independent sovereign state, a neutral republic, we must use our voice to speak to power and the abuse of power by president Trump,” she said.

Asked in March about the criticism, Trump replied: “He’s lucky I exist.”
