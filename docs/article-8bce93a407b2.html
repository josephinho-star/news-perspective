Beetaloo gas set to flow in NT, despite environmental concerns

The first gas from the controversial Beetaloo Basin fracking projects in Australia’s north is set to flow to homes and businesses in a territory touting its vast energy supply potential, AAP reports.

The basin is a major onshore shale gas resource in the Northern Territory’s Sturt Plateau, about 500km south-east of Darwin.

On Tuesday, first gas is set to flow, with more than 40 terajoules a day going into the territory’s gas grid. The territory uses about 60 terajoules a day of gas at peak levels for power generation and mines.

The NT chief minister, Lia Finocchiaro, said:

Every time Territorians flick on a light switch, or pull a cold beer from the fridge, they’ll see the benefits of the Beetaloo Basin at work.

Beetaloo fracking, construction of pipelines to get the gas to market, and using the gas to power AI datacentres have all been vigorously opposed by environmental groups. Fracking could see up to 6,000 wells drilled through underground water supplies, which supply 90% of the territory’s water needs, Environment Centre NT acting chief executive, Bree Ahrens, said:

Water is life in the territory and we are calling for a ban on fracking and AI datacentres before it’s too late.

South Australia asks federal government to remove telehealth barriers for voluntary assisted dying

South Australia has asked the federal government to amend the criminal code to remove legal uncertainty around the use of telehealth for voluntary assisted dying (VAD) services.

In a letter sent to the attorney general, Michelle Rowland, a slate of South Australian officials, VAD advocates and practitioners urged the changes were needed to make the service equitable. Australia currently prevents telehealth for voluntary assisted dying consults. Labor’s national conference recently voted to repeal the law, but the prime minister has thrown water on the idea.

The group wrote in a letter to Rowland:

As you are aware, the operation of the Criminal Code continues to create significant uncertainty regarding the provision of information about VAD via carriage services, including telehealth. This restriction is having a disproportionate impact on people living in regional and remote communities.

This restriction creates a barrier in equitable access to lawful healthcare and is becoming increasingly inconsistent with contemporary models of healthcare delivery. This is particularly true where telehealth plays an essential role in improving access for patients who are unable to attend appointments in person.

In a press release this morning, the shadow agriculture minister, Darren Chester, said the quietly announced “go-slow” approach – which aims to address political pressure over higher immigration levels in recent years – was causing farmers “enormous uncertainty”.

Working holidaymakers are a critical part of the seasonal agricultural workforce, particularly during peak harvest periods, so the timing of Labor’s delays is critical. This is not just an inconvenience for farmers.

If growers cannot access the workers they need when crops are ready, it affects food supply, supermarket shelves and the prices Australian families pay at the checkout … we need practical migration settings that support agriculture, strengthen regional communities and help keep food on Australian tables.

AI will likely have ‘profound’ impact on jobs in Australia

Treasury officials believe the adoption of artificial intelligence will likely have a “profound” impact on Australian jobs.

Jim Chalmers requested a departmental briefing paper on what AI means for productivity, investment and employment, ahead of the release of the government’s latest intergenerational report later this month.

To what degree AI replaces or augments Australian jobs remains an open question, and the answer depends on how transformational you believe the general purpose technology (or GPT) will prove to be.

The Treasury briefing note says:

Because AI has the potential to automate a significant share of cognitive, non-routine tasks currently performed by high-skilled workers, and accelerate productivity and economic growth, labour market impacts could be materially different to past waves of technology.

In either case, the effects are likely to be profound.

Jobs and Skill Australia has found that most occupations in Australia have more potential to be augmented rather than simply replaced by AI automation.

Separate research from the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations found no evidence that AI was having a big impact on jobs – so far.

Early uptake of AI in Australia is promising but task automation is still at an early stage and greater reorganisation within firms is needed to capture AI’s productivity benefits.

NSW HSC students limited to one take-home assessment per subject

Year 12 students in New South Wales will be limited to just one take-home assessment worth no more than 15% of the school-based assessment mark for each HSC course, under new rules to combat the use of AI announced this morning.

School-based assessment is worth 50% of a student’s HSC result, meaning the sole take-home assessment would only be worth 7.5% of the total HSC mark under the changes. The plan comes after the state’s minister for education, Prue Car, asked the NSW Education Standards Authority (NESA) to look at measures to protect student learning.

Right now, schools can determine how much of that 50% school assessment is given through take-home tasks. Schools will still be able to use a range of assessment types for each course, including exams, oral tasks and process diaries, but the take-home component will be dramatically reduced in value.

HSC major works are not included in the restrictions, but schools will still be required to authenticate the work.

The limit will apply to students in the class of 2027 beginning HSC studies in term 4 this year, and to students beginning year 11 in term 1, 2027.

The message this sends to schools and communities more broadly is that we are committed to protecting the teaching and learning happening in schools.

When students outsource their learning – whether that is to tutors, classmates or artificial intelligence, they are missing out on the rigorous, high-quality education they need to set themselves up for the future.

Attorney general says any action on smartglasses needs to strike ‘balance’ without ‘stifling innovation’

Michelle Rowland, the attorney general, said the spread of smartglasses has become an important issue and concern to many Australians, but the government needs to fully understand the pros and cons to new technology before banning or limiting its use.

Rowland spoke to RN Breakfast this morning after Australia’s online safety regulator urged smartglasses companies to automatically blur people’s faces among other changes as the government faces pressure to ban the technology.

The attorney general told RN Breakfast this morning there were legitimate reasons to use the glasses, including for those with vision impairment, but there were legitimate concerns as well:

One of the key points here when we talk about addressing digital harms is that we identify the harms and we need to address those harms without stifling innovation … and we need to understand that Australians have always been early adopters. So it is about striking that balance.

I do want to stress that whilst I completely understand and there are very legitimate concerns that people have about nefarious use, the government doesn’t want to see that nefarious use. But we do want to make sure that we get this right.
