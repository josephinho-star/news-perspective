From Gaza to Edinburgh: How poetry crossed every blockade

2026-09-25 07:39

In October 2024, riding a bus from Edinburgh Airport into the heart of the Scottish capital, I picked up a copy of "The Skinny," a local arts and culture magazine. Inside was a thoughtful feature on the Gaza-born poet Mosab Abu Toha. Thousands of miles away from the besieged enclave, his verses had found a home in the everyday rhythm of a Scottish commute.

It was a poignant reminder of a fundamental truth: while borders can be closed and land can be occupied, the sovereign word possesses an innate ability to breach every blockade.

In the Palestinian struggle for liberation, language has never been a mere literary ornament or an aesthetic luxury. It is an indispensable pillar of existence, a repository of collective memory and an act of existential defiance against erasure.

This historical continuity runs deep. Decades ago, Mahmoud Darwish articulated the soul of the resistance, demonstrating how poetry could reconstruct a homeland in exile. In Jean-Luc Godard's film "Notre Musique," set against the scarred backdrop of post-war Sarajevo, Darwish reflected on the weight of the Palestinian voice, showing that when a people's physical space is systematically dismantled, the written and spoken word becomes their ultimate, inviolable territory.

Tragically, the custodians of this word remain primary targets. The late educator and poet Refaat Alareer, who would have celebrated his 47th birthday this month, was killed in Gaza during the ongoing genocide. Yet his final literary testament, "If I Must Die," refused to be silenced:

"If I must die, / you must live / to tell my story..."

Recited by prominent global figures, translated into dozens of languages and immortalized on banners and paper kites across the world, Alareer's poem transformed from a personal farewell into an international anthem of conscience.

Poets like Mosab Abu Toha – who survived the bombardment – carry this legacy forward through works like "Forest of Noise," ensuring that raw human witnessing dismantles the sterilized statistics of mainstream news channels.

What we are witnessing today goes far beyond individual acts of expression; it is the birth of an informal yet powerful global Gaza Poets Society.

The battle for narrative supremacy, long dominated by a heavily skewed media apparatus, is no longer confined to traditional printed pages or television screens. Poets, MCs and musicians across continents have effectively coalesced into a single, decentralized collective on global platforms.

Young Gaza-born rapper MC Abdul channels the pain and endurance of his generation into sharp, rhythmic verse, reaching millions globally. Mediterranean-born artist Saint Levant seamlessly weaves Arabic, English and French on international stages, while artists and writers from the U.K. to the U.S. join this unified chorus, using their platforms to explicitly ground their art in solidarity.

This resurgence of vocal advocacy has exposed a stark litmus test within the global cultural establishment. During the early stages of the Russia-Ukraine war, international stages and award shows were swiftly draped in blue and yellow. Artists expressed solidarity freely, shielded by an institutional consensus that framed their stance as a universal humanitarian duty rather than a controversial political act.

Yet, when it comes to Palestine, that same cultural establishment has frequently enforced a swift regime of censorship, cancellations and selective neutrality.

This double standard was laid bare during recent tour arrangements in the United States. When Ed Sheeran moved to exclude Macklemore – a musician unwavering in his advocacy for Gaza – from his tour lineup, the attempt at censorship immediately backfired.

Macklemore dismissed the move, emphasizing that the true victims were not sidelined Western artists, but the people of Gaza enduring a genocide. Sheeran's subsequent expressions of regret failed to satisfy a global public that recognized the hypocrisy of performative activism.

Instead of stifling the message, the move triggered a chain reaction, transforming mainstream concert venues into active arenas of political accountability and exposing how calculated neutrality collapses under public scrutiny.

As an African proverb wisely reminds us, "When the music changes, so does the dance."

We are living through days in which the global narrative itself is undergoing a seismic shift. In building this new world framework, Gaza has composed both the lyrics and the music of our shared human condition. Today, where one stands regarding Gaza is no longer an invisible choice; it is an undeniable testament to where one stands on humanity itself.

Palestine will not be liberated by a single poem, a rap verse or a stadium speech alone. The restoration of land, rights and human dignity requires tangible justice. Yet no liberation movement in human history has ever prevailed without the force of a resonant, unyielding narrative.

As long as powerful words continue to break through the noise – from the refugee camps of Gaza to the buses of Edinburgh – the attempt to erase Palestine from the global conscience will remain an impossible task.
