AI is reshaping banking jobs – but its ability to drive profits remains unclear

2026-11-16 09:35

Banks' third-quarter results have brought their use of artificial intelligence into sharper focus, as lenders automate routine work, reshape hiring and test whether productivity gains can translate into revenue growth.

This earnings season, Barclays unveiled layoffs in certain functions where AI is being deployed; while UniCredit and Santander said technology was freeing employees for higher-value work and improving processes. It comes as U.S. giants such as JPMorgan Chase and Goldman Sachs make similar changes in staffing and workflows.

But as banks pursue efficiency savings, strategists say evidence that AI is generating material, demonstrable revenue gains from it remains harder to pin down.

UniCredit CEO Andrea Orcel called AI an "opportunity to accelerate" that was allowing a shift toward more commercial, higher-value work and greater client engagement.

Orcel said the Italian lender — which reported its best-ever quarterly profit of 3.3 billion euros — is using the technology to "get us to the next level."

Orcel called AI "a lever that we didn't anticipate we would have, and working extremely well," on CNBC's "Squawk Box Europe" on Nov. 9.

Santander said AI initiatives generated 194 million euros in business value in the first nine months of 2026. AI is now being embedded in "all the processes that we are conducting at the bank," said Chief Financial Officer José García Cantera.

"AI basically helps, at least for now, decrease low value-added processes, and the company employees can then focus on more value-added processes — this affects both revenue and costs," Cantera told CNBC's "Squawk Box Europe" on Oct. 28.

"It's difficult to just to say how much comes from AI, but clearly AI is improving the quality of our services to clients, is helping us in asset quality, in cost management, and is having clearly a very positive impact."

Dutch lender ABN AMRO is using AI to support anti-money-laundering checks and expanding its partnership with Infosys to modernize its technology, while preserving human judgement, CEO Marguerite Bérard said in the bank's third-quarter results on Nov. 11.

"These improvements make the bank easier to run and scale, and help colleagues spend more time on activities that create value for clients," she said.

Some lenders announced big headcount reductions during the third quarter, which are "partly, but not solely, due to AI," said Andrew Stimpson, managing director and head of European banks research at KBW.

"The cuts have tended to come from back-office and support functions rather than the front office," Stimpson told CNBC via email.

Barclays said it plans to cut 5,000 jobs — about 5% of its workforce — in 2026. Roughly 60% of the reductions will affect support functions, including developer roles, the British lender said in its third-quarter earnings update, though it sought to emphasize that AI is not responsible for all the cuts.

The Financial Times reported on Oct. 7 that HSBC could cut around half of management and specialist roles, and as many as 70% of financial adviser roles, in its U.K. wealth business as AI reshapes how it serves clients.

Goldman Sachs said it intends to "constrain headcount growth" and cut a limited number of jobs. JPMorgan managers have been told to avoid hiring as the bank deploys AI across its businesses, CFO Jeremy Barnum told analysts in the lender's third-quarter earnings call.

A KBW study published in September examined AI use across European banks. Customer service — including chatbots and sorting customer requests — was the most frequently cited use case, followed by behind-the-scenes functions such as operations, identity checks and preventing financial crime.

Cost savings remain the most commonly cited goal: just under half of the use cases KBW tracked were aimed at cutting costs or improving efficiency. The report found banks are increasingly exploring ways to grow revenue, while noting AI is not yet seen as a major driver of profits.

Wealth management and advisory are emerging as key tests of whether AI can boost bank revenues, rather than only lower costs, analysts say.

Banks seem "fairly aligned" that they can do this in wealth management, where AI could enable advisors "to serve a larger number of clients as AI makes onboarding and reporting much more efficient," said Stimpson.

Sophia Whitbread, an analyst at Ninety One, said the evidence is still strongest for AI improving service, engagement and employee productivity, rather than demonstrably generating revenue. But initiatives that let advisors use AI to support more clients could expand access to personalized guidance without a corresponding increase in relationship managers.

She said wealth management was likely "the most important revenue test because AI can augment rather than replace the advisor."

"For European banks, the probable model is 'human adviser plus AI', especially at UBS, Standard Chartered and the private-banking units of Deutsche, ABN and the French banks, rather than a fully automated relationship," Whitbread said, pointing to JPMorgan's advisor and wealth-planning tools as a U.S. example of that approach.

AI is also reducing "traditional project management and back-office opportunities," according to Mark Astbury, a director at recruitment consultant Morgan McKinley.

There is greater demand for "specialists in AI, software engineering and data," Astbury told CNBC via email.

"London's financial services sector is increasingly becoming a technology employer. Demand for talent hasn't disappeared, but the skills banks are investing in are changing."

As AI takes on more routine tasks junior employees traditionally handle, "banks will need to rethink how they develop the next generation of talent," he said.

"We're already seeing senior roles demanding specialist AI skills that go well beyond basic use of tools like Copilot or Gemini, and I expect entry-level positions will become increasingly technical too."
