Tories dismiss PM’s budget ‘everyday fixes’ as little more than ‘cheaper gherkins’, and ‘an insult to families’ – UK politics live

2026-10-27 10:52

Removing tariffs on more than 100 products, including gherkins and baked beans, was one of five measures announced this morning before tomorrow’s budget

Tories dismiss PM’s budget ‘everyday fixes’ as little more than ‘cheaper gherkins’, and ‘an insult to families

Good morning. Prime ministers tend to get judged by posterity by the decisions they take on the big issues of the day. But little things matter too in the here and now, often disproportionately, and Andy Burnham, more than his predecessors, has decided that what he calls “everyday fixes” are worth focusing on too. Alexandra Topping wrote a good analysis about the pluses and minuses of this approach last week. But she filed too soon, because this morning Burnham has announced another five.

These are all changes contained in tomorrow’s budget but being announced today – presumably because John Healey will have bigger surprises for MPs tomorrow, and the Treasury wants these measures to get some media attention today because tomorrow they will be overshadowed by another story.

One of the measures is of particular interest to the Guardian because we have highlighted the scandal that led to hundreds of thousands of people getting the carer’s allowance being ordered to pay back huge sums of money because inadvertently their weekly earnings went slightly above the threshold allowed. In other parts of the welfare system, people just have to return the overpayment, but the rules for carer’s allowance were draconian, and a tiny breach of the weekly earnings allowance could lead to people being ordered to pay back thousands of pounds.

As Patrick Butler and Pippa Crerar report in our splash, this rule is being scrapped.

But this is one of just five “everyday fixes” announced this morning. Here is what the Treasury says about them.

• Making higher tax-free mileage rates permanent and increasing support for nurses, care workers, self employed builders and others who drive for work. The chancellor is locking in the 55p per mile rate for the first 10,000 business miles and increasing the rate above 10,000 miles from 25p to 35p per mile, supporting around 3 million employees and self-employed people who use their own vehicle for work.

• Extending parental pay to more than 150,000 additional parents per year, including self-employed dads and partners who have previously missed out, giving eligible parents two weeks of support currently worth up to £194 a week so they can spend more time with their child.

• Ending the Carer’s Allowance cliff edge, so unpaid carers no longer lose their entire allowance simply for earning a penny over the Weekly Earnings Limit, equivalent to 16 hours of the National Living Wage. Instead, only £1 of support will taper away for every additional £1 earned.

• Removing import tariffs on more than 100 products, helping lower import costs on family favourites including baked beans, chocolate and gherkins, while also supporting farmers facing higher fertiliser bills and households that rely on kerosene heating oil. Together with tariff suspensions already introduced, households could save up to £21 on their food shop each year.

• Rolling out hundreds more Post Office drop boxes, bringing the total to 1,000, making it easier and more convenient for people to send and return parcels without having to queue at a Post Office counter, helping take some of the hassle out of everyday errands in the run-up to Christmas. This comes alongside upgrades to 600 Post Office branches by the end of the financial year, improving services for customers across the country.

There is more detail in the Treasury news release here.

It is always easy to mock, and the Conservatives could not resist. Here is their response, from Andrew Griffith, the shadow chancellor.

Rampant borrowing, soaring taxes and an utter inability to cut spending mean inflation has already wiped out the benefits of Burnham’s ‘everyday fixes’.

Shoppers face rising costs at the till and families are struggling to make ends meet, but Burnham’s plan is cheaper gherkins. This is an insult to families hammered by Labour’s tax rises.

The mention of gherkins in the Treasury press release did come as a surprise, given that they are not a key feature of the national diet, but maybe someone in Whitehall read the reports earlier this year about their importance for Pret a Manger sandwiches.

The problem with the Tory response is that, even though “everyday fixes” might not make a big difference, people tend to welcome them anyway. This is from Novara Media’s Aaron Bastani.

Part of the genius with this stuff is the negative polarisation, when people start attacking perfectly sensible and helpful – albeit small – changes.

Actually I want longer Post Office queues please!

Streeting says on visit to Poland that UK and Nato need to be ready for war with Russia

Jakub Krupa writes the Guardian’s Europe live blog.

Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, has warned that Russia’s Vladimir Putin is “testing us and testing Nato’s resolve” as he condemned hybrid threats and “state-sponsored terrorism” from Russia.

Streeting repeated his recent warnings that “we have to be ready for a scenario in which the United Kingdom and the Nato alliance finds itself at war with Russia”. He went on:

Not because we want war – we have no quarrel with the Russian people, and we have no desire for war with Russia; Nato is a defensive alliance – but when Putin gives assurances that he has no intentions to provoke a war with Nato, we are reminded of what he said about Ukraine only days before the invasion.

Speaking in Warsaw alongside Poland’s defence minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz, Streeting stressed Nato is ready “to defend every inch of Nato territory” and will continue its support for Ukraine.

The defence secretary added:

The more ready we are to face against Russia, to defend every inch of Nato territory, the less likely we are to find ourselves in that scenario.

Poland is one of our greatest and certainly our most reliable friend in Europe, and we value and treasure this friendship very much.

The partnership is “not simply about our shared history,” but also “our shared determination that in that contest between freedom and tyranny, the United Kingdom and Poland will not be bystanders.”

He said that UK forces will next month take part in major exercises as part of the multinational force for Ukraine.

An astonishing 80% of donations to Reform UK in the past 12 months have come from donors or companies recently based overseas, The Independent can reveal.

An analysis of data from the Electoral Commission shows the amount received from donors who were based abroad in the past two years by Nigel Farage’s party dwarfs that of other parties amid growing concern about overseas influence in British politics.

In comparison, Labour and the Conservatives received just 0.15% and 3.57% of their donations from people or companies based abroad respectively during the same period.

Asked about the Independent’s analysis in his Today programme interview, Farage said the conclusions were “completely wrong”.

Whoever’s done this research doesn’t understand where people domicile, how they operate, where they live, and where they vote …

I can assure you, these people vote here, live here, some of them are international businessmen who travelled the world constantly. In the old days, we used to be proud of British pioneers that went out there and made a great success of their lives.

Asked why Reform UK was so reliant on donors who spent a lot of time abroad, Farage said it would be a lot easier for him if he could just “sell some peerages” like other parties.

(Of course, parties aren’t allowed to sell peerages. But Farage is right to say that donating large sums to a mainstream political party does seem to have a remarkably positive effect on one’s chance of getting a seat in the House of Lords.)

Treasury claims its food tariff cuts, combined with Rachel Reeves' tariff cuts, will cut consumer costs by up to £595m

It is not just gherkins where the Treasury is cutting import tariffs. (See 9.07am.) Here is the lists from the Treasury of mostly food imports where tariffs are being cut.

Garlic (8% + £100 per 100 kg)

Citrus jams, marmalades and pastes (20% + £19 per 100 kg)

Throat pastilles and cough drops (8%)

Boiled sweets, filled or unfilled (8%)

Toffees, caramels and similar sweets (8%)

Plant-based beverages (soya, nut, cereal or seed) (8%)

Chocolate-coated waffles/wafers (8%)

Fertilisers (between 2-6.5%)

The Treasury also cut tariffs on some food imports in the summer. It says “together, the two packages are expected to reduce consumer costs by approximately £245m to £595m a year”.

When Andy Burnham opened a debate on revising the Brexit settlement in his Labour conference speech, he did so on the basis of a simple proposition. He declared: “I cannot say to you truthfully that where we are is good enough. Brexit has done more harm than good.”

The Conservatives have strongly attacked Burnham’s move – but mostly on the basis that the 2016 referendum decision should be honoured, that a new debate would be divisive and that Burnham’s initiative went against assurances he made during the Makerfield byelection. Very few Tories have challenged the proposition that “Brexit has done more harm than good”, and Kemi Badenoch has not seriously tried to take on this argument.

Reform UK has also been keen to attack Burnham more on the basis of problems that rejoining the EU would throw up than on the basis of the supposed Brexit benefits the UK might be sacrificing. But, in an interview this morning on the Today programme, Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, did argue that Brexit had worked.

Asked if he regarded Brexit as a success, he replied:

Yes, I do. We certainly stand taller in the world. The terrible projections of doom as to what would happen to our financial sector etc haven’t happened. Our growth rates are higher than our European competitors. Our exports, particularly of services to the European Union, have actually rocketed. And we’ve now got more trade deals and more options around the rest of the world.

But Farage also accepted that Brexit had failed to cut immigration numbers in the way that people who voted for leave were expecting. “So yes, in the eyes of many, the delivery of Brexit has been a failure.”

Farage also claimed Burnham had no mandate to reopen the Brexit debate. He said:

Five months ago, Andy Burnham says … I’m not going to reopen these arguments.

Two-and-a-half years ago, a Labour government got a massive majority in parliament saying we will not be re-entering the single market, the customs union, or opening up a debate on the free movement of people.

What I am saying is this: this prime minister has no mandate, and to hear that his officials are negotiating in Brussels already is really very, very poor indeed.

Unison says focus on cost of living, including tax-free mileage, 'commendable', but 'bolder' policies needed

Unison has particularly welcomed the announcement today about higher tax-free mileage rates being made permanent. (See 9.07am.) The union has been campaigning for this, and some of its members will benefit.

But, in her response, Andrea Egan, Unison’s general secretary illustrated how there is a limit to how much goodwill a government can generate from small “everyday fixes”. She said:

These are welcome measures, especially making the uplift to mileage rates permanent, something Unison has campaigned hard for. The government’s relentless focus on the cost of living is commendable.

But the cost of essentials, including food and fuel, is going to keep soaring, meaning breathing space will quickly be closed down. That’s why we need to see a bolder approach, based on making the wealthy pay their fair share and improving the lives of working-class communities in a more enduring way.

The Treasury says three million people are expected to benefit from the mileage changes. It says:

These changes will provide £120 a year in additional tax savings for a worker doing 6,000 business miles, with someone driving 15,000 business miles receiving up to £300 in additional tax savings.
