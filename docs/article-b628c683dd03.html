Why the NYC Council has gone completely mad — without power

2026-11-23 10:30

New York’s City Council has gone mad, scrabbling from one bizarre or dangerous scheme to another, because its 51 members have lost their main power to serve their individual districts, their authority over land use.

Without the ability to protect residents from development plans, members turn to gambits that appeal to ideologues or special interests, whether it’s serving the Teamsters’ bid to hold up Amazon or animal-rights nuts’ crusade to spare lobsters from the pot or a double-whammy like banning horse carriages, which pleases elites and the developers who want the horse-stables property.

By design, the City Council is not a powerful body. It’s stuck largely following the mayor’s lead on the budget; it can’t impose or raise taxes; it must rely on the mayor to execute the laws it passes.

Even when the council has an oversight hearing, the mayor’s office can blow it off, as it did recently when members sought to investigate the pied-à-terre tax-rollout fiasco.

Each member used to have significant power over land use: For decades, the principle of “member deference” let members veto real-estate projects contrary to the interests of their district.

That forced developers to bargain with council members to gain valuable zoning variances by offering to fund neighborhood amenities like new schools or libraries, or by reducing a project’s scale to match the area’s character.

It also let members nix development their constituents despised.

Member deference was real democracy in action, often the only way that neighborhood residents could make their voices heard about community density and texture.

Yes, this caused problems, blocking developments of clear benefit to the wider city and prompting an alliance of central planners (especially subsidized-housing advocates) and real-estate interests to unite behind ballot proposals that stripped the City Council of most of its land-use-approval power to allow fast-tracking of projects in the name of “cutting red tape” and “efficiency.”

No longer able to stop even wildly unpopular development, members rush to find other ways to win popularity, please donors or just feel important.

Hence the flood of oddball initiatives — prohibiting the sale of pet birds; a city-run “payment fund” for gig workers; tweaks to food-delivery apps to make people tip more; preventing AI systems from making fun of elected officials.

Reformers have left the City Council without much useful to do — and idle hands do the devil’s work.
