From mocked to mainstream: how Spain’s far-right Vox transformed its fortunes

2026-11-23 10:37

Almost exactly eight years ago, a video clip of a stocky, bearded man riding a horse through western Andalucía went viral in Spain. The rider was Santiago Abascal, the leader of the far-right Vox party, the soundtrack was a bombastic snatch of the Lord of the Rings score, and the caption was an equally unsubtle callback to the 700-year fight to wrest the Iberian peninsula from Islamic rule. “La Reconquista comenzará en tierras andaluzas,” it read – the reconquest will begin on Andalucían soil.

Celebrated by some, jeered by others – and inventively lampooned by those with sufficient skill and time on their hands – the video didn’t seem quite as amusing three weeks later when Vox broke through into the political mainstream by winning 12 seats in Andalucía’s regional parliament.

Until that moment, the prevailing wisdom had been that Spain’s experiences of the Franco dictatorship had inoculated the country against the far right, and that Vox was a tiny, fringe outfit whose prospects were terminally limited after it had picked up just 0.2% of the vote in the 2016 general election.

Fast-forward to late 2026 and the horseman seems less comical still. Vox, now the third largest party in Spain, is poised to pick up almost 20% of the vote in the snap general election on 29 November and, very possibly, to form a coalition government with the conservative People’s party (PP).

Abascal is going all out as the campaign enters its final days, peppering his patriotic appeals with familiar, baseless conspiracy theories. Last week he suggested that the socialist prime minister, Pedro Sánchez, was conspiring with dark forces to undermine democracy and change the country’s demographic makeup.

“The next elections may be the last that Spaniards get to decide,” he posted on X. “That’s what Sánchez and his accomplices at home and abroad are working toward … Vote so you don’t get replaced.”

How did Vox’s fortunes transform so massively and so quickly? The party was founded in late 2013 by disenchanted PP members who felt the conservatives had grown too indulgent of the demands of Basque and Catalan nationalist parties, but it struggled to make its mark. The highlight of its early years, during which it relied on hefty sum donated by the Iranian exile group the National Council of Resistance of Iran, was unfurling a large Spanish flag on the Rock of Gibraltar.

All that changed in the autumn of 2017 when a Catalan push for independence pitched Spain into crisis and left the PP government looking soft and ill-prepared. The crisis proved a godsend for Vox, simultaneously vindicating its distrust of Catalan and Basque nationalist parties and allowing it to position itself as an uncompromising defender of Spanish unity.

“Vox’s initial growth was driven by a reaction to Catalan nationalism – specifically, the sense around 2017 that Spain might fall apart,” says Miguel González, an El País journalist who covers Vox and the author of a new book, The Extremist Galaxy: Funders, Allies and Strategies of the New Far Right.

It gave Vox the profile it needed to pursue what González considers its most significant move: dragging immigration into mainstream politics and forcing a rattled PP to lurch further to the right.

“It was only an issue in a few specific places with high levels of immigration – such as El Ejido in Almería, or certain areas in Murcia,” González says. “[But] Vox has managed to put this issue on the political agenda, and, what’s more, the PP has gradually come to adopt some of their positions.”

This summer’s mass influx into Spain’s north African territory of Ceuta, when more than 80,000 men, women and children crossed over from Morocco, is a case in point. Abascal called it “an invasion and an act of war promoted by Morocco”, while the PP’s leader, Alberto Núñez Feijóo urged the expulsion of all who had entered, regardless of their age and individual circumstances.

The PP’s efforts to stop its voters slipping into the embrace of its far-right rival have led it into all sorts of contortions. Although Feijóo’s predecessor, Pablo Casado, famously rounded on Vox in 2020, accusing Abascal and co of peddling a politics based on “fear, anger, resentment and revenge”, the PP has repeatedly entered into regional coalition governments with Vox and now runs four of Spain’s autonomous regions with the party.

Vicente Valentim, a professor of political science at Spain’s IE University and the author of The Normalisation of the Radical Right, says that when centre-right parties adopt the language and postures of the far right, they end up legitimising it.

“After the initial normalisation moment when these parties first become successful – entering parliament etc – there’s a second step,” he says. “The centre right moves closer to them, starts speaking the same way that they do, starts going into coalitions.”

This can prove electorally expensive, he says. “The PP has moved closer to Vox, and when you have two parties that have such similar views on an issue as important as immigration, [some] voters simply prefer the original to the copy, and so they’re going to vote for Vox.”

Alongside a fixation on immigration, Vox has also brought its predilection for hate figures into the mainstream of Spanish politics – be they the secessionist former Catalan president Carles Puigdemont, the former leader of the leftwing Podemos Pablo Iglesias, or Sánchez.

“Vox talks about traitors and about criminals,” says González. “What Vox is doing isn’t offering political assessments, it’s making legal judgments.”

Abascal’s loathing of Sánchez is well documented – in 2023, the Vox leader said a day would come when the Spanish people would “want to see [Sánchez] strung up by his feet” – and his party’s manifesto vows to “undertake legal proceedings to have Sánchez and his government prosecuted for treason and crimes against the security of the state, based on their actions during the invasion of Ceuta”.

The party’s increasingly strident language has been matched by a more recent refusal to sever ties with some of the more extreme figures in its ranks and in its orbit.

In the run-up to the first of the two general elections in 2019, Vox was forced to drop the historian and commentator Fernando Paz from its list of candidates after Jewish groups complained about his “denialist and revisionist” approach to the Holocaust. Today he sits on the honorary board of the Foundation for the Defence of the Spanish Nation, a Vox-aligned platform of which Abascal is a co-founder and board member.

One pro-Israel lobbying group has expressed concerns in the past about Jordi de la Fuente, a Vox member and former neo-Nazi party leader who heads the Solidarity trade union, which was created by Vox. De la Fuente, who is awaiting trial for his alleged role in a violent attack on a centre housing unaccompanied migrant children in 2019, is standing as Vox’s candidate in Tarragona. Vox has insisted its candidate is innocent of a charge of “inciting hatred, hostility, violence or discrimination on the grounds of national origin”.

Alejandro Baer, a sociologist at the Spanish National Research Council who specialises in memory, genocide studies and contemporary Jewish studies, says: “A few years ago, the party was forced to distance itself from them, but now they don’t seem to represent a liability. Vox appears to be sufficiently consolidated and legitimate to openly incorporate people with backgrounds in these extremist spaces.”

Vox’s trajectory is plain to see in Rioja, a small town in the eastern Andalucían province of Almería. Long a socialist stronghold, Rioja was the only municipality where Vox finished first in May’s regional election – a poll that resulted in another PP-Vox coalition, after the conservatives lost their absolute majority. And yet the quiet town, whose palm trees and bougainvillaeas rustle in the warm November breeze, doesn’t exactly have the feel of a far-right frontier. Its divisions appear more generational than political.

Few of the younger people walking down the main road that bisects Rioja are surprised by Vox’s strong showing here. Fewer still appear bothered by Sánchez’s warning that a PP-Vox coalition will “roll back progress” and lead to the loss of hard-won social advances.

“My own ideology tends more to the right,” says Patricio López, 29, who works in an agricultural company. “I’ve noticed that people of my age tend to be more rightwing than leftwing, and I think that’s because they feel like they’ve been shortchanged. Housing, for example, is a much bigger issue for young people than it is for older people. And the left hasn’t looked after young people over the past eight years.”

One older resident, however, believes Vox’s surge in Rioja has more to do with protest votes from the young and the proximity of a large military camp than a rejection of its socialist past.

“I think it was just a gesture of distrust and revenge in the way Spanish politics is these days – it’s pure partisan theatre and it’s all very Game of Thrones,” says Rafael Ruiz Martínez, 63, who works for the local council. But he adds: “For those of us who lived through some of the Franco era, the Vox stuff doesn’t sound good.”

Another younger local man, who prefers not to give his name, agrees many people are sick of traditional politics and of a political class that has failed them.

“I reckon whoever gets in – whatever their political stripe – will do what others have done; they’ll dip into the till,” he says. “But politics is politics and people want a change.”
