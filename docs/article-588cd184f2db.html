Attorneys for Mahmoud Khalil, the former Columbia University student who has become the symbol of the Trump administration’s crusade against pro-Palestinian speech, have petitioned the US supreme court to review his case – the final chapter in a months-long legal saga that could determine countless foreign citizens’ ability to challenge their detention by US immigration authorities in federal court.

Khalil, a US permanent resident who was born in a Palestinian refugee camp in Syria, was arrested in his Columbia housing in March 2025, the first of several foreign students and scholars the Trump administration targeted over their advocacy for Palestinian rights. He was detained for more than three months in an Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) facility in Louisiana before a federal judge in New Jersey, where he had been briefly held before ICE moved him, ordered him released while his immigration process proceeds.

“This fight is not only about whether I get to stay in this country, it’s really about whether anyone in this country can speak out for Palestinians without being punished for it,” Khalil said during a press conference on Monday.

The US departments of state, justice and homeland security did not respond to requests for comment.

The government has cited an obscure McCarthy-era law to argue that Khalil and the other scholars’ pro-Palestinian expression posed a threat to US foreign policy objectives.

So far, no court has fully weighed in on the constitutionality of the law, with legal arguments instead focused on whether district courts have the authority to order people released from immigration detention while their immigration cases are under way. Khalil’s attorneys and immigrants rights advocates warn that if the supreme court rules the district courts lack such authority, that would effectively result in people the government has detained unconstitutionally to languish in immigration detention with no recourse, potentially for months or years.

Department of Justice announces arrest of 28 legal permanent residents over alleged voter fraud

The Department of Justice has announced the arrest of 28 legal permanent residents or green card holders in California “who are charged in criminal complaints with federal crimes, including that they fraudulently registered to vote in US elections, electoral contests in which most of them cast illegal and fraudulently obtained ballots”. (A reminder that, as the DoJ press release says, these are allegations, and not yet proven.)

Ahead of next month’s midterms, the Trump administration has escalated its fight with the Golden state, warning on Friday that local officials who process and tabulate ballots submitted by suspected noncitizen voters are putting themselves at risk of criminal investigation and potential prosecution.

That threat came just a day after the administration sued California to prevent it from counting mail-in ballots from people the federal government has allegedly identified as “likely” noncitizens until their immigration status can be confirmed.

As my colleague Sam Levinereported on Thursday, the suit came two days after the DoJ made a specious demand to the state. On Tuesday, the department sent election officials in Los Angeles county a thumb drive with a handwritten password purporting to contain the names of non-citizens registered to vote. The department asked officials to confirm the citizenship of anyone on the list and set aside ballots from those whose status could not be confirmed. It gave the state 48 hours – an extremely narrow window – to take action. It did not provide details on how it came up with the names on the list. In its lawsuit, filed in federal court in the central district of California, the justice department said it had provided the state with more than 20,000 names of likely noncitizens.

A reminder that noncitizen voting is extremely rare and the effort to investigate noncitizen voting ahead of the midterm elections has only turned up a handful of cases so far. Today’s DoJ press release, incidentally, reaffirms this, with 28 an absolutely minuscule number compared with the 174 million people who were registered to vote in the 2024 presidential election.

In Sam’s piece last week, he also highlighted that the Trump administration’s methodology for identifying non-citizens has come under scrutiny. Earlier this year, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) said there could be as many as 278,000 noncitizens on the rolls in four states, including more than 190,000 in California and about 16,000 in Nevada.

Officials at the DHS refused to share their methodology with Nevada election officials and later turned over just 185 names of people it had a strong belief were noncitizens. When Nevada officials investigated, they found every one of those people was a citizen.

With 15 days until the 3 November vote, Trump’s rally on the outskirts of Wichita aims to shore up the incumbent Republican senator RogerMarshall, who is locked in a dead heat with his Democratic opponent Adam Hamilton.

Trump won Kansas by 16 percentage points just two years ago, and the state has not elected a Democratic senator since 1932.

Yet analysts now rate it a “toss-up” and a key fundraising group for Republican senators has diverted money from North Carolina to defend a seat it once took for granted.

The University of Virginia’s Center for Politics told AFP it hesitated before moving deeply conservative Kansas into “toss-up” category – its most competitive – but was swayed by signs that a “full-on Republican rescue operation appears underway for Marshall”.

Control of Congress may hinge on such races, with Republicans holding slender majorities in both chambers, and Democrats needing a net gain of just four seats to capture the Senate.

Over the coming six days, Trump will follow Kansas with rallies in the Carolinas, Iowa, Florida, Georgia and Michigan. All seven states backed him in 2024, though his margins in Michigan, Georgia and North Carolina were narrow.

The schedule suggests the aim is less to win over undecided voters than to fire up loyal supporters, many of whom stay at home when he’s not on the ballot.

Two stops, North Carolina and Iowa, are damage control in GOP-held seats, and many national party officials have all but written off North Carolina.

Elsewhere the party is on the offensive, targeting a Democratic-held seat in Michigan, though it has struggled to make headway in Georgia.

Vice-President JD Vance is campaigning separately in NorthCarolina, Ohio and Nebraska. In North Carolina, sitting Republican representative, Jennifer Balkcom, is fending off a strong challenge from Democrat Jamie Ager.

The boss of a New York-based sticker company, whose staunch support for Donald Trump prompted him to record a Maga-themed rap album and call for an extension of Mount Rushmore to add the president’s face, is running for Congress.

Anthony Constantino, who began a brief pro boxing career at age 40, is the Republican candidate for GOP congresswoman Elise Stefanik’s seat in New York’s 21st district.

While Trump won the district by 21 points in 2024, and it has been represented by a Republican in the US House of Representatives since 2015, the party’s lead appears to have dwindled significantly as Constantino, 43, campaigns for voters to send him to Washington.

His lead in recent polls has fallen to single digits; one survey earlier this month put him four points behind. In September, the Cook Partisan Voting Index shifted the race from solid Republican to likely Republican. The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee added the district to its Red to Blue program in early October.

“I’m running for Congress. I built a company with over 1,000 people. Not an easy thing to do,” Constantino said, referring to his company, Sticker Mule. “I went pro in boxing at the age of 40. Not an easy thing to do.”

Mike Tyson “decided to endorse me”, he added in an interview, claiming the former boxing world champion had only endorsed four candidates. “It’s not easy to get endorsed by Mike Tyson.”
