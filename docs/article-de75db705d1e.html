Katie Zacharia to address media in first briefing as White House press secretary

The new press secretary is also a frequent commentator on conservative talkshows, often defending the Trump administration’s policies.

It is a date keeping US healthcare providers up at night: 1 January 2027.

As the midterm elections loom, everyone from hospital leaders and to patient advocates are focused on the New Year, when Republicans scheduled the most controversial elements of their healthcare reform law to go into effect.

Millions of Medicaid beneficiaries will be required, though a maze of government paperwork, to show they’re working 80 hours per month. And that’s just the marquee change.

“The costs to the family – and society as a whole – cannot be overstated,” said the American Academy of Pediatrics president, Dr Andrew Racine.

HR 1, also known as the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act” and signed into law by Donald Trump on 4 July 2025, cut roughly $1tn in federal spending from the healthcare industry – the biggest clawback of federal health spending ever. Paired with Republicans’ refusal to extend subsidies for plans in Obamacare marketplaces, the cuts are expected to result in about 17 million newly uninsured individuals through 2034.

Although the US has one of the most widely privatized healthcare systems in the world, nearly half of all health spending is paid for by government – whether federal, state or local, according to the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid (CMS). Nearly 40% of US health spending in 2024 was from just two programs: Medicare and Medicaid, the public health insurance programs for the aging and low-income. To put the scale of the $1tn cut into context, Medicaid spent $937bn in 2024, according to the CMS.

As a result, advocates, researchers, hospital leaders and doctors are all expecting more medical debt, more delayed care and more people losing coverage for which they technically qualify.

“There couldn’t be a clearer link,” said Dr Luke Messac, an emergency physician at Mass General Brigham and a historian who has written about medical debt. “We saw that when Medicaid eligibility was expanded the flow of medical debt rapidly declined and sustained that decline.

“The easiest way to reverse that trend – to make medical debt more of a problem – is to yank people off Medicaid and leave them uninsured and unprotected.”

The Kremlin said that it did not see any direct link between a deal on exporting Russian diesel to the United States and Moscow’s own stance on the Ukraine peace talks.

Analysis: Is punishing the poor Trump’s last hand to play?

There is a manic quality to Donald Trump’s desperate dash to do something, anything, ahead of November’s midterm elections to convince beleaguered Americans that he can make the pressures his policies have imposed on their livelihoods go away.

The US president offered to lower barriers against beef imports to limit surging meat prices, only to consider backtracking under pressure from the cow lobby. He offered to stop diesel exports, only to find oil companies werenot interested. He weakened fuel efficiency standards, claiming this would trim the price of a new car (and forgetting to point out it will also raise spending on gas.) In early October he started mailing $500 checks to nearly a million people touting his healthcare “wins”. Oh, and he promised adult Americans a $5,000 check if Republicans maintain control of Congress. That, too, will go nowhere.

In the face of such setbacks, maybe Trump felt he had to demonstrate he still can get something done. Still, it is somewhat counterintuitive that the administration, in the shape of Vice-President JD Vance, decided to broadcast the one initiative it did manage to bring to fruition: ejecting more than 750,000 enrollees from the Affordable Care Act (ACA) health insurance marketplaces, on grounds that they are not entitled to federal subsidies.

Trump could argue that the cuts fit within the administration’s promised commitment to restore fiscal discipline. But that part of the platform – which came as Scott Bessent, the treasury secretary, promised to trim the budget deficit to 3% of US gross domestic product – was never more than a rhetorical feint.

Six in 10 Americans think Trump is using presidency in unethical or illegal ways, new poll finds

Most Americans think Donald Trump is profiting off the presidency in unethical or illegal ways and is focused more on his wealth than helping people like them, a new AP-NORC poll finds.

As Trump’s family business pulls in record money from new ventures helped by his policies, the survey from the Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research finds that when it comes to allegations that Trump is profiting from the presidency, about six in 10 US adults believe he is either illegally profiting or doing something unethical but not illegal.

Only about two in 10 think the Republican president is doing nothing wrong, while a similar share say they don’t know enough to say.

At the same time, just over half of Americans say Trump has prioritized his personal financial interests “a lot” since returning to the White House, while only about one-quarter say he puts the same emphasis on the country as a whole. Few US adults, about one in 10, say he’s focused “a lot” on people like them.
