Trump touts lawsuit to block Senate race certification in Alaska: ‘I’m the one that said do it’ – live

2026-10-23 18:37

'I'm the one that said do it': Trump touts lawsuit to block Senate race certification in Alaska

Speaking to reporters in Florida, Donald Trump touted the justice department lawsuit that attempts to block the Senate race certification in Alaska, if the Trump-backed-Republican on the ballot loses.

“I’m the one that said do it,” Trump said, while standing alongside Ron DeSantis, the GOP governor of Florida.

Without evidence, the president claimed that Daniel J Sullivan, the retired schoolteacher and registered Republican running against the incumbent GOP senator Daniel S Sullivan, was “put up” by Democrats to siphon the vote against the Trump-endorsed candidate.

“I don’t think he ever voted before,” Trump said of the Republican challenger. “All of a sudden he decides to run for the Senate, and his name is Dan Sullivan. One is Daniel S. One is Daniel J. The Daniel J is the fake. And he wants to change his middle initial to Daniel S, but he said this has nothing to do with politics.”

James Talarico, the Democrats’ big hope for the US Senate in Texas, cast his vote in Austin on Friday and warned of voter suppression efforts by Republicans seeking to retain power.

“I was proud to cast my ballot today and I voted to send this movement that we’ve built in Texas to Washington DC,” said Talarico, 37, wearing a dark blue jacket, white open collar shirt and blue jeans. Predicting “something extraordinary, something historic” on 3 November, he added: “We’re gonna shock the nation and shock the world when we take back Texas.”

No Democrat has won statewide office in the Lone Star State since 1994 but an Emerson College Polling/ Nexstar Media survey released on Thursday found that 48% of voters support Talarico while 46% favour Republican Ken Paxton, the scandal-tainted state attorney general.

Talarico’s campaign has recruited 60,000 volunteers to knock on doors and make calls, while Paxton has received huge financial backing from tech billionaire Elon Musk and other outside groups.

Speaking to reporters in sunshine outside the Gustavo Garcia Recreation Center, where there was a steady flow of early voters, Talarico warned: “The people at the top are terrified of this movement we’re building in Texas and they’re doing everything they can to cling on to power. They have already poured more than $200m into our state to try to stop our movement. That is more than any Senate race in American history and more is on the way in the final days of this election.

“And they have made it harder to vote here in Texas. They have put more than a million people on the suspense list. They have mishandled voter files and they have closed polling locations across Texas, which means longer wait times, longer commute times to polling locations. And they’re hoping that that will mean lower voter turnout.”

Talarico, who did not take questions, nevertheless remained hopeful that “people power” would prevail, as reflected by historic turnout numbers so far. He did not mention Paxton by name but vowed: “We are going to make history in this state when we beat the most corrupt politician in Texas and take back our state.”

David Becker, executive director of the Center for Election Innovation & Research, said that the justice department’s lawsuit against Alaska – seeking to pause the US Senate race’s certification in the event that the Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan loses – is “an unprecedented intervention by the federal government”.

In a statement, Becker added that the administration had “absolutely no authority to tell Alaska when or how to certify the results of its Senate election”.

Attempting to intervene “at this late stage” is “entirely against department policy”, Becker said. “There is no legitimate enforcement of federal law here, and it raises serious concerns about political interference”.

Becker also said that he could not recall an administration “ever attempting to stop the certification of an election – and certainly not 11 days before polls close”.

What early voting numbers can tell us about the midterms so far

With just 11 days until the midterms, early voting data is rolling in nationwide. Democrats need to net four Senate seats and hold 218 House seats to reclaim a congressional majority. Here is where the numbers stand so far:

Turnout is surging in key battlegrounds: More than 22 million Americans have already voted in person or by mail. According to the University of Florida’s Election Lab, battleground states – including Michigan, Ohio, Georgia, Texas, Florida and North Carolina – have each passed 900,000 ballots cast as of 21 October. Turnout is being driven in part by widespread voter frustration, reflected in Donald Trump’s record-low approval ratings.

Democrats lead in early returns: Registered Democrats account for 45% of early ballots cast, compared with 30% from Republicans (tracking returned ballots, not candidate selection). The party gap is particularly pronounced in North Carolina, Kansas and Iowa. While Republicans traditionally favor election day voting, strategists worry this widened margin signals a “red undertow” – GOP voters staying home due to frustration over high gas prices, the conflict in Iran and sweeping tariffs.

Early votes are pacing well ahead of 2022: In Georgia, early votes already represent more than 37% of the total turnout from the 2022 midterms. Texas and North Carolina sit at 33%, while Florida is at 27%. We’re seeing similarly high paces recorded in Michigan and Ohio too.

Mail-in voting remains resilient: Almost 57m mail-in ballots have been requested or sent nationally – greater than the 52m requested in 2022, according to NBC News (using data from TargetSmart). Despite executive orders from the Trump administration attempting to restrict mail-in voting, which the supreme court struck down in September, voters continue to utilize the process at pre-pandemic rates.

The astonishing battle between the dueling Dan Sullivans in the Alaska US Senate race has resulted in the president weighing in to urge voters in the state, which he won by 13 points in 2024, to “VOTE FOR THE REAL DAN SULLIVAN”, during the August primary.

Trump has claimed, without evidence, that Daniel J Sullivan – who is challenging the two-term incumbent – is an “impostor”.

Meanwhile, many Republicans across the country have accused the former teacher, who lives in Petersburg – a town of about 3,000 people in the southern part of the state – of being a Democratic plant in place to cannibalize votes from Dan S Sullivan.

Pivoting back to the lawsuit filed by the Department of Justice against the state of Alaska.

The federal government argues in the complaint that “the sole purpose” of Dan J Sullivan’s of candidacy is to “benefit the Peltola campaign by reducing the number of votes for Senator Sullivan”.

As a result, “there is a substantial likelihood that a certification of the election will reflect an electoral process that lacked integrity”, it writes.

The administration is calling for there to be a pause in certifying the vote in Alaska “pending an investigation” to determine whether votes that are cast for Dan J Sullivan were actually meant to be for the Republican incumbent.

Voting rights experts say, however, the basis for the lawsuit is thin given the fact that any meaningful probe into voters’ preferences after they have voted would be practically impossible to conduct.

In June, the Alaska supreme court ruled that the Dan J Sullivan could remain on the ballot, despite the frustration it has caused the Trump administration and national Republicans hoping that the incumbent Sullivan can win against Peltola, a moderate Democrat, in their bitter fight for a Senate seat that could deliver Democrats a key victory in their path for control of the upper chamber of Congress.

Trump doubles down on support for widely condemned Ramaswamy ad, scolds reporter for asking about GOP backlash

As the president headed to Florida, for his fifth midterm campaign rally of the week, he doubled down on his support for Vivek Ramaswamy’s widely condemned ad that features the estranged mother of his Democratic opponent in the Ohio governor’s race, Amy Acton.

“If my mother said bad things about me, do you think the Democrats would put it up? I think so, but my mother thought I was wonderful,” Trump said in response to a reporter from NBC News.

A reminder that Acton, the public health official who is leading Ramaswamy in polls, was removed from her mother’s care when she was 12, after her stepfather was charged and indicted for sexually abusing Acton. Her mother, Donna Arthur, fled the state with her husband, before his trial, and remained married to him until his death in 2004.

While several Republicans have disavowed the ad, where Arthur calls her daughter “a liar”, and urged Ramaswamy’s campaign to take it down, Trump has encouraged the opposite. Outside the White House on Friday he scolded the NBC News reporter who followed up with questions about Acton’s childhood, and whether that changed the president’s perspective on the ad.

“You’re a horrible little reporter, aren’t you?” Trump said, while continuing to ignore the myriad questions around Arthur’s credibility and background.

“This is a person, a mother, a smart woman,” Trump added. “This has nothing to do with the stepfather or anything else … the mother of a person running for governor of one of the greatest states, where I set records, said that she’s a liar. Her daughter’s a liar.”

“Why wouldn’t you listen to a mother that says that? Why? Why would you miss NBC? Why wouldn’t you listen?” he said, while telling the reporter to be “quiet”.

The Trump administration’s targeting of Alaska’s US Senate race is particularly consequential as Democrats have been making waves in flipping the red-leaning state.

Alaska’s Democrats believe circumstances have aligned for them to win a slew of competitive elections in the 3 November midterms. The Senate seat currently held by two-term Republican Dan Sullivan is a top prize.

If Democratic candidate Mary Peltola were to win the election next month, she would be the first Democrat to win the seat in nearly two decades.

He also said that he had not spoken to Elon Musk regarding the issue, but suggested he would do so if the access wasn’t fixed.

Justice department files lawsuit to challenge results of Alaska Senate race

The Department of Justice has filed a lawsuit in federal court against Alaska’s and its election division, to block the certification of the state’s US Senate result if the Republican incumbent on the ballot doesn’t win.

The DoJ claims there could be “voter confusion” about the two GOP candidates on the ballot named Dan Sullivan – which might influence the race’s outcome in favor of the Democratic nominee, Mary Peltola.

A reminder, the Republican incumbent Dan S Sullivan will be competing against Peltola and another candidate named Daniel J Sullivan, a retired schoolteacher also running as a Republican.

The incumbent senator will appear on the 3 November ballot in Alaska with the words “(Registered Republican) Incumbent” next to his name to make it clearer for voters.

Since the Last Frontier uses ranked-choice voting in their elections, Alaskans will now rank the four candidates up for election in November. Their aim is to secure at least 50% of the vote in order to win the seat outright. However, Trump has routinely disparaged the voting method, calling it a “scam” and encouraging voters to support a ballot measure in the state to repeal it.
