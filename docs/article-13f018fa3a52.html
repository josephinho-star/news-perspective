Major California school district gives state officials the middle finger with bans on trans students

2026-11-22 18:07

A large California school district has thumbed its nose at Attorney General Rob Bonta by approving a sweeping policy barring transgender students from girls’ sports teams and restricting their access to bathrooms and locker rooms.

Chino Valley Unified School District’s board voted 4-1 Thursday to adopt the policy, refusing to heed a letter from the California Department of Justice urging trustees to reject the proposal or delay the vote over concerns that it conflicts with state protections.

The vote came during the final board meeting for outgoing president and former superintendent candidate Sonja Shaw, who spearheaded the proposal and has made restricting transgender participation in girls’ athletics a centerpiece of her conservative education agenda.

The Nov. 18 letter, signed by Deputy Attorney General Edward Nugent, specifically warned the district’s proposed rules conflict with California Education Code Section 221.5(f), which guarantees students the right to participate in sex-segregated school programs.

But Shaw wasn’t having it. “How about no,” Shaw wrote in a post on X directed at Bonta.

“Federal law is supreme. Look it up. Aren’t you a lawyer or do you just play one on X? I will never back down from protecting our daughters. You picked the wrong fight with this mom. I will fight until Title IX is followed and our daughters are protected.”

Shaw also dramatically ripped up the attorney general’s office letter during Thursday’s meeting, according to reports.

The newly adopted Board Policy 5145.72, titled Fairness in Girls’ Athletics and Privacy in Sex-Based Facilities, defines sex as a person’s biological sex — male or female — determined by reproductive biology and genetics at birth.

Under the measure, only female students will be eligible for girls’ interscholastic, intramural and club sports teams, regardless of hormone levels, puberty blockers or other medical treatments.

The policy also requires multi-occupancy restrooms, locker rooms, changing areas and showers on district property to be designated exclusively for males or females.

Students must use facilities corresponding to their sex, while overnight school trips must assign sleeping quarters according to the same classification.

The restrictions extend beyond athletics, making the district one of the latest California school systems to challenge the state’s protections for transgender students.

Shaw defended the restrictions as basic protections for female students, arguing that the measures were necessary to safeguard their privacy and safety.

“These are common-sense measures: no boys in girls’ sports, no boys sharing girls’ locker rooms or using their showers, and no boys and girls sharing hotel rooms on school trips. They protect girls’ privacy and safety in the most basic ways,” she said.

The policy does provide alternatives for students seeking additional privacy. Any student can request a single-user restroom, separate changing time or another reasonable accommodation without having to provide a reason.

It also allows students who are ineligible for girls’ teams to try out for open or coed teams, where available.

The district says students will not be required to undergo additional medical examinations or inspections to establish their eligibility.

The proposal further protects students and staff from punishment for declining to compete against or share a multi-occupancy facility with someone of the opposite sex.

The board’s lone dissenting vote came from Trustee Andrew Cruz, who had attempted to postpone consideration of the measure so incoming board members could weigh in before it was adopted.

Shaw criticized Cruz’s opposition, calling his actions “cowardly.”

The dispute centers on competing interpretations of federal and state law, with the district relying heavily on a recent Supreme Court decision to justify its restrictions.

In its letter, the California DOJ took issue with the district’s interpretation of the high court’s June 30 ruling in West Virginia v. B.P.J. and Little v. Hecox.

The Supreme Court held that Title IX permits states and schools to restrict girls’ and women’s sports teams to biological females without violating federal law or the Equal Protection Clause.

But the court did not rule that every school district must adopt such restrictions, nor did it decide whether Title IX requires schools to exclude transgender students from girls’ teams.

Nugent cited that distinction in arguing that the ruling does not invalidate California’s existing protections.

He also pointed to the Ninth Circuit’s 2020 decision in Parents for Privacy v. Barr, which found that Title IX permits schools to maintain sex-segregated bathrooms and locker rooms but does not require them to exclude transgender students from facilities consistent with their gender identity.

The DOJ warned that nothing in the Supreme Court’s decision authorized local school boards to disregard those state requirements.

The department urged the district to reject the proposal or, at minimum, postpone the vote to discuss how the new rules could comply with California law.

The board moved forward anyway.

The vote marked the culmination of a yearslong campaign by Shaw, a conservative Republican whose tenure at Chino Valley Unified has been marked by battles over transgender student rights, parental notification policies and restrictions on school library books.

Shaw was elected to the school board in 2022 and became one of California’s most prominent conservative education activists, building a statewide profile through her opposition to transgender participation in girls’ sports and policies protecting LGBTQ+ students.

She also championed a controversial policy requiring school employees to notify parents when their children expressed a gender identity different from their sex assigned at birth, as well as rules allowing parents to challenge library books they considered sexually explicit.

Shaw took that agenda statewide this year, running for California superintendent of public instruction on a platform emphasizing parental rights, traditional academic standards and protecting girls’ sports.

In her campaign, she argued that California schools needed to restore academic performance while protecting female athletes and pushing back against what she characterized as political interference in classrooms.

She secured the Republican Party’s endorsement and finished first in the June primary with 22.6% of the vote, narrowly ahead of Democrat Richard Barrera, who received 20.3%.

But Barrera defeated Shaw in the November general election, winning the superintendent’s race and leaving Shaw without either the statewide office she sought or another term on the Chino Valley school board.

Thursday’s meeting was her final one as board president and her last as an elected district official.

Despite the defeat, Shaw signaled that her fight over transgender student participation was far from over.

“Bonta may have the power and weaponize the state against us. But I have something that won’t be intimidated: a mother’s determination to protect children,” she said.
