Turkey’s defense industry offers lessons for South Africa, researcher says

2026-09-21 14:44

South Africa can learn from Turkey’s success in linking research, military demand and weapons production to reduce its reliance on foreign technology, a researcher at South Africa’s Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) has said.

Mphahlela James Thaba, who manages the CSIR’s technology for special operations program, spoke at the Africa Aerospace and Defence (AAD) Conference in Pretoria on September 17. The conference was part of the AAD 2026 exhibition, which ended Sunday.

Thaba said Turkey’s leading defense companies owed their growth to the connections among researchers, industry and the military, rather than individual corporate achievements alone.

“Sustained research and engineering, predictable domestic demand, targeted technology development and operational feedback created the conditions in which companies such as Baykar, ASELSAN, TAI and ROKETSAN could grow,” he said.

Baykar makes the Bayraktar TB2 and Akıncı armed drones. ASELSAN develops defense electronics, including radar, communications and electronic warfare systems. Turkish Aerospace Industries (TAI) develops aircraft, helicopters and drones, including the KAAN fighter jet, while ROKETSAN produces missiles, rockets and air-defense systems.

South Africa should not copy Turkey’s industrial structure, Thaba said, but examine how it connected research and military needs with the development, testing and production of equipment.

Turkey’s expanding defense industry

Ankara has promoted domestic arms production over the past two decades to reduce its dependence on foreign suppliers. Turkish officials say domestic production now meets more than 80 percent of the country’s defense needs, up from about 20 percent in the early 2000s.

The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) ranked Turkey as the world’s 11th-largest supplier of major arms in 2021–2025, accounting for 1.8 percent of global exports. Its export volume increased by 122 percent compared with 2016–2020.

ASELSAN signed more than $2 billion in new export contracts in 2025 and ended the year with $20.4 billion in outstanding orders, according to its financial results.

Armed drones have also helped expand Ankara’s influence in Africa, where governments facing insurgencies and internal conflicts have bought Baykar aircraft.

Those sales have attracted scrutiny. Human rights groups and analysts have criticized civilian harm linked to drone warfare in African conflicts and the lack of human rights conditions on Turkish arms exports.

Turkish companies formed the third-largest national contingent at AAD 2026, after South Africa and China, Turkey’s ambassador in Pretoria, Kezban Nilvana Darama Yıldırımgeç, told the state-run Anadolu news agency.

Deputy Defense Minister Salih Ayhan led the Turkish delegation. Exhibitors included ASELSAN, TAI and STM, which develops naval and other military systems.

Other participants were state-owned weapons and ammunition manufacturer MKE, gunmaker Sarsılmaz, armored vehicle manufacturer BMC and HAVELSAN, which develops defense software and military simulators.

The exhibition ran from September 16 to 20 at Waterkloof Air Force Base.

Thaba said building equipment locally did not necessarily give a country control over the technology its armed forces needed.

“We need to look beyond just production. We must examine the innovation system that makes an industrial capacity possible or more sustainable,” he said.

Dependence on foreign suppliers could persist if they retained control over designs, software, intellectual property, critical components or permission to modify equipment, he said.

He described the work needed to turn research into usable equipment — developing prototypes, testing them and making their components work together — as the “missing middle” between research and production.

“If we are not invested here, we create a disconnect between the research and the industry,” he said.

New technology must be tested against military requirements before equipment can be manufactured at scale, he added.

South Africa’s existing strengths

South Africa already had aerospace engineering, flight-testing and technical expertise on which to build, Thaba said.

The country exports major weapons on a smaller scale than Turkey. SIPRI ranked South Africa 18th among suppliers in 2021–2025, with a 0.4 percent share of global exports. Its export volume fell by 7.2 percent compared with 2016–2020. Its largest recipients were the United Arab Emirates, Singapore and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

South African government figures show authorities approved 582 export permits worth 10.1 billion rand (about $550 million) in 2025, compared with 3.6 billion rand in 2024. These figures cover a broader range of military products and represent authorized export values, not completed deliveries or SIPRI’s measure of major arms transfers.

The Department of Trade, Industry and Competition also warned that underfunding of South Africa’s armed forces limited purchases of new equipment and support for research and development.

Thaba cited the A-Darter air-to-air missile program, jointly developed with Brazil, as an example of how international cooperation could build expertise in guidance systems, software and integrating different technologies.

“The lesson is not that South Africa can do everything,” he said. “It is that the capability accumulated through innovation creates industrial options.”

During his presentation, Thaba also cited South African defense company Paramount as an example of how military needs could guide equipment design, testing and production, with further improvements made after manufacturing began.

Countries did not need to manufacture every component themselves, Thaba said. They needed enough control over strategically important equipment to modify it as military needs changed.

“The objective is not to eliminate dependence. The objective is to manage strategic dependence,” he said.
