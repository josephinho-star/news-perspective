Erdoğan’s LGBTQ crackdown ‘first step of election campaign’: activist

2026-09-20 19:58

Already jailed twice for his activism, Yıldız Tar has no doubt: A sweeping operation targeting Turkey’s LGBTQ community marks “the start of the election campaign” for President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan ahead of an expected reelection bid in 2028.

Speaking at a cafe in the capital, Ankara, the 36-year-old member of Kaos GL, one of Turkey’s leading LGBTQ rights organizations, spoke to AFP just days after authorities began a major crackdown on activists and members of the community.

The operation began last weekend, with dozens of social media accounts blocked, followed by overnight raids on bars, nightclubs, NGOs and the homes of LGBTQ people in what the government said was a move to protect “children and the institution of the family.”

For Tar, the latest crackdown by Erdoğan’s Islamo-conservative government, which has long demonized the LGBTQ community as a “deviant movement” of “perverts,” is consistent with a certain political logic.

“We are like the laboratory of the government. They exercise their power on us first, and if they succeed, they export it to the other parts of the society,” he said in an interview conducted in English.

“It was always like that… So this can be the sign of a bigger and a much worse era… beginning,” he explained, echoing long-standing concerns about the government’s increasingly authoritarian bent.

For Tar, it was no coincidence that the date of the next election was announced just days later. If all legal procedures are completed, the vote will take place on April 16, 2028.

That date is a month before the formal end of Erdoğan’s term in office, allowing him to sidestep a constitutional requirement that would otherwise bar him from seeking another term.

“This operation was related to the election, it was like a start of the election campaign. And probably the tension will rise,” said Tar, who is also editor-in-chief of Kaos GL’s online news site.

Justice Minister Akın Gürlek, who has vowed to press ahead with the campaign to protect traditional family values, appears likely to play a key role in the run-up to the vote.

“The minister of justice will be one of the vocal figures in here. He is not only the minister of justice, he also controls other ministries too. He is a super minister,” Tar said.

Tar said he believed authorities could arrest him at any moment on charges such as obscenity, aiding prostitution or even “financial ties to foreign countries,” accusations that have been used against some of those detained in the operation.

Although same-sex relations are legal in Turkey, hostility toward LGBTQ people remains widespread, with homophobia evident even at the highest levels of government.

The community has experienced threats, abuse, violence and imprisonment at the hands of a government that wants to silence it, he said.

“They are very angry that every year they are banning LGBTI marches, but we are still in the street. They detain us, they arrest us, but we are still in the streets, we are still in the parliament, we are still in other parts of society.

“They want to get rid of us, so we’re not seen.”

Police rounded up demonstrators in İstanbul, Ankara and İzmir, with more than 100 people detained in İstanbul before being released 36 hours later.

In a polarized society split between the government’s Islamo-conservative stance and those favoring more secular and liberal traditions, issues of sexuality and gender have become highly politicized.

Turkey’s LGBTQ community has faced increasing pressure since the government declared 2026 the start of its “Decade of the Family.”

“The government is trying to shape society in their own vision, to make it more homophobic,” Tar said, clearly concerned that it could fuel a surge in anti-LGBTQ violence.

He also lamented what he described as a lack of support from opposition parties, noting that the main opposition New Party issued only a muted response.

Although the pro-Kurdish opposition Peoples’ Equality and Democracy Party (DEM Party) reacted more quickly, it was still “not enough,” he said, arguing that LGBTQ groups could largely rely only on themselves.

Despite the government’s accusations, activists simply wanted to live peacefully, he said.

In response to officials’ claims that they had a hidden agenda, Tar said: “Our hidden agenda is to not get murdered!”

“We were born in this country, we want to have a peaceful life… to work in peace, have a home, have friends, to be respected. We want dignity. What’s wrong with that?”
