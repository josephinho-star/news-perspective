How many Neets are not claiming benefits, and who are they?

Regarding Neets, does the figure of a million just refer to those claiming benefits or does it also include those not claiming benefits? If it doesn’t include those not claiming benefits, what is the true Neet figure?

Is the government only concerned about Neets who claim benefits?

Alan Milburn is definitely interested in both categories of Neet. The Treasury is probably more worried about just the Neets who claim benefits, but they should be worried about the other Neets too because if they don’t get into the labour market, they will end up claiming benefits one day.

The interim Milburn report said there were 957,000 Neets aged 16 to 24 at the end of 2025 and this figure includes people who claim out-of-work benefits and people who don’t.

The report describes the ones who don’t as “hidden Neets” and it says 44% of young Neets are in this category. It uses figures from this Resolution Foundation report exploring the problem.

The RF report includes this chart.

And this is what the RF says about the figures.

What figure 4 also shows is that there are consistently hundreds of thousands of young Neets who are not claiming out-of-work benefits. In 2024, we estimate that 410,000 – 44% of all Neets – were not in receipt of an out-of-work benefit. This is a slightly lower proportion than in 2019, and we estimate that the number of Neets who are claiming out-of-work benefits has risen more quickly since 2019 than the number who are Neet and not claiming (with increases of 24% and 13% respectively).

The fact that welfare spending is lower than it could be if all Neets claimed an out-of-work benefit may seem like good news to some policy makers, but it also highlights a policy dilemma – how can we reach and support Neets who have little incentive or opportunity to engage with the state?

And this chart, from an analytical annex published alongside the interim Milburn report, gives a bit more information about who the hidden Neets are.

On the Today programme this morning, the Labour MP Bell Ribeiro-Addy, a prominent leftwinger, indicated she might vote against legislation to implement the Alan Milburn proposals. She said she was concerned by the idea that the cuts would apply to people already getting sickness and disability benefits, and she said it appeared to be a breach of a promise given last year that people already getting Pip (the personal independence payment) would not lose it.

I just don’t think that you get a disabled young person into work by taking away the money that helps them to get into work.

The cuts seem to land on young disabled people, and many of them are in college already or already in work. So I don’t see how you fix the first by punishing the second.

Milburn says proposed welfare cuts won't be 'pain-free' - but says they'd be offset by higher employment support spending

Here are more quotes from what Alan Milburntold the Times about the proposals in his youth unemployment report, which is being published next week.

Milburn said he concluded fundamental change was needed. He said:

The whole system needs to be changed, because the system is geared to keeping people inactive when, for young people in particular, it’s all about how can you make them active, get them earning, learning, so that they can rely on a wage rather than on a benefit. So, this is not about cutting one benefit, it’s about rebuilding the system from scratch.

He said that he decided existing claimants should be covered by the changes, not just new changes.

It’s a big rewriting of the welfare system. And obviously one of the conundrums was, ‘Should this apply to only new claimants?’

I came to the conclusion that wouldn’t be right, not least because you’ve got hundreds of thousands of young people stuck in a benefit system that is trapping them in poverty, giving them no prospect of prosperity or a job.

That conclusion makes it difficult politically because it means people are getting a current entitlement and that entitlement is going to change. But it needs to.

It is relatively normal for welfare changes to cover existing claimants as well as new ones. But governments seeking to cut benefits do often restrict those changes to new claimants because people are much more likely to complain about losing something they already get than about losing something they might notionally get in the future.

Milburn admitted this meant the plans would not be “pain-free”.

I’m not saying that any of this will be easy or that it will be pain-free because it won’t. You know, candidly, it could mean that some people will get less financial support at the beginning of their benefit claim than under the current system.

He said he wanted the system to prioritise improving people’s life chances in the long term over easing their financial hardship in the short term.

Actually for young people there’s a different debate that we’ve got to resolve, which is whether the priority is their longer-term life chances or just their immediate shorter-term finances.

I think what the current system does, it prioritises the latter over the former. I believe that’s the wrong choice and it needs to be the other way around.

The approach I’ve taken on this is to try to define what would give young people the best chance of building a good life, not just over a year or two, but over the course of their lifetimes.

He said, alongside the cuts to benefits he is proposing (see 11.56am), he wanted to improve employment support for young people.

We need to make sure people have the opportunities to get an apprenticeship, a job, a work placement, a pre-apprenticeship, a college place.

The menu of opportunities has got to increase. They’ll have a personal adviser, they’ll be coached all the way through. The adviser will stick with them until they have found a job or an apprenticeship or college place.

They will get immediate access to support if that’s what they need, if that’s the thing that is holding them back from being in education or training.

He admitted that offering better employment support and mental health support would mean his plans would initially cost money. According to the Times, he did not did not deny claims the whole package could cost £6bn. But, in the long run, there would be savings, he said.

Obviously this costs money. And frankly it’s a better use of money, both from the point of view of the young person and the taxpayer, to spend that money getting them into work rather than spending that money keeping them on benefits.

This is the sort of fatuous thing about this debate. You’ve got to invest upfront in order to get savings out of the back end. Cost-free is fatuous. So we’ve got to be candid about that. It will need significant investment.

When it was put to him that it could take a decade for the savings to materialise, he replied:

To get a return on investment will take time … the only way of cutting the benefits bill sustainably is by getting young people into work, paying their taxes and being less reliant on benefits. Unless you are prepared to put the investment upfront it will never be possible.

He said he was “confident” that Andy Burnham was full-square behind the changes that are needed.”

What changes to youth sickness and disability benefits will Milburn propose?

The Alan Milburn report on youth unemployment, or Neets (young people not in education, employment or training), is expected to lead to the biggest overhaul of the welfare system for at least a decade (when Iain Duncan Smith merged six different benefits into universal credit). Arguably, it could be even bigger.

It is certainly the most heavily-trailed benefits reform plan in recent history. Milburn, a former Labour cabinet minister, began pitch-rolling with the publication of an interim report in May. Unusually for a Westminster document, it was a compelling read, and was widely praised at the time. Since then, barely a week has gone by without Milburn dropping further hints about what the final report will say. Milburn says he has government backing for what he is recommending and he wants this to be a blueprint for legislation, not a set of recommendations ultimately ignored by ministers.

We still won’t get the final report until next week, but in his interview in the Times today he has given the fullest account yet of what he will propose.

This is how Steven Swinford sums up the plans in his write-up of the interview.

Under [Milburn’s] proposals, young people aged 18 to 24 would be barred from claiming the two main sickness and disability benefits: the health element of universal credit and personal independence payments (Pip).

They would instead be given a new youth allowance, which would be paid at the standard rate of universal credit, while billions would be invested in one-to-one employment support to get them into work.

Many of those who currently receive Pip would be moved on to a new benefit called the “additional cost allowance”. It would be paid to those with “significant, well-established and ongoing” costs relating to their disability.

Covering assistance, mobility, specialist transport or daily living support, the allowance would go to a smaller cohort than the 453,000 young people currently claiming Pip. It would not be means-tested and people would receive the money “regardless” of whether they were in work or not.

The most severely disabled individuals — those with “profound” and “lifelong” conditions who can never be expected to work — would be given a new benefit. It would be paid at a higher rate than current disability benefits and would have no waiting period.

More than 3,500 patients a day getting corridor care in English hospitals, NHS figures show

More than 3,500 patients a day received care in hospital corridors or makeshift treatment areas in England last month, as other figures show the NHS is already facing growing pressures before winter has arrived, the Press Association reports. PA says:

The waiting list for hospital treatment has also risen to its highest level in nearly a year.

Officials said this autumn has been the “busiest ever” for the health service, with record numbers of people attending A&E.

The latest performance data from NHS England shows an average of 3,558 patients received corridor care each day in October, with 2,718 in A&E departments and 840 in other hospital areas.

This is up from a revised average of 3,310 patients a day in September and the highest since figures for corridor care started being collected in May.

Prof Nicola Ranger, general secretary and chief executive of the Royal College of Nursing, called the figures “a mark of shame”.

She said: “Nursing staff will be filled with dread thinking of just how bad things will get as temperatures drop.

“The data shows once again that corridor care is occurring not just in emergency departments but across hospitals. This is an incredibly dangerous situation with much colder temperatures on the horizon.”

Liberal Democrat health spokeswoman Helen Morgan claimed the “true scale of the crisis is likely far worse”.

She added: “We are hearing troubling reports of trusts reclassifying spaces to keep the numbers down.

“Hospitals are under pressure to hit targets rather than put patients first. Patients on chairs in waiting rooms or in converted cupboards deserve to be counted.”

In emergency departments, some 74.0% of patients were seen within four hours in October, down slightly from 74.4% in September.

Elsewhere, an estimated 7.38m treatments were waiting to be carried out at the end of September, relating to 6.24m patients.

This is up slightly from 7.37m treatments at the end of August and is the highest since the list stood at 7.40m treatments in October 2025.

The figure for patients is broadly unchanged month on month, but is also at a level that was previously seen in October 2025.

Under-25s could be moved off key sickness and disability benefits in welfare shake-up

Good morning and welcome to the UK politics live blog. Young people could lose entitlement to the main sickness and disability benefits under a significant overhaul of the welfare system, according to proposals expected to emerge from the government-commissioned review into young people and work.

Led by former health secretary Alan Milburn, the review is examining the barriers preventing young people from entering the workforce and how to address the growing number classified as not in education, employment or training (Neet), which currently stands at about 981,000 across the UK.

The review, which has been under way for a year, is expected to include proposals that would remove under-25s’ entitlement to the health element of universal credit and personal independence payment (Pip), replacing them with a standard-rate youth allowance. Under the plans, first reported by the Times, those with additional needs arising from a disability would receive a separate, non-means-tested allowance, while those with severe disabilities and who are not capable of working would qualify for a higher rate.

Speaking to the newspaper, Milburn acknowledged that the changes would not be “pain-free”, admitting some young people could initially receive less financial support than under the current system. The final review is due to be published on Tuesday.
