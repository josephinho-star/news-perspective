How much of the sharp fall in UK immigration is due to Labour’s policies?

2026-11-26 19:38

David Cameron was the leader of the opposition when he made a comment on overall migration that was used to interrogate every Tory leader for the next 14 years.

Asked in January 2010 on the BBC by the interviewer Andrew Marr to address comments made by the archbishop of Canterbury calling for the population to remain under 70 million, Cameron said net immigration levels of 200,000 a year were “too much”.

“We would like to see net immigration in the tens of thousands rather than the hundreds of thousands,” he said.

It became a full-blown promise in Cameron’s Tory manifesto that year; and it was there again when he won the 2015 general election, alongside a promise to hold an EU referendum. And the promise was repeated by Theresa May in 2017 as she narrowly defeated the then Labour leader, Jeremy Corbyn.

Both Tory leaders struggled with the actual net migration numbers, which fluctuated between 195,000 and 303,000.

Boris Johnson was pressed on whether he would revive Cameron’s pledge, and his 2019 manifesto said “overall numbers will come down”. Rishi Sunak, five years later, said he would like to see the figures get to tens of thousands but his manifesto promised that “numbers would fall every year”.

It has led to some crowing by Labour – and frustration from the Tories – that numbers have finally dipped below six figures under the current government.

Migration analysts said the Tories struggled throughout the 2010s because the UK was a full member of the EU and had no way of controlling inward EU migration.

EU citizens made up a majority of immigration and net migration in the run-up to the 2016 EU referendum. Since 2022, however, EU net migration has been negative.

As the UK emerged from the Covid pandemic, Johnson opened the doors to non-EU migration and to Ukrainians and people from Hong Kong.

Madeleine Sumption, the director of the Migration Observatory at the University of Oxford, said: “The previous government introduced some relatively liberal policies, including visa schemes for care workers, mid-skilled workers, Ukrainians and Hongkongers, and post-study work visas for international students.”

Several conditions that enabled Labour to achieve net migration of below 100,000 did not exist for most of the time that the Tories were in power, Sumption said.

First, the UK was part of the EU for most of the Tories’ time in office and so it had little control over inward EU migration.

Second, the Office for National Statistics still relied on an older methodology for estimating migration of British citizens under the Conservatives. The new method, introduced last year, has taken “tens of thousands off the net migration figure”, she said.

Third, high migration under the previous government – and particularly under Johnson – has left a temporary legacy of high numbers of non-EU citizens leaving the UK under Labour.

“Non-EU emigration is around 130,000 higher than it was for most of the 2010s, and that is only possible because so many non-EU citizens arrived a few years ago,” she said.

The Tories throughout the 2010s were also under pressure from business and their own party to ensure that they could expand the economy and offer tax cuts, a former cabinet minister said.

“Migration was an issue then but not top of the agenda as it has been for the last few years,” they said.

The Tories claimed credit for the fall on Thursday, after introducing immigration restrictions in the last parliament. But Labour insisted it had been the one to “restore control” at the borders after net migration came close to 1 million under the Tories.

Sumption said the net migration figure could fall further but may also be expected to rise again.

“The lower figures we see today are partly a result of government policy but partly the result of methodological changes and an increase in emigration that is expected to be temporary. The numbers could still have further to fall but there is no reason to assume they will remain below 100,000 ffor ever, even if policy remains the same,” she said.
