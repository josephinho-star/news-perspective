California college coffee shop’s first manager scorches woke students after massive flag fight

2026-11-22 17:20

A Jewish woman who was the first manager of a student-run coffee shop at Scripps College has scorched her successors following a massive fight over the Israeli and US flags.

Youths working at The Motley Coffeehouse in Claremont kicked off when the school told them to erect US and Israel flags the same size as a large Palestinian one they had hung on the wall.

The kids decided to cave in and instead removed the Palestinian flag after they claimed 90% of their staff voted around Nov. 13.

Now, Jewish alumna Catherine Schuster, 72, has said she will never set foot on campus again after what she considers blatant antisemitism.

Schuster, who graduated in 1978 from Scripps College, told the California Post: “I think it’s incredibly illiberal. Either you’re an inclusive space, and that means everybody, or you’re not.

“Scripps College owns the coffeehouse, and if you’re not willing to be inclusive, you shouldn’t be allowed to work there.”

Schuster is all too familiar with the coffee shop. Back in the mid-1970s, she became the first manager of the Motley, spending a year and a half working there.

She said she bought an espresso machine, found a local bakery so the shop would have pastries and helped open the venue.

The original Motley, located in Balch Hall, had what Schuster remembers as a collection of hand-me-down tables and chairs, possibly a poster with a line from Shakespeare that inspired the café’s name — “Motley to the view” — and little else on the walls.

“There was no big political stuff at Scripps, at least from my perspective, from what I remember, people were pretty serious about studying and being undergraduates,” Schuster said.

Professors held office hours there, and students from across the Claremont Colleges stopped by. On later visits she would introduce herself to the baristas as the coffeehouse’s founding manager.

But for Schuster, the college environment she remembers has changed for the worse. She claimed Scripps has failed to ensure campus spaces remain welcoming to Jewish students.

She said she raised her concerns in a Dec. 18, 2025, letter to the college president and trustees but has yet to receive a response.

“People get to believe whatever they want to believe,” Schuster said. “But I don’t think you get to shut other people out of the discussion.”

Regarding the coffeehouse dispute, the Motley’s student co-heads told the Claremont Colleges newspaper The Student Life that administrators had exercised their authority over the café only when Palestinian speech was at issue.

Schuster, however, said she viewed the dispute as part of a broader concern about whether Jewish students could fully participate in a space intended to serve the entire campus community.

“If this were against Hispanic students or black students, this would never fly,” Schuster said.

The flag controversy was the latest in a series of incidents that had already prompted Schuster to raise concerns with the college.

She said she was moved to act after reading about a 2025 federal civil rights complaint filed with the U.S. Department of Education by the Louis D. Brandeis Center for Human Rights Under Law, the Anti-Defamation League and the law firm Arnold & Porter.

The complaint accused Scripps of failing to adequately protect Jewish and Israeli students from harassment and discrimination, alleging the college had allowed a hostile campus environment to develop without taking sufficient action to address it.

The allegations centered on the college’s response to antisemitism, pro-Palestinian demonstrations and incidents in which Jewish students allegedly felt excluded or targeted because of their identities or their support for Israel.

Among the incidents Schuster cited in her letter was an allegation that a student had been told to remove her Star of David necklace.

She also pointed to allegations that the Motley displayed a Palestinian flag while declining to hang an Israeli flag or host a vigil for victims of the Oct. 7, 2023, Hamas-led attack on Israel.

The complaint also alleged that while students from neighboring Pomona College faced expulsions and other disciplinary measures following pro-Palestinian protests, Scripps students accused of participating in demonstrations involving vandalism and classroom disruptions did not.

Schuster said Scripps should have taken stronger disciplinary action against students who violated campus rules.

Schuster also told The Post about an account she had read about a Jewish student who was allegedly left alone in a classroom after a professor and other students departed to attend a pro-Palestinian protest.

Schuster said the professor who left class to join the demonstration should have faced serious disciplinary consequences. “You do not leave a student behind in a classroom,” she said.

In her letter, Schuster quoted the college’s mission statement and its policy on demonstrations, arguing that administrators had failed to enforce their own rules when protests disrupted classes or infringed on students’ rights.

She also called for the suspension of coffeehouse workers she held responsible for displaying the Palestinian flag and refusing to host an event in support of the Israeli hostages.

“I have always been proud of my Scripps education,” she wrote. “While I have donated small amounts to Scripps over the years, I am now in a position to make much more substantial gifts to my alma mater.”

She went on to say she had “no intention of ever making another contribution.”

Schuster remembers Scripps as academically rigorous and largely politically neutral during her time there.

She recalled Angela Davis speaking on campus but said most of her attention was focused on her comparative literature classes.

Being Jewish, she said, was “simply a non-issue.” Now, Schuster says she has no plans to return to Scripps.

When the California Post asked what it would take for her to reconsider, Schuster said there was something the college could do.

“I think they’d have to come out and say we made a mistake,” she said. Scripps College did not immediately respond to requests for comment.
