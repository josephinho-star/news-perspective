Is an extended car warranty worth it?

2026-09-04 11:11

Auto financing and car insurance rates have continued to climb in recent years. And the cost of basic repairs has also been steadily rising. The median repair bill in 2026 is about $725, according to industry data tracked by Empire Auto Protect, with more than half costing $1,000.

Engine replacement ($3,000 to $7,500) and transmission repair ($1,800 to $6,500) were the two costliest fixes.

An extended car warranty, also known as a vehicle service contract, can cover work on a variety of parts after your manufacturer's warranty expires, including the engine, transmission, drivetrain, electrical system and air conditioning.

But not all plans have the same coverage or terms. Here’s what to look for when you're shopping for an extended car warranty and how to decide it’s the right option for you.

An extended car warranty is a service contract that pays for specified repairs after a manufacturer's warranty expires or if they’re not covered by the original warranty. These are repairs needed because of wear and tear, not due to a collision.

Extended warranties can be offered by the manufacturer, the dealership or a third-party provider. Coverage typically lasts for a specified number of years or miles, whichever comes first. Some plans cover a broad range of mechanical and electrical components, while others cover only specific parts or systems, such as the powertrain. Read the contract carefully — your warranty will only cover the components and repairs specifically described in your agreement.

The cost of an extended warranty depends on the provider, plan, vehicle's make and model, age, mileage and the level of coverage you select. Among the providers we reviewed, quoted monthly costs ranged from $40 to $200.

There is typically also a deductible when you file a claim, which can range from $100 to $500.

When comparing plans, look at the total cost of the contract, not just the monthly payment. And keep in mind that the price can often be negotiated. If you finance the warranty through your auto loan, you'll be paying interest on the cost of the service contract as well.

You can pay for an extended warranty from a third-party company upfront, often at a lower price, although most warranty providers offer monthly payment options.

Coverage varies by provider and contract, but extended warranties generally fall into five categories:

- Named-component warranty: Covers only the parts or systems specifically listed in the contract. It’s usually the cheapest option, but it’s also the most limited.

- Drivetrain warranty: Covers components that transfer power from the engine to the wheels, such as the transmission, driveshaft and axles. Depending on the contract, the engine may not be covered.

- Powertrain warranty: Covers major components, such as the engine and transmission. It can be a more affordable option than a bumper-to-bumper contract.

- Bumper-to-bumper warranty: Covers most mechanical and electrical systems, from the engine, transmission and drive axle to the electronics, heating and AC, steering and suspension. Despite the name, these contracts still have exclusions. Many plans do, however, include roadside assistance, rental car reimbursement and travel interruption protection.

- Wrap policy: This extends certain coverage after a manufacturer's bumper-to-bumper warranty expires. These plans can be useful when a powertrain warranty remains in effect but broader coverage is ending.

Most extended warranties don't cover brake pads, wiper blades, tires or batteries. Cosmetic repairs, oil changes and tune-ups are also usually excluded, as are pre-existing issues and damage resulting from accidents, misuse, neglect or improper maintenance.

Don't assume a repair is covered simply because it involves a component listed in the plan. Read the exclusions, limitations and claim requirements in the contract before purchasing.

Consider the benefits and drawbacks before getting a service contract.

- Covers eligible mechanical repairs after the manufacturer's warranty expires.

- Can make unexpected repair costs more predictable.

- May include roadside assistance, rental-car reimbursement and other benefits.

- Some contracts let you choose your repair shop.

- A transferable contract may add value when you sell the vehicle.

- You could pay more for the contract than you receive in covered repairs.

- Pre-existing conditions and routine maintenance are generally excluded.

- Claims may require deductibles, prior authorization and other restrictions.

- Coverage could overlap with the manufacturer's warranty, meaning you're paying for protection you don't yet need.

A manufacturer's warranty typically comes with a new vehicle and covers certain defects or failures for a specified period or mileage.

An extended warranty or vehicle service contract is optional and costs extra. It may extend protection beyond the manufacturer's warranty or cover components the original warranty doesn’t.

Before buying an extended warranty, check how much time and mileage remain on your manufacturer's warranty to avoid paying for overlapping coverage.

Mechanical breakdown insurance provides similar benefits to an extended warranty, but is offered by your car insurance company. An extended warranty or vehicle service contract is typically sold by a manufacturer, dealership or third-party provider. The availability and terms of mechanical breakdown insurance vary by insurer and state.

Rather than assuming one is more affordable or comprehensive than the other, compare the specific coverages, exclusions, deductibles, premiums and repair-shop requirements between any two plans.

When comparing service contracts, consider the following:

- What level of coverage you need. Compare bumper-to-bumper, powertrain, drivetrain and named-component plans.

- Eligibility requirements. Make sure the provider covers your vehicle's age, mileage, make and model and operates in your state.

- Exclusions. Ask which parts and repairs aren't covered.

- Claims. Find out whether repairs require prior authorization and whether the provider pays the repair shop directly or reimburses you.

- Repair shops. Confirm whether you can use your preferred mechanic or must choose from a network of approved garages.

- Contract length. Ask how long or for how many miles coverage lasts and whether it can be renewed.

- Ask whether the contract is transferable. This could matter if you plan to sell the vehicle.

- Total cost. Consider premiums, deductibles and any fees rather than focusing only on the monthly payment.

- Provider reputation. Check customer complaints, reviews, Better Business Bureau grades and any legal settlements or judgments.

Whether an extended warranty is worth the cost depends on your budget, the age of your vehicle, your likely repair costs and your tolerance for surprise expenses.

A service contract could be a good idea for a driver who wants to offset potentially large repair bills, particularly if they plan to keep a vehicle beyond its manufacturer's warranty or are concerned about expensive mechanical failures.

But if your vehicle doesn't need many covered repairs, you easily pay more in premiums than you get out of the contract.

An alternative to a warranty is to set aside money in a dedicated high-yield savings account for future car repairs, so your cash is earning a healthy return until it’s needed. That does mean taking on the risk of paying for a major repair out of pocket.

To help decide if an extended car warranty is right for you, weigh the cost of the contract and what it covers (and excludes) against how long you expect to own the vehicle and how you plan to use it.

Avoid buying under pressure. An extended warranty is always optional, and you don't have to purchase one right away when you get your vehicle.

What's the difference between an extended car warranty and car insurance?

An extended warranty or vehicle service contract generally covers specified mechanical or electrical breakdowns caused by normal wear and tear. Car insurance covers losses resulting from collisions, theft, fire and other covered incidents.

How long does an extended car warranty last?

The length of an extended warranty depends on the provider, plan and vehicle. Most last between three and seven years, although your specific contract will state the maximum number of years and/or miles covered, and coverage ends when either limit is reached.

Should I buy an extended warranty on a used car?

It depends on the vehicle's age, mileage, reliability, remaining factory coverage and your ability to pay for unexpected repairs. It also depends on whether the car still has manufacturer or certified pre-owned coverage. A service contract may be worth considering if you're buying a used vehicle and want protection against certain expensive repairs. Before purchasing, compare the contract's price and coverage with the vehicle's expected repair costs.
