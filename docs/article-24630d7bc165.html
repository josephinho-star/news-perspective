Catholic alcohol consumption drops to record low as Americans rethink moderate drinking habits

2026-10-25 13:00

A historic shift in American drinking habits shows Catholic alcohol consumption reaching record lows, though scientific experts caution against attributing the drop strictly to religious or cultural changes.

The share of Catholic adults in the United States who report drinking alcohol has fallen to 55%, according to a Gallup report authored by analyst Frank Newport.

That marks a decline from the 2002–2004 period, when 79% of Catholics reported consuming alcoholic beverages. Over that same timeframe, self-reported Protestant drinking rates remained flat at 54%.

"Drinking has dropped significantly within both the group of those who have no religious identity and, in particular, among Catholics," Newport wrote in the analysis.

However, experts urged caution when drawing sweeping conclusions from subgroup survey data.

Dr. Joshua Gowin, an associate professor in the Department of Radiology at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus who studies alcohol's effects on the brain, noted that self-reported polling figures can fluctuate based on sampling.

"There's definitely a general pattern of alcohol use decreasing across groups," Gowin told Fox News Digital.

"As far as the subgroups go, it wasn't clear or necessarily compelling to me that one group was showing a broad trend that wasn't reflected in the other groups," he went on.

"It seemed like really only one data point showed that effect ... which made me a little skeptical that this is a broader phenomenon versus a single survey sample."

Despite methodological limitations in tracking specific religious subgroups, researchers agree that broader social and health trends are driving a shift in overall drinking habits.

Gallup findings indicate that a majority of Americans now view moderate drinking — defined as one or two drinks per day — as bad for their health.

In the latest polling, 52% of Catholics said moderate alcohol consumption is bad for health, alongside 56% of adults with no religious affiliation and 48% of Protestants.

Gowin noted that this public shift reflects decades of neuroscience and medical research.

"Twenty years ago, there was still kind of a push and pull between some benefits of alcohol and then some things that even then we knew were potentially harmful," he said.

"It's not like the serious problems have gone away."

"Over time, we've gotten a lot more clarity on the things that are bad for our health ... the balance has just shifted more in favor of the evidence for negative health effects."

Gowin pointed out that large-scale neuroimaging studies now consistently link higher alcohol consumption to reduced brain volume, reinforcing public health warnings.

Researchers also attribute shifting consumption patterns to broader societal changes, including shifts in social behavior and loneliness.

Much of America's alcohol consumption has historically occurred in group settings, and changes in how people gather socially may be influencing drinking habits, they believe.

At the same time, experts caution that a drop in general social drinking does not mean severe alcohol problems have dissolved.

"One of the things that is a bit of a contradiction ... is that although the overall prevalence among all adults is down for the number of drinkers, there is actually a larger number of alcohol-related deaths in the past few years," Gowin noted.

"It's not like the serious problems have gone away."
