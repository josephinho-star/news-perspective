Why China Is Watching Taiwan’s Local Elections

2026-11-23 15:35

Taiwan’s November 28 local elections are just days away. The Kuomintang (KMT) starts from a strong position: it currently governs 15 of the island’s 22 counties and cities. But the final stretch of campaigning is proving far from comfortable, with several races closer than expected. On November 11, KMT chair Cheng Li-wun acknowledged that the races in several cities and counties, including Taipei, were proving particularly challenging.

Taiwan’s local elections by nature are not a referendum on cross-strait relations. That said, the outcome of local races will more or less shape Taiwan’s 2028 general election of the island – and thereby the dynamics of the Taiwan Strait.

As such, China is watching with interest.

One question that hangs over the local elections this year is the fate of the KMT’s current chair – a figure who is seen as friendly toward Beijing.

Cheng Li-wun has become more visible since taking over the KMT. Her April meeting with Xi Jinping in Beijing gave her a profile few Taiwanese opposition politicians can match. But her authority remains shaky. The local campaigns are a reminder that the KMT still relies on a wider circle of senior figures and local networks.

New Taipei mayor Hou Yu-ih, Taichung Mayor Lu Shiow-yen, and Legislative Speaker Han Kuo-yu have joined cross-county campaigns to shore up support, while Cheng has focused on more difficult races in Yilan, Hsinchu County, and Changhua. This division of labor demonstrates the party’s organizational reach, but also serves as proof that its campaign still depends on coalition building rather than the chair alone.

Meanwhile, internal frictions have surfaced. A KMT Taipei City councilor was photographed with DPP mayoral candidate Puma Shen, angering some Blue camp supporters. Another dispute followed political commentator – and former KMT vice presidential candidate – Jaw Shaw-kong’s appearance with a Taiwan People’s Party candidate in Hsinchu.

Cheng tried to quell the controversy, describing some reactions as misunderstandings. However, she acknowledged that the party’s Hsinchu primary had been fiercely contested and that unity still needed work. The controversies are a sign of how much effort is required to keep the party’s factions and electoral interests together, particularly in local races where different candidate’s personal interests may be at odds.

Cheng’s position is even more complicated because local candidates are cautious about how closely they want to be associated with her – and her cross-strait message. If the KMT underperforms in the local elections, Cheng may be forced to resign to take responsibility.

Some mainland Chinese nationalists may see a poor KMT result in the elections as an opportunity. If the party loses ground while the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) becomes more assertive about Taiwan’s separate identity, the political contradiction across the strait will become harder to avoid. In that scenario, they argue, Beijing may eventually have a clearer case for using force.

However, Beijing’s interests are better served by a KMT that can win elections. The small parties that reliably echo mainland positions – like the People First Party (PFP) or New Party (NP) – have little influence over Taiwan’s political direction. The KMT, by contrast, remains a major organization with established social networks and a long history of governing. The KMT currently runs local governments and enjoys a legislative plurality; it unquestionably has the capacity to compete for national power in 2028.

The KMT is a political interlocutor with real support in Taiwan. That offers Beijing something military pressure could never replicate.

The KMT does not share Beijing’s entire Taiwan agenda, of course, and most of its voters are not demanding immediate unification. But the party rejects formal independence, accepts some version of the One China framework, and supports cross-strait dialogue. Its continued relevance prevents Taiwan’s politics from being reduced to a simple contest between the DPP and Beijing.

The changing China-U.S. relationship makes the KMT’s role even more significant. During Xi’s September state visit to Washington, the two governments agreed to build a “constructive relationship of strategic stability” and advanced trade, technology, and crisis-communication mechanisms. Yet their public accounts differed on Taiwan. Beijing’s readout highlighted Xi’s call for Washington to oppose Taiwan independence, while the White House fact sheet didn’t mention Taiwan at all. Taiwan remains central to Beijing’s concerns, but from Washington’s perspective it is only one part of a much wider China-U.S. relationship.

Washington has reasons to deter Beijing, but also incentives to prevent Taiwan from becoming an issue that blocks every other form of cooperation. That may explain why U.S. President Donald Trump has so far delayed approving a pending $14 billion arms sale to Taiwan. Beijing, for its part, wants to resist deeper U.S. involvement in the Taiwan Strait without triggering a confrontation that could undermine wider diplomatic and economic engagement.

In this context, a competitive KMT gives both China and the United States another political channel through which tensions can be managed. If that channel disappears, the Taiwan question risks becoming more directly a contest between Beijing and Washington. That would leave less room for political bargaining inside Taiwan and place greater weight on military signaling between the two powers.

This is why Beijing should not judge the KMT simply by how closely its leaders echo mainland positions. The more closely a KMT leader is seen as aligned with Beijing, the easier it becomes for the DPP to question the party’s autonomy. In this scenario, Beijing may gain a friendlier partner at the top of the KMT while making the party less attractive to voters in Taiwan. Cheng’s access to Xi may strengthen her standing in Beijing, but it could actually undermine her popularity in Taiwan.

For Beijing, the KMT’s real value is its ability to persuade Taiwanese voters that closer cross-strait engagement is compatible with protecting their own interests.

The November 28 local elections are not about cross-strait relations and will not determine Taiwan’s long-term relationship with the mainland. When voters choose their mayors, county magistrates, and local representatives, local governance will matter more to many than cross-strait policy. But the results will reveal whether the KMT under Cheng retains the level of public support to sustain dialogue across the strait in the future.

For Beijing, a weakened KMT would mean losing one of its few remaining political channels inside Taiwan. By changing the balance of power, it could also strengthen the DPP’s claim to speak for Taiwan against Beijing and make the Taiwan Strait more dependent on military signaling between China and the United States.

The KMT cannot solve the Taiwan question on its own, and Beijing should not mistake the party for an instrument it can control. A party that is close to Beijing but unable to win votes is not much use. But an electorally competitive KMT can help keep the Taiwan question partly political rather than entirely military.
