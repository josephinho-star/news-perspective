She was kind and polite as they worked for an hour, stabbing her arm with needle after needle, and then she felt the burn of the pentobarbital.”

She’s asked about her team. She’s asked about the victim’s family … I think she is angry and confused about everything right now.

“She is very aware that the state tried to execute her,” Spivey continued.

Explaining the steps ahead, Christa Pike’s lawyer said: “Until yesterday, Ms. Pike was unable to to speak. She was unconscious. She was not able to to authorize us to file any any sort of new lawsuits.”

“And so the motion that we filed is is just to preserve evidence. It’s it’s the very very first step of of what will be a long legal battle, we presume,” he added.

Christa Pike’s lawyer continued to describe her physical appearance following the botched execution attempt, saying: “I will say that like the swelling is what I noticed.”

“Her hands looked like if you were to blow up a surgical [glove],” he added.

Christa Pike has not yet eaten and can't move her arms, lawyer says

Christa Pike’s lawyers are providing more details on the state Pike has been in following the botched execution attempt.

“On Monday night, it was our understanding that Christa was brain dead, and that life support would be removed the next day. But on Tuesday, to the shock of everyone, including me, including her team, and including the medical team, Christa woke up and began talking,” her lawyer Randy Spivey said.

“Christa has a long road to recovery. She has a blood clot in one of her arms. She has pneumonia. She is not able to swallow yet, so she is not eaten. She cannot move either of her arms. Both of her arms and hands are tremendously swollen, and we do not know what, if any, function she will regain in her arms. But we do know that there’s a long road ahead, and that is just the physical toll that all of this is taking,” Spivey added.

Christa Pike’s lawyers are now holding a press conference following a court hearing at the Davidson county chancery court in Nashville, Tennessee.

“Today’s hearing was about transparency, about why Christa, the courts, the media, and the public should not simply take the state’s word about what happened last Wednesday, because as we saw a little bit today, their word is shrouded in secrecy, in legal jargons, in attempts to obfuscate and obscure what really happened,” Pike’s lawyer Luke Ihnen said.

Judge I’Ashea Myles has also said that “counsel are expected to follow all visitation policies that are in place at the respective hospital”.

She added: “This court will not run afoul of whatever the facility has already in place,” saying that “there is a two-hour response on whether or not visitation will be allowed”.

“If an incarcerated individual is allowed visitation once a day from counsel, the court does not expect TDOC to deviate from that simply because the petitioner is in a hospital facility versus a prison facility,” Myles added.

Judge grants motion to preserve evidence from execution day

The judge says she is granting the motion to preserve all relevant evidence after telling the court: “This case is unique to say the least.”

I’Ashea Myles goes through a list of things Tennessee officials must preserve. It includes all drugs, IV lines and tubing that are in the state’s possession – “anything that was in the actual room” must be preserved and “that includes trash”.

Myles adds that anything that relates to the drug or supplies used must be preserved, as well as photographs, videos, audio recording, surveillance footage and anything in the witness rooms.

She also wants all communications, whether encrypted or not, to be preserved, stressing that this includes “anything concerning deviation from protocol or delays or equipment issues”.

She also warns: “Take special care not to lose, misplace, accidentally delete any information pertinent to this litigation.”

Tennessee attorney rejects claims Pike's access to lawyers was being limited

Assistant attorney general John W Ayers also pushed back against Christa Pike’s lawyers’ claims that their access to Pike is being limited by the Tennessee corrections department (TDOC).

He said: “TDOC does not intend to obstruct access to counsel. That’s why there’s been a process set up to request visitation to facilitate that. Now, these requests that were just layered on today go above and beyond what the conditions were before.”

Ayers said that Pike “never had unfettered access to a phone”, explaining that phone calls were arranged in advance but that Pike did have “unfettered access to a tablet”.

“Again, access to counsel is being facilitated. TDOC is not taking the position that counsel be should be excluded,” he said.

Evidence collected on 30 September by prison staff following the botched execution attempt on Christa Pike include observation logs, medical waste and products, as well as EKG readouts, assistant attorney general John W Ayers said.

He added that some evidence may not be in prison officials’ possession, saying: “It’s my understanding that the IV lines were cut, and so there may be portions of those IV lines that could have been still attached to Ms Pike when she was transported to the hospital.”

He added: “[The Tennessee correction department] can’t be ordered to preserve evidence that’s in the custody of another party, and so there may be items that were originally in TDOC’s custody that got transported to the hospital on the night of September 30. Again, counsel can confer on that and establish where that evidence is, but it would be premature and prejudicial, quite frankly, to enter an order requiring preservation of that kind of evidence before counsel had a chance to confer on it and get a picture of where that evidence might be.”

Pike's lawyers ask for her to be unshackled from hospital bed

Christa Pike’s lawyers said that Pike remains shackled to her hospital bed.

“Based on our understanding, medical staff have asked that the shackles be removed and have been denied that request by [the Tennessee corrections department],” they said, adding: “We would also amend our request and ask for that relief.”

Pike’s lawyers have also said that although they have been able to visit Pike since her botched execution attempt, “there are still obstacles being put in our way for that,” including waiting in a hospital lobby for over two hours before their visit request was approved.

Pike's lawyer says evidence must be preserved because Tennessee is 'likely' to seek future execution

Christa Pike’s lawyer tells the court that the state of Tennessee had made no promises that it will not seek a future execution.

“The state has made no promises that Ms Pike will not be subject to a future execution, and the evidence that we are seeking to preserve now is relevant for any future challenge that she makes, because as we pled in January, the same thing is likely to happen,” Luke Ihnen said.

He argues that is a reason why records must be preserved as it is likely Pike will face a future execution attempt.

Ihnen accused officials of “secreting away” evidence.

Judge I’Ashea Myles said the state has agreed that the Davidson county court has the authority to hear the case. She will now officially reopen it.

Christa Pike’s lawyer, Luke Ihnen, is now going through the complaints her team has filed, pointing out issues in the botched execution protocol and the qualifications of prison staff.

“The new protocol allows for the placement of a ‘central line’. However, upon information and belief, the defendants do not have the medical personnel or equipment and cannot secure the same,” he said.

He also said: “Ms Pike has a history of difficulty with blood draws and needle insertion. Prison medical staff often use a butterfly needle on her arm to draw blood, because her veins are so difficult to access. The plaintiff’s physical characteristics will make it significantly more difficult to achieve and or maintain.”

Christa Pike lawyer tells court she is speaking 'limitedly'

One of Christa Pike’s lawyers, Luke Ihnen, said Pike was “speaking, limitedly” as he addressed Judge I’Ashea Myles about procedural issues in the case, including whether the Davidson county court has jurisdiction.

Meanwhile, John W Ayers, an assistant attorney general, called Pike’s survival from two lethal injections an “unusual, procedural position”.

In the modern era of the US death penalty, Tennessee has never executed a person who was 18 at the time of their offence – the youngest was 23. Over the past 50 years, the state sent seven men who were 18 when they committed murder to death row, but in each case the sentence was vacated.

Tadryl Shipp, Christa Pike’s accomplice, held a fascination with satanic imagery. It was he who carved a pentagram in the victim’s chest, sending the local media into a frenzy. Yet because he was 17 at the time, he was barred from execution under Tennessee law; today he is serving a life sentence and had his first, unsuccessful, parole hearing in October 2025.

Had Pike been 10 months younger, she too would have been legally protected from the execution chamber. As time has passed, experts in children’s brains development have come to recognize that 18 is but an arbitrary line in the sand; adolescents – whatever their specific age – are susceptible to impulsive behavior.

And then there’s gender. Some argue that the gender of the 45 women who are now held on death rows across the US is irrelevant to their eligibility for the ultimate punishment. But in Pike’s case, it seems all too pertinent, given the agonizing litany of sexual and physical abuse she endured throughout her childhood.

She was subjected to a bewildering array of abuse, including multiple rapes by three separate men and beatings by numerous relatives.

Age. Gender. Abuse. The ingredients are all there, Pike’s legal team argues in the clemency petition for her crime, terrible though it was, to be recast in a different light.

Tennessee’s top prison official is resigning following Christa Pike’s botched execution attempt.

Over the weekend, governor Bill Lee announced that Frank Strada, the department of correction commissioner, will leave his position in late October amid an independent investigation into Pike’s failed execution attempt.

Despite calling the case “deeply disturbing,” Lee told reporters last week: “So far as we know, the department of correction followed exactly the protocol that was outlined.” Meanwhile, Strada said that his resignation would “be in the best interests” of the state as the investigation goes underway.

Death penalty lawyers said that Strada oversaw five “botched” executions, with common threads including prolonged suffering among prisoners and questionable injection procedures.

On September 30, Christa Pike, who was set to be the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than 200 years, survived two doses of pentobarbital, a sedatative.

Witnesses to the attempt to execute Pike described how they heard she was choking and struggling for an hour after receiving the first injection. She was then hospitalized after a second dose failed to kill her. In the hospital, she was put on a ventilator and had been in critical condition.

Speaking to the BBC, Joel Zivot, an anaesthesiologist advising Pike’s team, said: “She has some memories of being in the hospital but no memory, I believe, of the execution attempt.”

Zivot added that Pike was able to remember her name but incorrectly guessed the current month as September.

Christa Pike's lawyers hold press conference after saying she is 'conscious' following execution attempt

Christa Pike’s lawyers are holding a press conference today after revealing last night that their client was “conscious and speaking” following last week’s botched execution attempt in Tennessee.

The 50-year old death row inmate is still receiving critical care in a hospital while “handcuffed and shackled to the bed”, her lawyers said on Tuesday, adding: “Her recovery is medically unprecedented. Her prognosis remains unclear but, at a minimum, we expect a long recovery.”

Speaking to the BBC, an anaesthesiologist advising Pike’s team said that she is able to communicate in a “basic way” but did not seem to remember last Wednesday’s execution attempt.

Her legal team will ask a court on at a hearing on Wednesday to require the Tennessee department of correction to preserve all “relevant physical, written and electronic evidence” related to the failed execution, court documents said.

The press conference is scheduled for noon ET.
