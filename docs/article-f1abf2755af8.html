Haigh tells CBI that even US which is ‘hardly a hotbed of socialism’ has more public control of utilities than UK – UK politics live

2026-11-23 11:21

Haigh tells CBI that even US, 'hardly a hotbed of socialism', has more public control of utilities than UK

In her speech to the CBI, Louise Haigh, the first secretary of state, delivered one of the most detailed explanations we have had from a minister as to what Andy Burnham means when he calls for more public control of utilities.

She said that the government was not against markets, and that in areas like telecoms privatisation had worked. But in water and energy it hadn’t, she insisted. She suggested that, in wanting more public control in these sectors, the government was aligned with the US, “hardly a hotbed of state socialism”.

Now I know the word privatisation can start an argument. So let me be precise. This is not about whether markets work. They do. Where there is genuine competition, as in telecoms, markets have given consumers choice and kept prices low while delivering infrastructure.

The social licence to operate must be won every day by building trust and having the best value, product or service. Where competition exists, it should be left to thrive, supported by our strong, independent Competition and Markets Authority.

But when it comes to essential services, we have spent four decades trying and failing to recreate market conditions when consumers have no alternative.

Public control is about getting the right outcomes. It’s not ideological. Ownership is not a requirement. We took the railway back into public ownership because it wasn’t working for passengers or the taxpayer. But we want Great British Railways to be challenged by open access operators in Greater Manchester’s Bee Network, which you may have heard of. Routes are set by the elected mayor to benefit the community. The services are still run by private operators.

Delivering the homes we need depends on a buoyant developer sector.

Looking at water and energy, we must accept that the status quo is not the neutral, pragmatic, centre ground. It’s the outlier, an exception that no other nation is trying to emulate.

Around the world, the core infrastructure of water overwhelmingly stays in public hands. England is almost unique in handing the entire system to private owners. The only other country in that corner with us is Chile.

We are also one of the few countries in Europe whose electricity and gas transmission networks are entirely privately owned. And here’s the irony. Foreign governments now own a bigger share of our utilities than the British government does – significant stakes in our water, electricity and gas networks, returns paid to their taxpayers.

I welcome that investment. Let me be clear about that. But it raises a question: why do other governments see the strategic and commercial value in holding Britain’s essential assets, when for 40 years the British government couldn’t?

And it isn’t just Europe. Look at the United States of America, hardly a hotbed of state socialism. Most Americans get their water from a public utility. Fewer than one in 10 get it from a private one. Community-owned power companies serve one in seven American electricity customers in Los Angeles and Seattle, in Nashville, in Austin, and the federal government still owns the Tennessee Valley Authority, one of the biggest power companies in the country. Nobody thinks America is anti-business.

So let’s be honest with ourselves. The status quo is the ideological position, and saying so should not be taboo.

Haigh also said that past governments had resorted to trying to make the privatisation of utilities work through excessive regulation.

To make the experiment work, successive governments leant on regulation, first to try to recreate competition from monopolies through a spreadsheet; then to manage what private markets would never price on their own, like the spare capacity that keeps the lights on; then to deliver social policy, treated as a free good because it went on the bill, not the budget.

Ofgem now has six times the staff it had in the early 2000s, almost three times the administrative budget in real terms, and a rule book more than six times longer. We’ve got a hulking, complex behemoth of a regulatory state. It satisfies no one. Not consumers, not investors. When you prioritise everything, you prioritise nothing.

In the fog of complexity, bad actors can thrive, while the rest of us can’t move with a clarity and pace this moment demands.

That is why public control should not alarm anyone in this room. It’s not ideological. It’s pragmatic, and it’s essential – for people, for places, and for business.

We will measure our progress against three tests: affordability, investability, accountability.

What Haigh told CBI about government plans to curb powers of quangos

And this is what Louise Haigh said in her speech this morning about curbing the power of quangos. (See 9.32am.)

The prime minister is clear eyed about the limitations of centralised government, and the failings of politics. That is why we are rewiring the state.

Over decades, politicians were content to duck accountability by giving responsibility away from ministers to a sprawling web of regulators and arm’s length bodies.

More than twenty statutory consultees, all with veto power …

Notionally bound by statutory deadlines, but obfuscation has become habitual …

As part of rewiring the state we will reform arm’s length bodies to increase public control

We will take a look at each of their functions and pose the challenge: what should be devolved, what can be dissolved and what could be delivered departments We’ve already begun that process with the Environment Agency, Natural England, Ofwat and the Drinking Water Inspectorate

Where decisions are intrinsic to the fabric of a place, those powers and responsibilities must be made in that place, not in Whitehall.

GB Energy chair Juergen Maier appointed government's northern economy and reindustrialisation commissioner

In her speech, Louise Haigh announced that Juergen Maier, chair of Great British Energy, has been appointed as the government’s northern economy and reindustrialisation commissioner. She said:

Giving business the confidence to invest here, and create good jobs in our industrial heartlands. Creating good quality jobs in all parts of the country. The role of the private sector is essential to fulfilling that. That is why I am delighted to announce today a new northern economy and reindustrialisation commissioner.

With an impeccable industrial career we can learn much from, welcome, Juergen Maier.

Juergen will be based in No 10 North.

He will report to the business secretary and I, implementing our reindustrialisation agenda … And crucially on linking with business and leveraging private capital.

Haigh hints UK will increase tariffs on Chinese cars to secure exemption for Britain from Made in Europe rules

Q: [From the BBC’s business editor Simon Jack] Will the UK have to put up tariffs on Chinese cars if we want to join the Made in Europe programme?

Haigh implied the answer was yes. She replied:

We’ve set in place a process in order to protect our critical industries, including the automotive industry and steel, from the Made in Europe agenda.

And our message to Europe was that, on industry, they are worried about the Chinese flooding of products onto the market. We share that concern, and we share that challenge. So let’s work together. We don’t have to be competitors in that space; we benefit each other in the trading relationship.

The EU is particularly worried about the threat from Chinese electric vehicles (EVs). The Made in Europe programme will exempt foreign goods from EU procurement spending, and the UK is seeking an exemption so that British goods, particularly cars, aren’t covered by the Made in Europe protectionist rules.

Haigh says UK to be consulted about EU food standards rules under PM's deal - but accepts MPs won't be able to block them

Q: Can you confirm that, under the SPS deal announced on Friday, MPs won’t be able to vote down any new regulations on food and drink standards?

Haigh did not challenge that assertion from the FT’s Lucy Fisher. But she said that, under the deal, the UK would get “a seat at the table” when EU laws were being drawn up. “We will be consulted on every stage of the legislation,” she said.

Haigh tells CBI government has responded to business concerns about EU's Made in Europe rules

Louise Haigh, the first secretary of state, is speaking to the CBI now.

She started by telling the audience that the government had responded to the concerns of business by getting the EU to discuss a UK exemption from the Made in Europe protectionist legislation it is implementing. She said:

When Andy Burnham became prime minister, it was obvious that the Made in Europe policy posed serious risks to the UK’s critical industries, including automotive and steel, both fundamental to this government’s industrialisation plans.

Everyone told us that getting Made in Europe on the agenda at the summit would be impossible.

But thanks to the prime minister’s ambition and courage in taking this debate on, we have a far better deal for Britain.

The deal announced on Friday did not explicitly cover Made in Europe. The UK and the EU are still negotiating on that, and concessions for the UK are implicitly linked to the UK offering the EU concessions on the tuition fees paid by EU students attending British universities.

But last week Hamish Falconer, the EU relations minister, told MPs he was he was “pretty optimistic” of a good outcome for Britain in the Made in Europe negotiations.

CBI chief Rain Newton-Smith welcomes Burnham's debate on closer relations with EU, saying 'Brexit was wrong turn'

Rain Newton-Smith, the CBI chief executive, opened the organisation’s conference in London this morning with some relatively positive comments about the PM. Here are extracts from what she said.

Newton-Smith praised Burnham for bringing “hope” back to politics. She said:

In the last few years, we in the UK have been too ready to talk ourselves down. To put on our gloom-tinted glasses. They don’t sit well on me.

The prime minister has brought a dose of hope to our politics. And just as pessimism can be self-fulfilling, confidence can be contagious. It’s time we gave a shot of confidence to our economy. It’s time we got serious about a brighter future for our children.

She said the government needed to reassure business that “public control” of utilities did not mean nationalisation.

On investment … to some, “public control” sounds like code for renationalisation. That is kryptonite to investment. We know that’s not what’s coming – but give us the reassurance we need. Show this is a model built on partnership, not primacy. That government wants private capital at the table – not heading for the exit.

The government has heard this message. See 9.15am.

She said Burnham was right to start a debate about a closer relationship with the EU.

The prime minister is right to have this conversation. Brexit was a wrong turn for our economy. It gave us greater sovereignty. But sovereignty without prosperity is not enough. We didn’t vote to be poorer.

This is no time to re-fight the referendum. But we must come back to the table with bolder ambition.

She said that regional devolution could unlock investment and that the CBI was involved in a scheme to help give mayoral authorities more financial expertise.

There is promise in devolution. Done right, it can give business one front door for decisions that unlock investment. Done wrong, it could fracture confidence… leave regions competing with each other when they should be competing with the world.

The difference is whether authorities have the capacity and skills. Today, I can announce the CBI and our members are partnering with the National Wealth Fund and mayoral authorities across the UK … to parachute in senior financial secondees with the experience to make investment happen.

Tories welcome Haigh's hint government could adopt their plan to scrap Natural England

Louise Haigh, the first secretary of state, has declined to say whether Natural England will be abolished as part of plans to cut the number of quangos.

The Times has been told that proposals for greater public control, due to be announced next month in Burnham’s ten-year plan for government, will include cuts to the arms-length bodies Haigh blames for planning delays, pollution and high energy costs.

Natural England, the environment regulator, could be among the casualties. Senior ministers believe it is obstructing government plans to build hundreds of thousands of homes, piling unnecessary costs onto developers and preventing the construction of infrastructure.

Asked about this report, Haigh told Sky News this morning that the government did want to reduce the number of quangos – and she declined to say whether or not this would include Natural England. She said:

We’re considering all arms-length bodies, both devolving, dissolving, and bringing back into departments because we cannot continue with this situation where politicians look round and can’t take responsibility for the actions that people have elected their government for.

Today Katie Lam, the shadow housing secretary, said:

For far too long, Natural England has been allowed to hold the country to ransom, blocking desperately needed homes and vital infrastructure.

That’s why the Conservatives have pledged to abolish it. Now, it seems Labour are lifting that plan wholesale.

I don’t know who they think they’re kidding: this is a government that has spent two years piling on ever more bureaucracy. Only the Conservatives will take on the anti-growth ideologues and build the homes and infrastructure Britain needs.

Burnham to give update to MPs on UK-EU summit, as Haigh tells CBI plans for more public control of utilities 'not anti-business'

Good morning. This afternoon Andy Burnham will make a statement to MPs about the UK-EU summit on Friday, where agreements were reached on food and drink, emissions trading and a youth mobility scheme. At one point there were suggestions that he might use this Commons moment to give a speech with further details of his plan for a debate on forming a closer relationship with the EU, including possibly rejoin. Now, that won’t happen today; Burnham has decided to make that announcement part of his 10-year plan, which is being unveiled next month. But dozens of MPs will get the chance to put a question to Burnham, and so we will get a sense of what they feel about the prospect of revisiting the Brexit settlement.

According to the BBC, Burnham will describe the Friday deal as “the single biggest growth measure to be delivered in this parliament”.

He will tell parliament on Monday the “landmark” deal struck with the EU means there is a “very real prospect” the next decade can be “much better” than the last one.

We will hear alternative views at the CBI conference today. All the main UK opposition party leaders are speaking, and Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, and Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, are planning to criticise the agreement with Brussels, according to extracts of their remarks released overnight.

Burnham won’t be there himself. But he is sending his de facto deputy, Louise Haigh, and she is planning to reassure the CBI audience that Burnham’s plan for more public control of utilities does not mean the government is anti-business.

This government will put pragmatism and public interest ahead of failed political ideology. Public control is not anti-business – it’s about getting the foundations right, so that private enterprise can do what it does best.

She will point out that foreign governments already own “significant stakes in our water, electricity and gas networks with returns paid to their taxpayers” and she will say:

The status quo is not the neutral, pragmatic centre ground. It’s the outlier. An exception that no other nation is trying to emulate.

In its overnight briefing on the speech, the Cabinet Office says:

Challenging the misconception that public control means wholesale nationalisation, the first secretary will say this approach is not about whether or not markets work, but how effective monopolies have resulted in a lack of genuine competition.

Here is the agenda for the day.

9.30am: Rain Newton-Smith, the CBI chief executive, opens the annual CBI conference with a speech. There are also speeches from Louise Haigh, the first secretary of state, at 9.45am and Kemi Badenoch at 12.20pm, and Q&A events with Zack Polanski, the Green party leader, at 2.35pm, Ed Davey, the Lib Dem leader, at 3.55pm and Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, at 4.40pm.

Morning:Andy Burnham is due to visit a Morrisons in Kent to promote claims his SPS deal with the EU will help keep down food prices.

11.30am: Downing Street holds a lobby briefing.

2.30pm: Pat McFadden, the work and pensions secretary, takes questions in the Commons.

After 3.30pm: Burnham is expected to make a statement to the Commons about Friday’s UK-EU summit.
