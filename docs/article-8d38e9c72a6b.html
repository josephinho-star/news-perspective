Minns says Molly Ticehurst’s murderer deserved longer sentence for ‘vicious’ crime’ – as it happened

2026-09-16 08:11

Minns says Ticehurst’s murderer should have more jail time

The New South Wales premier, Chris Minns, says Daniel Billings is a “bastard” and his prison sentence is inadequate.

Billings, 31, will spend at least 20 years behind bars, before becoming eligible for parole, for murdering his ex-girlfriend MollyTicehurst, who was a childcare educator and young mother from Forbes, in central western NSW.

Justice Natalie Adams sentenced Billings to a maximum term of 28 years in the NSW supreme court on Friday, finding he acted out of revenge after Ticehurst reported allegations of sexual assault to police.

At a press conference earlier today, the premier was asked whether he thought Billings’ sentence was adequate. Minns responded:

No, it’s a vicious, horrible crime. I would have loved to see him far, far longer.

And obviously, we’ve changed the law since that horrific murder to have a standard non-parole period of 25 years, but you know the judge made the decision, and I just feel terrible for the family who are decent, honest, hard-working people who’ve lost their daughter at the hands of a complete bastard.

Billings was out on bail when he smashed through Ticehurst’s bedroom window and stabbed her 15 times with a hunting knife just before midnight on 21 April 2024.

Crackdown on reckless prescribing as regulator ramps up peptides oversight

Australia’s medical practitioner regulator is cracking down on high-volume prescribers of drugs including unapproved peptides, medicinal cannabis and GLP-1 weight-loss drugs such as Ozempic and Mounjaro.

The Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (Ahpra) announced on Wednesday it will also soon release conflict-of-interest guidance for doctors as concerns grow about commercial influence over their prescribing.

In a statement, Ahpra said the agency has been investigating unsafe prescribing and “is concerned growing consumer demand for certain medicines, coupled with undue commercial influence, has led some practitioners to depart from established professional standards”.

Telehealth prescribing and the provision of certain medicines require practitioners to demonstrate even greater rigour to prevent harm to patients.

Ahpra can take regulatory action against practitioners over unsafe prescribing, with the most serious cases referred to tribunals.

Medical Board of Australia chair Dr Susan O’Dwyer warned doctors and prescribers “active in these new pharmaceutical frontiers” that they have ethical and legal obligations, saying that:

A patient may want peptides, but that doesn’t mean they need peptides. It doesn’t mean that peptides are clinically justified.

For patients, the Ahpra chief executive Justin Untersteiner,said people “should not be taking chances on black-market peptides or advice from online influencers with zero clinical knowledge or training”.

Anyone with concerns about a doctor or other health professional’s conduct should contact Ahpra.

‘We do not want Peter’s death used to promote political agendas’: family issues statement after Victorian motorcyclist dies in reported pothole crash

The family of Peter Mason, a 71-year-old motorcyclist who died on Monday after reportedly hitting a pothole on a regional Victorian highway say his loss has “left an enormous hole” in their lives.

Mason, known as Tiki, was the president of a social motorcycle club, Eureka Social Riders, and was widely respected within the community. He died on Sunday after reportedly losing control on the Western Highway near Trawalla Road, with his death reigniting a political debate over the condition of Victoria’s roads in the lead-up to the November state election.

His family issued a statement this afternoon asking people not to use his death to “promote political agendas”. It reads:

There are no words to express the heartbreak our family is experiencing following the sudden and devastating loss of Peter Mason.

Peter was so much more than the circumstances of his death. He was a much-loved member of our family and a friend to many. His loss has left an enormous hole in our lives, and we are struggling to come to terms with a life taken far too soon.

We are aware of the public interest surrounding the circumstances of Peter’s death, including the condition of the road where the incident occurred. If, through the appropriate processes, changes are identified that could prevent another family from experiencing this devastation, we sincerely hope something positive can come from Peter’s death. However, these matters should be dealt with by the appropriate authorities.

We do not want Peter’s death used to promote political agendas, and we ask that no one speak on behalf of our family or claim to represent our views.

The family thanked everyone who had reached out with condolences, support and memories of Peter. They also asked reporters to be “respectful , factual and sensitive”.

Hugh Riminton to leave 10 and retire from journalism after near-five decade career

Veteran Network 10 journalist Hugh Riminton has told his colleagues he will retire on 2 October after a 48-year career.

Riminton made the announcement in an email to Network 10 staff earlier today, in which he said:

One of my emotions is excitement. I got my first job as a cadet reporter aged 17. I am now 65. 48 years in daily news is probably enough.

This reporter’s life has given me the world. I leave with nothing but gratitude for every hour of it.

In the coming weeks and months, I plan to support my son as he does his HSC. I also want to spend some time with my splendid Dad who is coming on 98.

Every hour is precious.

After that, I am sure projects will emerge to keep me busy.

I want to thank you all for being my colleagues and friends for the past 17 years.

There is no one among you I am not proud to work alongside.

Martin White, a vice-president of news at 10’s owner, Paramount, wrote:

As you’ve heard already, the great Hugh Riminton has made the tough decision to move on. After a near half-century on the day-to-day news, it’s an innings few of us can comprehend, let alone get close to.

While I’m sad, and will miss him greatly, I’m also proud to have been able to call him a colleague. I’ve even subbed a few of his scripts, though I can’t recall ever changing a word!

He is rightly celebrated as one of the premier journalists in this country and has had a vital impact on the Australian media, and on Australian democracy.

Sarah Abo has announced she will finish up as co-host of Today and leave Nine at the end of the year to pursue other opportunities.

In a statement today, Abo said:

Words can’t express how grateful I am to our viewers for letting me into your homes to be part of your morning routine. Without you, there is no Today show.

Together with 60 Minutes, I’ve spent my time at Nine chasing stories, travelling and reporting from all over the world, interviewing some incredible and inspiring people from all walks of life. Becoming a mum has heralded a new chapter and sharing that with our audience has been truly special. As many of you know, this has been a significant journey for my family and will continue to be my greatest achievement.

Nine’s executive director of news and current affairs, Fiona Dear, thanked Abo and said she had been an “integral part” of the team, “bringing warmth, energy and first-class journalism to the program each morning”.

Police appeal after woman’s body found in central Queensland sand dunes

A young woman has been found dead in sand dunes days after visiting a hair salon, sparking a police appeal, AAP reports.

Payge Leembruggen, 31, was discovered by a man walking his dogs at a popular Mackay beach in central Queensland about 8am on Tuesday, police said.

She was last seen at 8.55am on Friday at an Andergrove salon, located about 6km from the beach.

Speaking to reporters in Mackay earlier today, Det Insp Emma Novosel said:

We’re trying to fill a four-day gap at this time.

That’s why we’re seeking information from members of the public.

We’re obviously going through that bushland area to try and find any items of interest that could assist us in determining what happened to Payge and how she came to be in that location.

The mother-of-one’s cause of death was unknown but an autopsy had been scheduled, Novosel said.

Leembruggen was not reported missing and it was not unusual for her to travel from her Mount Jukes address – where she lived with extended family – to Mackay, about 40km away.

Support was being provided to Leembruggen’s family who were “understandably” upset, and her young child was being cared for by family members, police said.

Leembruggen is described as Caucasian and about 178cm tall with brown hair.

She was last seen wearing a white top, bright red yoga pants and a red bow in her hair, with a small backpack-style handbag.

Police have urged anyone who saw her between Friday and Tuesday or with any information to contact them.

First hearings announced in royal commission into Victoria’s Big Build

The royal commission into the Victorian government’s Big Build will hold its first public hearing next week.

Known formally as the “royal commission into the integrity of major public and civil infrastructure construction projects in Victoria”, the inquiry headed by Chris Kourakis confirmed it will hold its opening hearing next Wednesday.

The hearing will be held in the Melbourne magistrates court from 10am and will be live-streamed.

The spotlight was heavily on the prime minister and health minister Mark Butler today, who defended himself over planned cuts to private health insurance rebates for over 65s.

Anthony Albanese’s patience was also somewhat tested over his links to former Play School host and ex-Labor staffer Rhys Muldoon, who was yesterday stood down over “abhorrent” social media posts.

Tony Burke acknowledged immigration numbers do need to drop, ahead of his appearance at the National Press Club tomorrow (a month after he was first due to speak).

The opposition also tried to corner Labor on whether the prime minister had sought cabinet approval before extending the increase to Australia’s humanitarian intake to 20,000. Burke replied that it showed how far away the Coalition was from understanding government function.

‘All care, no responsibility’, Albanese defends private health insurance changes

Why doesn’t the government properly tax coal and gas, cut the $11bn diesel fuel rebate or reconsider the $368bn Aukus agreement if it needs to find savings, asks Monique Ryan, instead of targeting pensioners by cutting private health insurance rebates for over 65s.

It’s not a misprint – Ryan has been given a second question today, lucky her.

Anthony Albanese spends half of his answer defending Aukus, and then also defends the decision to change the private health insurance rebate.

He then takes aim at the crossbench.

I expect the Independents don’t have to come up with a budget, and that’s fine. All care – no responsibility. But as a government, we have to.

Rhys Muldoon comments ‘outrageous and completely unacceptable’ says Albanese

Before we get to the next question, Ben Small is booted, after a dixer to Tony Burke where he was railing off about the Plymouth Brethren church and their involvement in the election.

Then Tony Pasin returns to the dispatch box, and asks the prime minister point blank if he provided Rhys Muldoon a reference for his role as an Australia Day ambassador or any other role.

Anthony Albanese says it was appropriate that Muldoon was immediately stood down after several old social media posts surfaced.

There are two roles in which action has been taken. One was he was a relief staff casual employee for Senator Dowling, and the comments, when people became aware of them and the senator became aware of them, he took immediate action and that’s appropriate.

Albanese says Muldoon was an Australia Day ambassador since 2014 but was not this year.

The assistant minister has taken action today, who has responsibility for those issues, to clearly say that it is not appropriate for him to be an ambassador. I find his comments outrageous and completely unacceptable.

Butler on the defence over decision to lower private health insurance rebates for over 65s

Monique Ryan has picked up the baton and is also asking the government about its decision to lower the private health insurance rebates for over 65s. Modelling by the parliamentary budget office that she commissioned found half of the $3bn in savings would be paid for by pensioners.

She asks if pensioners were “really the only place that you could find budget savings?”

Mark Butler says, for the 50th time, that the government has had to make “hard decisions” and that it’s necessary to pay for more aged care beds and home care support.

He says the initial decision by John Howard to give over 65s a higher health insurance rebate was done in a very different economic scenario.

I would like to be able to maintain that arrangement but we have had to make some difficult decisions to find the aged care investment that is necessary.

Now in this debate there have frankly been a lot of misinformation about what the impact will be. Our modelling indicates that first of all the average impact on a private health insurance member of these changes will be in the order of 70 cents per day.

‘Zero to do with me’: Albanese distances himself from Muldoon

Anthony Albanese has tried to completely distance himself from Rhys Muldoon, the former Play School host, and brief Labor staffer who was yesterday stood down after “abhorrent” posts were unearthed.

Liberal frontbencher Tony Pasin asks what involvement Albanese had in Muldoon’s appointment as a Labor adviser and if he provided Muldoon with a reference for his Australia Day ambassador role.

Albanese says no, and no.

On the first part of that question, none. On the second part, his Australia Day role began under the former Coalition government … and his appointment had zero to do with me.

‘We do need to get net overseas migration down’, says Burke

Net migration does need to come down, says Tony Burke, ahead of his national press club address tomorrow.

Regional independent MP Helen Haines asks him whether the government will speed up visa processing for regional areas, who are struggling to get critical workers.

We do need to get net overseas migration down, we do …

To be able to work through this, we need to be able to make sure that we deal with the numbers – we need to make sure that we deal with the numbers overall and do so in a sensible, targeted way that provides the easiest pathway through for the Australian economy.

He doesn’t exactly answer the question on whether he’ll reverse the “go-slow” on visa processing for the regions.

He does say there are more tools that the government has at its disposal to improve the migration system and he’ll says he’ll have more to say about that tomorrow.

Nationals MP Michelle Landry is next and asks about a warning from the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists which says Labor’s reduction of private health cover rebates for over 65s will lead to “increased costs for older women who require gynaecological care”.

Anthony Albanese says broadly that the government has introduced the largest ever women’s health program.

We are doing this … to find necessary savings to fund more beds in aged care, more packages at home and better care, particularly changing the co-contribution arrangements which I think have been advocated by the opposition, among others, around showering and other hygiene activities.

The changes that we make restore arrangements to their original design which did not differentiate between members or private health insurance holders based on their age.
