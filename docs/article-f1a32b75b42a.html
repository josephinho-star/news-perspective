Jensen Huang’s Nvidia debacle reveals America’s real problem: no skilled builders

2026-11-19 09:50

If there’s anything more insufferable than New York Democrat Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez's choreographed "on the go" interviews on social media... it’s AOC when she has a point.

Her recent comment that we can’t have "five guys in tuxedos" control our national economy and security is, alas, reasonable in the wake of the Jensen Huang Nvidia debacle.

But seizing and breaking up companies built by brilliant, entrepreneurial Americans (as she advocates) isn’t the answer (it’s also not constitutional, but that likely wouldn’t deter her). Socialism isn’t the solution here. It’s the problem.

And the leftist turn in our education system is why we’re stuck relying on foreign actors for America’s critical supply chains.

Because even when we want to build here at home... we no longer have the ability.

We all know the Nvidia facts by now. Jensen Huang, the company’s flamboyant, now former CEO, is accused of extramarital affairs, including one with an alleged Chinese spy. As part of that liaison, Huang may have sold billions of dollars' worth of America’s most advanced chips to the woman’s companies, potentially violating U.S. sanctions.

Huang ran NVIDIA like a potentate: 60 direct reports, no succession plan, and a reputation for abusing underlings. He also behaved like an adolescent in public.

In short: maybe not the guy we want single-handedly embodying one of America’s biggest technological advantages.

Adding to this vulnerability, NVIDIA doesn’t manufacture its chips; it designs them. Its highest-end chips are made by TSMC in Taiwan—80 miles off the coast of a nation that considers Taiwan its territory.

And TSMC operates two manufacturing plants inside mainland China.

What could go wrong?

The threat of China capturing Taiwan’s advanced facilities is so serious that TSMC and its Dutch equipment supplier, ASML, reportedly possess technology capable of remotely disabling critical manufacturing equipment.

Consider that. The world’s most important chip manufacturer has a plan to disable its own factories to keep China from capturing them. And a European nation has the kill switch.

Get the feeling we’re vulnerable here?

So did both the Biden and Trump administrations.

Recognizing these vulnerabilities, in 2022 the Biden administration signed into law the CHIPS and Science Act to bring critical manufacturing home. TSMC received up to $6.6 billion in federal funding to build an advanced chip fabrication plant—a "fab"—in Arizona.

Trump then prioritized manufacturing revival through tariffs and investment negotiations. In July 2026, TSMC raised its planned U.S. investment to $265 billion.

There was only one problem. America—literally—couldn’t build the factory.

You read that right. The nation that built the Panama Canal and Hoover Dam, put men on the moon and invented the internet... lacked the expertise to build the plant.

America must stop treating manufacturing—you know, actually building things—as a lesser occupation. To own the future—a safe future—we need electricians, pipefitters, welders, precision technicians and engineers.

TSMC struggled to find workers experienced in specialized construction and equipment installation. So in 2023, it announced plans to bring approximately 500 specialized workers from Taiwan.

American construction unions objected, of course, insisting qualified domestic workers were available. Eventually, a compromise emerged.

But thousands of Taiwanese employees and their families still relocated to Arizona, creating an area known as Tiny Taipei. Taiwanese restaurants, businesses, and residential communities emerged around the project. One apartment complex became known as TSMC Village.

And labor wasn’t the only problem. America’s regulatory morass further delayed construction, with TSMC spending $35 million to address 18,000 work rules.

TSMC’s first Arizona fab began producing chips in late 2024. A second is preparing for production, and a third is under construction. Yet worker shortages persist. In July 2026, TSMC CFO Wendell Huang (is everybody in chip tech named Huang?) acknowledged continuing shortages of construction workers.

How can America lack the people for these jobs? Look at our education system.

For decades, educators have emphasized four-year degrees while treating vocational training and skilled trades as an embarrassment.

As podcaster Adam Carolla told this writer during a recent interview, "So now you attract a bunch of people… attracted to what is a socialist lifestyle. And now you put those people in charge of educating your kids. And how do you think that’s going to turn out?"

Meanwhile, a semiconductor workforce study commissioned by the Semiconductor Industry Association projects tens of thousands of unfilled technical positions by 2030.

Yet teachers’ unions continue pushing young Americans toward useless degrees and desk jobs increasingly threatened by AI—while the expertise needed to manufacture that technology grows overseas.

All while the top foreign donors to American colleges remain shielded from scrutiny.

It’s time for a reset.

Then-Republican presidential candidate Sen. Marco Rubio understood this when he declared, at the fourth Republican presidential primary debate on November 10, 2015, "For the life of me, I don't know why we have stigmatized vocational education. Welders make more money than philosophers. We need more welders and less philosophers."

Naturally, Rubio faced media backlash, with The Washington Post and The Associated Press rushing to fact-check welders’ and philosophers’ salaries (there’s a philosopher career track?).

Rubio couldn’t have been more right. America must stop treating manufacturing—you know, actually building things—as a lesser occupation. That means technical high schools, apprenticeships, and community colleges committed to teaching useful skills.

Above all, it requires a paradigm shift in how we think about education and teachers. To own the future—a safe future—we need electricians, pipefitters, welders, precision technicians and engineers.

In short, people who know how to swing a damn hammer. Accurately.

So enough with $100,000 in student loans for degrees in Hate America 101. If America wants manufacturing independence, it must rebuild the workforce—and the education system capable of training it.

Otherwise, we’ll remain dependent on foreign labor to build the very factories intended to protect us from foreign dependence.

And we'll remain exposed to foreign intrigues in a region increasingly threatened by our main global adversary.
