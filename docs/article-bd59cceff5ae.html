China’s Visibility Gap Around the Trump-Xi Summit

2026-09-25 16:44

Beijing formally confirmed Chinese President Xi Jinping’s September 23-25 visit to the United States on September 21, just two days before the trip began. The news had barely registered among many of the Chinese people I spoke with the following day. Official coverage began appearing online following the announcement, but visible discussion among ordinary users on platforms such as Weibo, WeChat, and Xiaohongshu (RedNote) remained scarce.

That changed once Xi’s visit was underway. Chinese social media filled with reports about the summit, with much of the visible content coming from official outlets and accounts, emphasizing the diplomatic significance and choreography of the visit, including Trump’s reception of Xi and calls for “constructive strategic stability.” The shift from limited information to abundant official coverage raised a different question: How much did ordinary Chinese people know about the summit before official coverage moved into full gear?

On September 22, the day after Beijing’s formal announcement and one day before Xi’s visit began, I conducted informal interviews with a dozen individuals across China and the diaspora, including overseas students and professionals, domestic public-sector workers, private-sector employees, and an older relative. Awareness varied considerably by location and occupation.

Overseas students and professionals had generally learned of the trip earlier through foreign news outlets or private messaging groups. Some public-sector employees in China learned about it only after the official announcement, noting that senior leaders’ schedules are typically kept confidential until the last minute. Meanwhile, many of the private-sector employees I spoke with in China remained unaware of the visit even after the announcement.

Yet when asked about it, many responded by searching for the news themselves. What they found was a remarkably uniform information environment. On Weibo, Douyin, and Xiaohongshu, searches related to Xi’s U.S. visit were dominated by official and state-media content. On Xiaohongshu, some targeted searches produced either zero visible results or unrelated posts concerning personal travel and visas. Under some official media posts, comment sections were restricted or turned off. The interviews suggested that curiosity could exist even when little of it was publicly visible.

It is tempting to attribute this lack of visible discussion solely to censorship, but the interviews pointed to a more complicated picture. One respondent pointed to self-censorship: “Some of what people might say in private wouldn’t feel appropriate to post publicly.” Others suggested that interest might increase only after the meetings produced concrete outcomes.

The searches themselves could not reveal whether the lack of visible discussion reflected content controls, platform ranking, self-censorship, limited public interest, or some combination of these factors.

The summit itself added another layer to this ambiguity. Once Xi arrived in the United States, information about the visit was no longer difficult to find. Official reports and accounts circulated extensive coverage of the meetings and their diplomatic symbolism. Yet the greater volume of information did not necessarily make public opinion easier to observe: Much of what was readily visible still came from official sources rather than ordinary users.

However, in specialized, interest-driven spaces, discussion was easier to find. On WeChat financial blogs like “Liu Xiaobo Talk Finance” and their commentary threads, retail investors and market watchers debated the summit’s possible implications for tariffs, stock prices, and exchange rates. According to the CGCC-USA 2026 Business Survey Report, 79 percent of surveyed Chinese companies operating in the United States reported negative impacts from tariffs, while 36 percent anticipated a slight improvement in bilateral trade relations, underscoring the concrete economic stakes surrounding the summit.

The Trump-Xi summit thus exposed a broader “visibility gap” in China’s information environment. On September 22, even after the visit had been officially announced, many of the people I interviewed had not heard about it, although they often became curious enough to search once asked. Days later, information about the summit was everywhere, but the most visible narratives remained overwhelmingly official.

For outside observers trying to gauge public sentiment in China, the difficulty is not simply that Chinese people are silent. It is that the Chinese internet makes a lack of discussion unusually difficult to interpret.
