5 signs you may not know you’re drowning in credit card debt

2026-10-24 21:45

Credit card debt has a way of sneaking up on you. A few hundred dollars carried over from one month becomes a few thousand, and before long, you’re paying more in interest than you spend on new purchases.

If this sounds like you, you’re not alone. According to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, outstanding credit card balances reached $1.26 trillion in the second quarter of 2026, an increase of $54 billion from a year earlier. A study among a nationally representative sample of 2,000 U.S. adults who reported having some type of debt in the past year by Accredited Debt Relief found that two-thirds of respondents say their own spending or borrowing decisions contributed to their debt.

Of course, using credit cards doesn’t have to be a problem. When you pay your balance in full every month, you can take advantage of rewards, purchase protections and the convenience of not carrying cash without paying interest. The trouble starts when your balance grows faster than you can pay it down. And because credit card debt may accumulate gradually, the warning signs aren’t always obvious until your finances are under pressure.

Here are five signs that your credit card debt is getting out of control, and what you can do before it becomes a financial crisis.

1. You’re only making the minimum monthly payment

Making the minimum payment on your credit card keeps your account current, but it can also keep you in debt. Credit card companies usually calculate minimum payments as a percentage of your outstanding balance, sometimes with accrued interest and fees added. When you carry a large balance at a high interest rate, much of your payment goes toward interest rather than reducing what you owe.

Consider a $5,000 credit card balance with a 26% annual percentage rate (APR). The interest alone would amount to roughly $109 in the first month, assuming no additional purchases and a 30-day billing cycle. If your minimum payment is $157, only about $50 would go toward reducing your principal, the $5,000 you originally borrowed. And as the balance declines, your minimum payment may fall too, stretching repayment over many years.

Depending on the interest rate and the issuer’s minimum payment formula, paying only the minimum can extend repayment for decades — actually grow your debt. Some card issuers base their minimum on a flat percent against your balance, so in this example, a minimum payment equal to 2% of your balance is $100. At that rate and assuming you make no other purchases on this credit card, your balance will actually grow creating what lenders call negative amortization.

Recognizing that minimum payments make consumers pay more in interest, Congress passed the Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009, commonly called the CARD Act. The law requires issuers to include a minimum payment warning on monthly statements that shows how long repayment would take and how much interest consumers would pay if they made only minimum payments.

If you’re making only the minimum payment because that’s all you can afford, it’s a sign that your credit card debt may be consuming too much of your disposable income.

2. Your interest charges are higher than your new purchases

Personal finance is all about ratios. For example, your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is a factor lenders take into account when deciding whether to loan you money for a house, a car or for personal use. Your credit utilization rate — the percentage of available credit that you’re using on your credit cards and other lines of credit — accounts for 30% of your FICO score.

Another ratio can let you know when it’s time to stop spending and start paying down your credit cards: the ratio between your monthly interest charges and your monthly spend.

Suppose you have a $10,000 credit card balance with a 24% APR. At that rate, you could accumulate approximately $200 in interest during a 30-day billing cycle, even if you don’t make another purchase. If you charge only $150 in new purchases during that period, you’re paying more to finance your existing debt than you’re spending on goods and services.

Many cards calculate interest on a daily basis, allowing unpaid interest to become part of the balance on which future interest is assessed. This can accelerate the cost of borrowing. And if you’re carrying a balance, you may lose the interest-free grace period on new purchases. That means new charges can begin accumulating interest immediately, depending on your card’s terms.

The result is a cycle in which your existing debt generates additional borrowing costs even when you’ve reduced your spending.

3. You’re using credit cards to pay for basic necessities

There’s a difference between using a credit card to buy groceries because you want to earn rewards and using it because you don’t have enough money in your checking account. The former could be a smart point-harvesting strategy if you pay your balance in full every month. The second suggests that your household expenses are exceeding your available income.

Groceries, utilities and transportation are recurring expenses, but they aren’t investments like your mortgage payment, which builds equity in your home. When you borrow money to cover them, you’re effectively financing today’s living expenses with tomorrow’s income. And if you can’t repay those charges, you’re adding interest to expenses that will come around again next month.

In accounting terms, the underlying problem is a cash flow deficit, meaning more money is leaving your household than coming in. That doesn’t necessarily mean you’re spending irresponsibly. Rising housing costs, medical expenses, childcare or a reduction in income can create a shortfall even for households that carefully manage their budgets.

But relying on credit cards to make up the difference isn’t a sustainable solution, and it leaves you particularly vulnerable to unforeseen financial shocks. A major car repair or medical bill could push you close to your credit limit, leaving you with fewer options when you need money most.

4. Your credit utilization ratio is above 30%

You got a taste of the credit utilization ratio above, but let’s spend some more time on this important metric.

Your credit card balances don’t just affect how much interest you pay. They can also influence your ability to borrow money in the future. For example, if you have three credit cards with a combined credit limit of $20,000 and outstanding balances totaling $8,000, your credit utilization ratio is 40%.

According to the CFPB, consumers should generally aim to keep their credit utilization below 30%. But the beauty of a ratio is its continuous nature: 30% isn’t a hard cutoff that automatically damages your credit or triggers action from a lender. The models that create your credit score are mathematical assumptions about the difference between knowing when something is a pile and when it’s a heap.

As the models have become more sophisticated in the last half century and pull in far more data than before, lenders and credit scoring models can consider both your overall utilization and the balances on individual accounts. That means having one card nearly maxed out could hurt your credit score even if your combined utilization across all accounts remains relatively low.

If your balances keep rising while your available credit remains unchanged, that’s a warning sign worth taking seriously.

5. You’re paying late fees or considering debt settlement

Missing a credit card payment is one of the clearest indications that your debt may be getting beyond your ability to manage it.

Late payments can trigger fees, increase borrowing costs and damage your credit score. And when a payment becomes at least 30 days overdue, the issuer may report the delinquency to the major credit bureaus. That negative information can generally remain on your credit report for up to seven years, potentially affecting future borrowing decisions.

Besides the financial penalties, missed payments can create another problem. Once you fall behind, catching up becomes more difficult because you’re trying to cover both your current obligations and overdue amounts. Some borrowers respond by looking for debt settlement services. These companies typically offer to negotiate with creditors to reduce the amount owed, often in exchange for a fee.

Debt settlement can sometimes reduce an outstanding balance, but it also carries risks. Some companies encourage consumers to stop making payments while accumulating money for a potential settlement. During that time, interest and fees may continue to accrue, creditors may pursue collections and credit scores can deteriorate.

There’s also no guarantee that a creditor will agree to accept less than the full balance. And forgiven debt may be taxable, depending on the borrower’s circumstances and applicable exclusions. Consumers should be especially cautious of companies demanding payment before delivering results.

Under the Federal Trade Commission’s Telemarketing Sales Rule, covered debt relief companies generally cannot collect fees until they have successfully negotiated or otherwise resolved a debt, the customer has agreed to the arrangement and the customer has made at least one payment under it.

What to do before your credit card debt gets worse

If you recognize several of these warning signs, the first step is to determine exactly how much you owe.

Review your credit card statements and make a list of each balance, interest rate and minimum monthly payment. Then compare your total monthly debt obligations with your take-home income and essential living expenses.

If you have enough income to cover your minimum payments and pay extra toward your balances, your best option may be to adopt one of two popular debt management techniques: the avalanche method or the snowball method. With the former, you pay all your minimums and the extra money goes to paying off your highest interest rate debt; with the latter, you pay off the smallest debt first and roll the extra money into larger debts.

If you’re struggling to make even the minimum payments, contact your credit card issuer before missing another due date. Ask whether you qualify for a hardship program and whether the company can reduce your interest rate or establish a manageable repayment schedule.

You can also consider working with a nonprofit credit counseling organization. A counselor may help you develop a budget or establish a debt management plan that consolidates payments and potentially reduces interest charges through agreements with participating creditors.

Unlike debt settlement, a debt management plan generally aims to repay the principal you owe, often with concessions from creditors.

Credit card debt becomes especially difficult to manage when interest charges, fees and new purchases consistently exceed what you can afford to repay. Per the latest study by Accredited Debt Relief, over the past 12 months, 45% of Gen Z saw their debt increase, compared with 39% of millennials, 34% of Gen X and 30% of baby boomers.

If your budget cannot cover essential living expenses and minimum debt payments, seek assistance before accounts become delinquent. Addressing the shortfall early may preserve repayment options that become harder to obtain after missed payments and collection activity begin.

What happens if I only pay the minimum on my credit card?

Paying only the minimum keeps your account current but traps you in debt. Because minimum payments are often just a small percentage of your balance, the majority of your payment goes toward interest rather than the principal. In high-interest scenarios, this can stretch repayment over decades or even cause your total debt to grow over time (negative amortization).

What is a good credit utilization ratio to aim for?

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau recommends keeping your credit utilization ratio — the percentage of available credit you are actually using — below 30%. Carrying balances above this threshold, or nearly maxing out a single individual card, can negatively impact your credit score and limit your ability to borrow money in the future.

Is it a bad idea to use credit cards to pay for basic necessities?

Using a credit card for groceries or utilities is only a good strategy if you pay the balance in full every month. If you are relying on credit because you lack funds in your checking account, it signals a cash flow deficit. Financing daily living expenses with debt leaves you highly vulnerable to unforeseen financial emergencies like a medical bill or car repair.

How do I know if my credit card interest charges are too high?

A major warning sign is when your monthly interest charges exceed the amount you are spending on new purchases. This means you are paying more to finance your existing debt than you are spending on goods and services, creating a cycle where your debt generates significant borrowing costs even if you actively reduce your spending.

What should I do if I can no longer afford my credit card payments?

If you are struggling to make minimum payments, contact your credit card issuer immediately to ask about hardship programs, reduced interest rates or manageable repayment schedules. You can also consult a nonprofit credit counseling organization to set up a structured debt management plan. Exercise caution with for-profit debt settlement services, as stopping payments to negotiate can accrue more fees, trigger collections and severely damage your credit score.
