Fidan rebuffs criticism of Mecca pact, cites NATO’s Article 5 record

2026-09-22 14:49

Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan has rebuffed criticism that the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan is failing its first test amid Houthi attacks on the kingdom, citing NATO’s record to argue that it is too early to judge the new alliance.

Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed the pact on August 7. Like NATO’s collective-defense clause, it states that an armed attack on one member will be considered an attack on all three.

Fidan was asked during a panel in New York on Monday how the alliance had performed since the Iran-backed Houthis stepped up attacks on Saudi Arabia.

He turned to James Jeffrey, a former US ambassador to Turkey who was attending the panel, and asked how many times he had seen NATO invoke Article 5 during his career. The audience responded that it had happened only once.

“Nobody criticizes NATO for being a ‘paper tiger,’” Fidan said.

He claimed that it was unfair to point to Yemen’s decade-long conflict as proof that the recently established alliance did not work.

“We have only just started,” he added.

Fidan was speaking at a panel titled “Türkiye–U.S. Relations on the Eve of the Centennial,” organized by Turkey’s Presidential Communications Directorate on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly.

NATO says Article 5 has been formally invoked only once, following the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks on the United States. The provision treats an armed attack on one member as an attack on all, although each ally decides what assistance it considers necessary and is not automatically required to use military force.

Fidan previously described the Mecca pact’s mutual-defense commitment as technically equivalent to Article 5.

He said a country under attack would have to request assistance and consult the other two members about what support it needed, which could range from intelligence and logistics to ammunition or military units.

His latest remarks came two days after he said Turkey was prepared to meet possible Saudi military needs, particularly in technical areas. He did not say Riyadh had formally requested assistance or identify what Ankara might provide.

The Houthis have attacked Saudi cities, energy infrastructure and shipping since declaring a maritime blockade against the kingdom in July, opening another front in the wider regional war that began with US and Israeli strikes on Iran in February.

No member of the Mecca pact has publicly requested its formal activation or announced a joint military response to the attacks.

Foreign and defense ministers and military chiefs from the three countries met in İstanbul on August 31 and agreed to establish a permanent secretariat in Saudi Arabia, initially led by a Pakistani secretary-general. They also agreed to pursue joint training, technology development and defense production.

Fidan said in New York that the alliance would be opened to other countries once its institutions were in place.

“We have a roadmap with our partners Saudi Arabia and Pakistan,” he said, according to Anka news agency. “There are countries that want to be part of this regional structure.”
