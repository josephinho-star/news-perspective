UK says reset deal with EU agreed but talks will continue ‘in some areas’ – live

2026-11-20 10:30

'Deal is done,' No10 spokesperson says, but talks 'will continue in some areas'

Senior political correspondent

The UK and EU have agreed a reset deal on food and drink, emissions trading and a new youth mobility scheme, Downing Street has said, although talks on the level of tuition fees EU students will pay remains unresolved.

“The deal is done – negotiations have concluded,” a No 10 spokesperson said, less than an hour after Andy Burnham arrived at the summit venue in Brussels, where he was greeted by Ursula von der Leyen, the European Commission president, António Costa, the European Council president.

“Now, both sides will work towards implementation of the youth mobility scheme, the SPS deal and the emissions trading scheme. Both sides have agreed when these will come into force. And those dates are set out in the joint statement, which will be published in due course.

As with all international agreements, there is a legal process in each respective country which will now take place. And talks will continue on some areas – these will be set out in the joint statement later.”

Talks “will continue on some areas”, it added (10:40).

Tuition fees hold up mostly political - snap analysis

Just to highlight Jennifer’s point there (10:40): the “hold-up” over tuition fees is nowhere near as catastrophic or dramatic as some headlines elsewhere would have you think – and, in fact, was pretty anticipated.

The three sectoral deals are still getting signed today and it counts as a genuine step forward in the post-Brexit relationship; it’s just that their full implementation get suspended until the tuition fee issue is resolved.

The explicit link on their implementation does create a new high-pressure political deadline in six months’ time, adding urgency for the two sides to resolve the tuition fees issue and unblock the whole process.

Ah, classic EU-UK negotiations stuff.

'Heavily choreographed' welcome ceremony with several ministers in attendance

Senior political correspondent

The arrival of Burnham and his European hosts was heavily choreographed – there were no words to the assembled media, or any shouted questions – but one notable element was the sheer number of ministers the UK PM has brought with him, in a sign of how seriously the summit is being treated by London.

These included Lou Haigh, the first secretary of state; John Healey, the chancellor; Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary; Jonathan Reynolds, the business secretary; Angela Eagle, the environment secretary, and Anneliese Midgley, the chief whip.

Three sectoral deals to be signed, but won't enter into force until tuition fees issue resolved

Andy Burnham’s Brexit reset deals will not materialise unless the UK reaches a settlement with the EU over tuition fee rates for European students.

EU sources said on Friday that the three deals – on food and drink, emissions trading and youth mobility – will not enter into force without equal treatment for European students at British universities.

In a statement seen by the Guardian from the EU council and European Commission spelt it out:

“The Council and Commission consider that this agreement is to provide for equal treatment with nationals as regards tuition fees for all students. The Council and Commission acknowledge that these four agreements form a package of the 2026 EU-UK summit.”

The EU wants to ensure its students pay UK domestic rates of tuition fees, rather than the far higher international rate. In a concession last night, the bloc agreed to defer agreement on tuition fees by six months, but that does not mean they have accepted the UK’s position that their students pay higher rates

As the Guardian has reported, it intends to use its leverage, by making the three deals contingent on an acceptable agreement on tuition fees.

“I think our position has been clear. We think this is an issue that needs to be addressed.”

The European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen and the European Council president António Costa are now in position and waiting for UK’s Andy Burnham.

Tuition fees deferral seeks to neutralise London's leverage in talks - snap analysis

Making today’s reset deal contingent on a further agreement on the level of tuition fees for EU citizens is designed to neutralise the leverage London gets in the next six months, EU sources have said.

Senior diplomats had already confirmed this week that the EU would defer the deal on the level of tuition fees for another six months, a move designed to give the UK leverage in parallel negotiations to have the UK included in Made in Europe legislation designed to protect EU supply chains against China.

But the move to make the food and drink deal also contingent on a tuition fees deal effectively seeks to neutralise the leverage the UK gets today.

This is a serious chess game and shows the EU, again, is willing to speak very warmly about the UK but will play absolute hard ball when it comes to conceding any points to a former member state.

It was the same point France was making last night insisting the UK could not have voting rights on legislation in relation to food and drink.

Deals on food and drink, emissions trading, youth mobility agreed, but tricky question of tuition fees 'kicked down the road'

Peter Walker and Jennifer Rankin

With Andy Burnham arriving soon at the UK-EU summit the position is – well, much as it was last night, with a broad agreement but the most tricky issue kicked down the road for six months.

The official mood music from EU officials on Friday morning was positive, with talk of “concrete deliverables” for citizens on both sides in the three key areas: a deal on food and drink imports and exports; emissions trading; and the planned mutual “youth experience” exchange scheme.

“We expect the leaders to confirm and unlock the conclusion of three important negotiations that have been ongoing.”

There is, however, one snag remaining: no agreement on whether EU students coming to the UK to study should pay lower tuition fees than currently, and ideally for Brussels, the domestic rate. This aspect is subject to six more months of negotiations, and none of the other deals will be completed until this is also done.

While EU officials are not explicit about this, the assumption is that they are seeking UK tuition fee levels. This would, for Burnham and his ministers, be a tricky sell, especially to the university sector, who currently get fees several times higher than the UK rate for EU students.

For now, there is something of an impasse.

“I think our position has been clear. We think this is an issue that needs to be addressed,” one EU official said.

UK and EU plan first talks on sending refused asylum seekers to third countries

Rajeev SyalandLisa O’Carroll

The UK and the EU plan to establish formal talks for the first time about sending rejected asylum seekers to third-country hubs, according to a leaked document.

A draft of a joint statement expected to be signed by Andy Burnham and Ursula von der Leyen at a summit on Friday morning states that both sides will establish “a structured dialogue” to discuss “returns to third countries”.

Until now, the Home Office has been seeking bilateral agreements with third countries allowing the removal of migrants, but with no obvious success.

Last week, five EU countries – Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands – announced an agreement in principle with Rwanda to establish a migrant “return hub” there in 2027.

Burnham hinted at possible cooperation with EU countries last week when he told reporters that the UK had not had direct discussions with European governments involved in return hubs but “we will watch that [the five-country deal] with interest, learning from what went on in the past”. He said: “The public want us to do whatever we can to deal with illegal immigration.”

The UK has been fighting for deeper cooperation with the EU on migration, with the new deal on a “strategic dialogue” secured in the last two weeks by negotiators in Brussels.

It is a key part of the UK-EU reset pact, which also covers a youth mobility scheme, tariffs on Chinese imports, food and drink exports to the EU and a wider restatement of commitment on defence and security.

Informed sources confirmed this was the first time the UK and the EU had agreed to “open a dialogue on returns” after the launch of the bloc’s Channel action plan in June this year.

Whitehall sources said the UK was now open to the possibility of sending rejected claimants to Rwanda – a recent pivot from the position under Keir Starmer’s government, which rejected the move.

According to a draft of the joint agreement, negotiations over third-country hubs will be part of increased cooperation to tackle irregular migration.

Row over voting rights dispels myths that UK comes to negotiations from position of strength - snap analysis

France’s insistence that the UK could not have voting rights at any stage during the shaping of legislation on food standards and public health risks relating to food and agri products is a line in the sand for Andy Burnham.

The last night tensions at the ambassadors meeting will be a sharp lesson for anyone in the UK who clings to the idea that negotiating with the EU comes from a position of strength: the UK is not a member of the EU and despite the warm feelings to the Labour government in European capitals, benefits of the club are not extended to non members.

It also underlines the feeling expressed by several large member states this week that the way forward after this summit is not more iterative reset summits or mini-deals.

Yes, there will be negotiations on allowing the UK into the energy single market as this is in the joint interests of both sides in the wake of the Ukraine war. But no talks on pharma or chemicals, according to some capitals.

“It puts down a marker warning the UK that cherry picking bits of the single market mean you are a rule taker.”

“It makes the choice for Britain starker and more binary” and shows that “the alternatives are basically muddling along with a version of the status quo or rejoining,” they said.

UK prime minister Andy Burnham will be welcomed with a rendition of God Save the King and the Ode to Joy when he attends the opening ceremony around 10.30am local time.

In what is known as a “silence procedure”, the EU issued a late night compromise text to all member states after a meeting of crunch hours long meeting of ambassadors in Brussels last night.

Under the procedure if no member states breaks the silence the text is considered “adopted” and would then be made available for signing by the UK – bar any last minute hitches on its side.

This morning diplomats said “no one had broken the silence”.

Morning opening: Second post-Brexit EU-UK summit gets under way in Brussels

The EU and UK will hold a long-awaited summit in Brussels on Friday – the second since Brexit – to hammer out the UK’s longer-term relationship with the bloc.

It will be attended by the prime minister, Andy Burnham, the head of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, and the president of the European Council, António Costa.

As our Brussels correspondent, Jennifer Rankin, explained:

“The two sides hope to announce three deals, which were initiated under Starmer’s government.

First, a food and drink agreement that will remove a lot of Brexit red tape on animal and plant products, making it easier to trade meats, cheese, wine and flowers. To secure the deal, which is not expected to take effect until 2028, the UK will align with EU standards, meaning the government, for example, must abandon a planned ban on foie gras imports.

A second agreement will link the British emissions trading system with its bigger EU cousin, restoring a link severed by Brexit. It is a significant step for industry because EU and UK businesses will be exempt from each other’s charges on high-carbon imports such as steel, cement and electricity. This agreement also requires alignment with relevant EU rules.

The third deal has proved the most contentious: a youth mobility scheme that will give those aged 18-31 the right to live, work, travel and study on both sides of the Channel for up to three years. The scheme may not be up and running until 2030 – which is bad news for anyone in their late 20s.”

The leaders are expected to arrive 9.30am GMT (10.30am Brussels time), with “the leaders’ meeting” between Burnham, von der Leyen and Costa to follow.

A plenary session with UK ministers and EU commissioners is expected at 10.45am GMT (11.45am Brussels), and if all goes well we should then see the leaders tell us what they agreed around half past midday (1.30pm Brussels time).
