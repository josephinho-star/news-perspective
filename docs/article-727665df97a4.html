Trump says he will close historic DC golf course for 15 months of renovations

2026-10-18 17:03

President Donald Trump announced Sunday plans to close a historic public golf course in the nation's capital for 15 months and redevelop it, despite an ongoing court challenge to his long-sought renovation.

Trump posted on social media that he intends to shutter the East Potomac Golf Course, not far from the White House, starting Nov. 15, to make it "one of the Best and Most Spectacular Golf Courses anywhere in the World."

A judge last week, however, ordered a halt to his redevelopment plans until at least Oct. 26 after scolding the government for starting a large-scale tree removal at the course over a holiday weekend while providing little notice to the court or the plaintiffs trying to stop the makeover.

Trump did not acknowledge the legal fight in his post, which included an artist's rendering for the course redesign.

"When completed, it will be open to the Public while, at the same time, designed to the Highest Level of Golf and able to hold World Class Tournaments such as the U.S. Open, the PGA Championship, the Ryder Cup, and the Presidents Cup," Trump wrote.

Democracy Forward, which represents the plaintiffs challenging his renovation plans, responded that the course is part of a national park and recreation facility meant for everyone to enjoy.

"Our litigation is intended to protect this community jewel for the people and communities it serves and we will continue to work with our plaintiffs to defend it from unlawful and harmful intrusion," Skye Perryman, the president and CEO of the group, said in a statement Sunday.

Capital makeover extends well beyond the fairways

Trump, a frequent golfer, had already announced plans to makeover the links as part of his broader push to renovate and refurbish places around Washington, including the White House and the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts. Courts have challenged many of his projects, including plans for a huge ballroom at the White House, the installation of his own name at the Kennedy Center, and the construction of a massive arch.

The 106-year-old park and course has drawn many emergency hearings as the president has sought to renovate it. While it has been open to Washingtonians of all income levels, opponents of Trump's plans to turn it into a high-end course argue it will change the space's character and make it less affordable.

In his post, Trump echoed arguments he has made to justify his plans to remake the Kennedy Center, claiming it is in such dangerous condition that it needs to close.

"The current Course is old, broken, dilapidated, and of no further use or effect. It is in very poor condition, with falling, dangerous, and disease infested trees and, importantly, a collapsing and hazardous seawall," Trump wrote.

Debris from the demolition of the White House East Wing to make way for his ballroom project has also been dumped on the grounds. The National Park Service has said the debris tested positive for lead, chromium and other toxic metals.

Trump also sought to justify the National Park Service's removal of hundreds of trees that the judge paused, writing in his announcement that when the golf course was originally built, "it was famous for no trees, with views of Washington, D.C., for all to see, but now, it is completely overgrown, dysfunctional, and unsafe."

Legal case returns to court and the administration seeks to remove the judge

Trump's post outlining a clear construction plan complicates a federal court hearing scheduled for Monday in a lawsuit filed by the DC Preservation League and two golfers opposing the construction.

U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes has ordered key members of the Republican administration to testify about the recent tree-cutting and whether those actions tie to the president's plans for the historic public links.

The administration on Friday asked for Reyes to be replaced on the case, questioning her impartiality and citing what it called "egregious" behavior from the bench in response to an emergency hearing Oct. 12. Her frustration boiled over in a three-hour hearing when she lambasted the government's attorney for the lack of notification she and the plaintiffs received before the trees were chopped down.

At the upcoming hearing, the judge is expected to address her request to step aside. Meanwhile, over the weekend, the plaintiffs asked the court to grant them a longer-term preliminary injunction and/or a stay.
