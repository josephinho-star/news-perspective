As lockout looms, MLB raises minimum salary offer while union eyes media deals

2026-11-23 23:08

In a series of proposals aimed toward making up ground with a lockout looming, Major League Baseball suggested more measures Monday to get players paid earlier in their careers while the MLB Players Association agreed to share local media equally among the 30 teams.

League owners attempted to sweeten their cap-and-floor proposal by increasing the minimum salary to $1 million in 2027 -- a $220,000 bump from 2026 -- and providing centrally funded bonuses to arbitration-eligible players who settle without a hearing and veterans signing one-year contracts.

Those measures, though, are unlikely to create much momentum with a players' union that is categorically opposed to a salary cap and has vehemently turned one down at every juncture.

MLB spokesperson Glen Caplin refuted the rationale as part of his statement on Monday, saying: "The Union's claim that our proposal would negatively impact 'all categories of players' is not accurate. To the contrary, especially in light of today's new proposals to increase the minimum salary, provide an automatic signing bonus for arbitration-eligible players and offer a new veteran signing bonus, MLB's proposals on the table would provide benefits to most players in every stage of their career."

MLBPA interim executive director Bruce Meyer said the league's proposals "don't add a single dollar in overall player compensation," alluding to the fact that all of their concessions are being made within the same cap-and-floor system.

MLB made its initial offer on May 28, suggesting a $245.3 million cap and a $171.2 million floor beginning in 2027. On Nov. 3, MLB proposed pushing it back to 2028, with a projected cap of $258.2 million and a floor of $180.2 million.

The union has repeatedly referred to the cap-and-floor system as a "zero-sum game" in which players compete against one another for finite amounts of money, as opposed to the current free market.

"Any supposed benefits to certain classes of players are simply taken out of the pockets of other players and are vastly outweighed by the negative effects of the cap and MLB's other restrictions," Meyer said in a statement. "These negative effects include, but are not limited to, a drastic reduction in overall player compensation and benefits, the end of fully guaranteed contracts, and dramatic restrictions on players' career choices and freedom to contract."

The current collective bargaining agreement expires at 11:59 p.m. ET on Dec. 1, at which point owners are expected to impose a lockout that would freeze transactions and cut off all communication between teams and major league players.

The previous lockout occurred after the expiration of the last CBA in December of 2021 and lasted 99 days. MLB's insistence on a salary cap, which prompted the cancellation of the 1994 World Series, have people throughout the sport fearing that this lockout could last much longer.

MLB proposed equal distribution of local media as part of its initial cap proposal, adding that the concept would eliminate the territorial exclusivity of regional sports networks and thus do away with blackouts. The MLBPA formally agreed to that form of revenue sharing on Monday, albeit outside of a cap-and-floor system, while maintaining prior proposals aimed toward rewarding low-revenue teams that win.

Under its proposal, the MLBPA says low-revenue teams would receive an increase of more than $1 billion in assistance over the life of a new CBA.

On Monday, the MLBPA proposed an increase from roughly $700 million to more than $950 million for low-revenue markets from 2026 to 2027, while maintaining $20 million monetary rewards for revenue-sharing recipients that either qualify for the postseason or fail to qualify but finish with a better record than the worst playoff team. Revenue-sharing recipients who finish with a record above .500 would receive a $10 million reward under the MLBPA's revenue-sharing model, which originally allowed teams to keep more stadium-related revenue.

In addition to a $1 million minimum salary in 2027, MLB proposed a $100,000 bonus for players who receive a full year of service time and spend less than half the season on the injured list. The league also established a centrally funded signing bonus equal to 5% of an arbitration-eligible player's base salary if he agrees to terms without the need for a hearing. MLB said 93% of players settled their arbitration cases without a hearing under the current CBA.

Under the league's proposal, each team can reward a $500,000 bonus to a veteran player -- one with at least six years of service time -- who signs a one-year contract valued at $10 million or less. MLB once again agreed to increase the pre-arbitration bonus pool by 30%, from $50 million in 2026 to $65 million in 2027, and to reduce free agency to five years for 30-and-older players.

Those players can still be kept by their teams if offered a one-year contract equal to the average salary of the 125 highest-paid players, the same model used to determine qualifying offers under the current system. MLB agreed to eliminate the qualifying offer for six-year free agents.

To the union, though, it's all essentially window dressing at this point.

The overall pot of money for major league players would be fixed under MLB's proposal; the distribution of money is simply different. The league fully acknowledges that is the case. Its hope is to eventually sway a large enough percentage of the player population to attain the cap it so desperately desires. As part of a fact sheet distributed to media members Monday, the league included a chart noting when every player would qualify for free agency under its current proposal.

"Every other major U.S. sport has a salary cap and floor system to limit payroll disparity, and the fact remains that growth in player compensation has been slower in baseball than every one of those leagues," Caplin said in his statement. "MLB is trying to reach an agreement with the players by meeting their objectives on a number of important issues, including earlier access to free agency and increased compensation for players earlier in their careers."
