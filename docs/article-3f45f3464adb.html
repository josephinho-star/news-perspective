Lawyer for media outlets banned by Trump tells court he thought White House letters citing national security grounds were fake

Ted Boutrous, a prominent First Amendment lawyer who represented CNN, Politico and MS NOW in court on Wednesday, reportedly told the judge that, when he first saw three unsigned letters sent to the news organizations on Tuesday, four days after they were banned from the White House by Donald Trump, which attempted to justify the bans on national security grounds, he “didn’t think they were real”, the legal journalist Adam Klasfeld reports.

Boutrous pointed out that the three letters – to CNN, Politico and MS NOW – were unsigned and not on White House letterhead. All three cited articles published by the outlets months ago, most of them written by reporters who do not have passes to the White House.

The letters, Boutrous said, looked a “post hoc” attempt to justify the bans that the president himself told reporters on Friday were in response to overly negative coverage of his performance as president, by grafting on national security grounds.

In a supplemental declaration filed with the court Sudeep Reddy, MS NOW’s Washington bureau chief, pointed out that only one of the three articles cited in the White House press office letter, a report published on 11 June 2026 by Jake Traylor, “was written by a journalist who held a White House hard pass.”

Reddy also noted that the broadcaster submitted a request for the renewal of Traylor’s hard pass on 18 June 2026, a week later, “and his hard pass was subsequently renewed.”

Federal court hearing on Trump's 'ban on the free press' concludes

A federal court in Washington just finished hearing arguments in the lawsuit over Donald Trump’s self-described “ban on the free press,” in which he barred three news organizations – CNN, MS Now and Politico – from the White House over what he considers overly negative coverage of his work as a president.

C-SPAN posted an audio stream of most of the court hearing on YouTube, which has now, sadly, been removed, but before it was, the judge overseeing the case promised to issue a ruling soon on the request from the media organizations to have the credentials of the outlets restored while the court case goes on.

Judge Timothy Kelly, a former federal prosecutor nominated to the US district court for the District of Columbia in 2017 by Trump, is hearing the arguments from the government and the news organizations. In 2018, Kelly ordered the White House to restore the press pass of a CNN reporter, Jim Acosta, after he challenged Trump at a news conference and was briefly banned.

CNN’s chief media correspondent, Brian Stelter, reports that Kelly “began the hearing Wednesday by telling attorneys that the White House did not give reporters appropriate due process before revoking their access to the building, running against previous court decisions.”

Kelly himself previously held that the White House cannot suspend or revoke a journalist’s access without notice or giving them a chance to appeal that decision on due process grounds.

That process “wasn’t played out here,” Kelly said. “I just say to both sides that’s where I am,” Kelly said. “Before we get to any core First Amendment issues, there is a due process claim and a due process issue that seems to me the first stop in figuring out how to resolve the motion.”

Zelenskyy, speaking to the United Nations, tries to rally support for a grinding war

Speaking to the United Nations annual assembly of world leaders for the fifth time since the invasion of his country, Volodymyr Zelenskyy sought to rally global support for the defense of Ukraine from Russia.

Zelenskyy warned that Putin would not be appeased by a partial victory in Ukraine, and would continue pursuing conquest in Ukraine even if granted a piece of its territory.

Bernie Sanders introduces bill to “ban artificial superintelligence”

Bernie Sanders, the left-wing independent Senator from Vermont who has quickly become one of the loudest AI-skeptical voices in US politics, announced on Wednesday new legislation that would, in his words, “ban artificial superintelligence.”

“The leading experts are clear: AI is rapidly escaping our control,” Sanders wrote in a post to social media announcing the bill. “Congress must act NOW. Today I’m introducing legislation to permanently ban artificial superintelligence and pause advanced AI until clear safety rules are in place.”

The Associated Press reported earlier this morning that the legislation – backed in the House of Representatives by Texas Democrat Greg Cesar – would pause the development of frontier AI until a new “Department of Artificial Intelligence” could establish guardrails around the industry.

It would also ban, in perpetuity, “artificial superintelligence,” a category that includes AI systems that exceed the limits of human intelligence or have “sufficient capabilities to plan and execute the destruction or disempowerment of humanity,” per AP.

Maine Democrats seize on pay-for-play donation allegations against Collins

The allegations, first reported in a ProPublica story yesterday, were the latest development in an already chaotic Maine Senate race: that Maine GOP senator Susan Collins’ campaign had accepted large donations from a defense contractor in return for Collins steering government funds to the company.

A day later, Maine Democrats are centering those allegations as they seek to win Collins’ seat in the November midterm elections.

“I cannot stress enough today the seriousness of these allegations,” Devon Murphy-Anderson, executive director of the Maine Democratic Party, said at a party press conference called to highlight the investigation.

Collins, Murphy-Anderson said, had “sold us out yet again, and gotten bailed out of trouble by the President of the United States. It is not the first time she has betrayed us. However, this November we have the opportunity to ensure it is one of the last.”

Collins’s team has fiercly denied the allegations, which emerged from an FBI investigation into Martin Kao, former executive of the defense contractor Navakek. Kao, who detailed the alleged scheme to the FBI, has since been convicted and sentenced to prison on unrelated charges.

ProPublica reports that the FBI has been investigating Collins as part of a wide-ranging probe into members of Congress, but that the inquiry died as part of the Trump administration’s restructuring of the FBI.

Troy Jackson, Collins’ opponent for her Maine Senate seat in November, has also seized on the corruption allegations, implying that Collins has been deferential to Trump because his FBI helped kill the probe into her conduct. (Jackson was chosen by the Maine Democrats to replace their previous nominee, one-time progressive darling Graham Platner, after allegations of sexual assault led him to terminate his campaign.)

Trump administration drafting up plans for 90-day embargo on diesel exports: report

The Trump administration is readying to launch a 90-day ban on diesel fuel exports from the United States, following mounting calls from his party to take action to bring down the fuel’s price ahead of the midterm elections, POLITICO reported Wednesday.

Several people with inside knowledge of the conversations told POLITICO (currently one of three outlets banned from the White House by Trump) that the plan has created a schism within pro- and anti-embargo camps in the administration. It’s also prompted furor from the oil and gas industry that has broadly supported the president.

One oil industry executive privy to the conversations told the outlet that Trump seemed intent on announcing a ban by the end of the week.

The price of fuel has weighed heavily on Republicans in the lead-up to the midterms, which will determine whether Trump’s party retains control of Congress for the second half of his term. Trump ran in 2024 in part on bringing down the price of gas, but the war he helped kick off between the US and Iran, and the closure of vital oil shipping arteries it prompted, has sent the cost of fuel skyrocketing.

Diesel, in particular, has become a sore spot. The fuel for everything from semi-trucks to tractors to heavy machinery, it has surged in price even more than standard gasoline, to more than $6.50 per gallon.

That’s prompted some Republicans, especially those representing farming-heavy states, to publicly call on the president to take drastic action to lower the price of diesel.

The pushback, POLITICO reports, is coming from oil and gas executives and administration officials who warn that the embargo could raise fuel prices in the long run.

'I am the code': Trump reportedly clashed with White House ballroom architect after dismissing safety concerns

The Washington Post (paywall) reports today that James McCrery II, the first architect on Donald Trump’s controversial $400m White House ballroom project, resigned after repeatedly clashing with the president, who dismissed his warnings that design features Trump was insisting on violated building safety codes.

McCrery withdrew from the project last October after warning Trump that a preliminary design he wanted “did not provide adequate emergency exit routes or fire-containment measures”, according to the Post’s report, which cites two people with knowledge of the discussions and corroborating documents reviewed by the paper.

The sources said Trump brushed off the architect’s concerns, and argued that no building codes apply to White House construction. “I am the code,” Trump reportedly said.

The Post previously reported that they had also clashed over the size of the ballroom. One document obtained by the paper features an image of a planned data center overlaid on a map of Manhattan, with a note that appears to be poking fun at McCrery’s concerns. “James,” it reads. “And you think we are big.”

Another note made in Sharpie makes clear Trump’s preference for Corinthian-style columns.

When approached by the Post, McCrery didn’t say why he stepped down. A White House official said it was “factually false and totally inaccurate” to suggest he left the project over issues with the code, and said he was a “valued member of the initial design team.”

Discussions about the safety issues continued for months after McCrery’s departure, according to White House records reviewed by the Post.

It is not clear from those records whether the safety issues he identified have been resolved in more recent designs by Shalom Baranes Associates, the architecture firm that took over McCrery’s firm’s role in the project late last year.

The ballroom project is – unusually – being overseen by the Executive Residence, rather than the General Services Administration or National Park Service. The small office is exempt from rules that require federal agencies to solicit competitive bids and disclose details to the public, the Post previously reported. Typically, it’s responsible for routine repairs, entertainment expenses, and the purchase of furniture, art and other items for the executive mansion.
