Five-Om! Buddhist monk-turned-NYPD cop promoted to one of department’s highest ranking jobs

2026-11-26 20:26

A Buddhist monk-turned-NYPD cop — who was inspired to spread “empathy” on the force in the wake of the Sept. 11, 2001, terror attacks — has been promoted to one of the department’s highest ranking jobs.

Tawee Theanthong, 53, was bumped up to inspector of his Bronx precinct at a ceremony this week that honored the unconventional career path that took him from robe to uniform.

During his climb through the ranks of the NYPD, the Queens-born father of four said he has used the “philosophies” he learned as a 22-year-old monk fasting in rural Thailand to become a better cop.

“Empathy and compassion is something that we really should focus more on [as officers],” Theanthong told The Post after the promotion ceremony at One Police Plaza Tuesday.

“We always look at the numbers, the crime, the statistics… we don’t always talk about the people side of that, the human element.”

“What do they feel, what do they fear… I think that that’s just as important to crime fighting as the numbers,” he added.

Born the son of Thai immigrants, Theanthong — whose recent busts have included that of an alleged crew of moped-riding jewelry thieves — was inspired to move to Thailand and become a monk during a period of post-college self-reflection in order to connect with his ancestral culture.

In 1995, he traveled to a small village near the Mekong River, where he joined a monastery and dedicated himself to learning moral conduct, meditation and the teachings of Buddha.

He shaved his head and eyebrows, dressed in a robe, and was provided with just a single possession — a bowl — as is customary in monk life.

He was instructed to eat only when villagers donated food — a practice that allows monks to rely on the good will of others.

‘When you join a monastery, when you are ordained as a monk, you give up everything. You’re no longer a civilian, so that is pretty much one of the ultimate sacrifices because you give up all material goods, your names, your titles, everything,” Theanthong said.

“These are the lessons that kind of humbled me.”

Theanthong said that spiritual journey helps him remember that his priority as a cop should be helping people.

“I also use a lot of the philosophies behind that, especially of empathy and compassion, and we lose sight of that [as cops] sometimes because we’re so busy,” he said.

“We run around from job to job and to us that might be the 10th job of the day,” he said. “But to that person, that’s the most important thing that happened that day —and how we respond to that will dictate the entire course of how they were received.”

After 10 months in Thailand, Theanthong returned to the US and got a job in tech in the 1990s — before the terrorist attacks of 9/11 inspired him to serve his hometown by joining the NYPD.

“After 9/11, you know, that pretty much hit us very hard. I was working in the city at the time, and I said, ‘You know what? If there’s ever a time that I was going to join a job or do something that was going to help people, this would be the time,” he said.

Theanthong began training at the Police Academy in 2003 and was soon assigned to his first beat patrolling New York City Housing Authority buildings in the Bronx.

Over the past 20-some years, he went on to solve cases on Detective Squads in the Bronx for neighborhoods including Morris Park and Woodlawn. He also served on the Bronx Robbery Squad before becoming a Deputy Inspector in March 2025.

Trading an orange robe for a blue uniform also came with some big changes, he said.

“Being a cop is different because we’re in such a closed environment, and that camaraderie is more kind of ingrained, so you do find more of the family or fraternal style relationships in police than you do with the monk [life],” he said.

Theanthong, the commanding officer of the NYPD’s 43rd Precinct, was joined by his wife, Lek, and four sons, Thomas, Timothy, Titus and Tate, at Tuesday’s promotion ceremony.

“The funny thing about us as cops is you could probably just give us our shield in the mail, and we’d be fine with it,” he said. “But what makes it special is that it brings in your families.

“When our family recognizes us, then it becomes that much more special,” he said.
