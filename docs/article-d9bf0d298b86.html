Gavin Newsom signs bill to create new film and TV tax credit in California

2026-09-19 22:42

Gov. Gavin Newsom is offering a multimillion-dollar tax break to Hollywood producers as California scrambles to keep entertainment jobs from fleeing the state.

The push comes after President Trump proposed a federal film incentive and as a bitter fight over whether Paramount could take its business elsewhere continues to play out.

Newsom signed Assembly Bill 2319 on Friday at the Television Academy in North Hollywood, creating California’s first standalone tax credit specifically for post-production work. The program will offer a 35% to 50% credit on qualified California expenses for picture editing, sound, music, visual effects and finishing.

The move comes weeks after President Trump met with former LA mayoral candidate Spencer Pratt to discuss a proposed 25% federal film and television tax credit aimed at bringing production jobs back to California. Pratt subsequently said he was “working with the President” on the proposal to “bring back Hollywood.” Trump has since publicly backed a federal film tax incentive.

Newsom’s new credit takes a different approach, targeting the work that happens after cameras stop rolling. Unlike California’s existing film and television incentive, productions receiving the new post-production credit do not have to shoot in the Golden State.

The existing program can cover post-production costs when at least 75% of filming or the overall budget is spent in California.

The tax break arrives as California’s grip on the post-production industry has weakened. The state’s share of US post-production employment has fallen from 53% to 42% over the past 13 years, according to CVL Economics, with roughly 12,000 post-production jobs remaining in California last year.

For workers caught in Hollywood’s employment crunch, the downturn has hit particularly hard.

Culver City resident Ben Urquhart, 51, told the Times he spent 18 years as a post-production executive at NBCUniversal before being laid off. Two and a half years later, he said he still hasn’t found another job.

“It’s grim and it’s hard. There are jobs, but we have a large amount of extremely qualified people competing for every level of job,” Urquhart said.

The new program, however, is starting considerably smaller than supporters originally envisioned. Assemblymember Nick Schultz, a Burbank Democrat who authored the bill, initially sought $100 million for the credit, with the Motion Picture Editors Guild backing the larger figure. Lawmakers ultimately approved $10 million, one-tenth of the original proposal.

The push comes as Sacramento has been throwing more money at an entertainment industry that has increasingly taken its tax breaks and jobs elsewhere. California recently more than doubled its broader Film and Television Tax Credit Program from $330 million to $750 million annually, with the increased funding authorized through June 30, 2030.

At the same time, Newsom and California Attorney General Rob Bonta have found themselves on different sides of the increasingly heated Paramount-Warner Bros. battle.

Bonta is leading California and 11 other states in a lawsuit seeking to block David Ellison’s $111 billion takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, while Newsom has urged Bonta to pursue an out-of-court settlement, citing concerns about the potential impact on California’s entertainment jobs.

The California Post has reached out to Bonta’s office to confirm the negotiations.

Newsom’s latest tax-credit push underscores the broader scramble to keep Hollywood work in California as lawmakers, studios and the White House pursue competing ways to bring production and jobs back.

“This is a big victory in our fight to save California’s entertainment industry, and we’re just getting started,” Schultz said. “This program will literally save jobs here in Los Angeles and across the state.”

Newsom’s office said 170 projects announced since last year’s expansion of the state’s incentive program are expected to generate more than $6.6 billion in economic activity and nearly 35,000 cast and crew jobs statewide.

Newsom also signed Senate Bill 186, which makes changes to the existing incentive program through enhanced refundability and relief for certain independent productions beginning in 2027.

The governor capped Friday’s Hollywood push with a visit to the Burbank set of HBO Max’s “The Pitt” as California tries to keep more of the entertainment industry’s work in the Golden State.
