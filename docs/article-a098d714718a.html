Army detonates nearly 5 tons of lead azide at Iowa ammunition plant

2026-11-25 15:10

After multiple delays and a federal court intervention, the U.S. Army has detonated nearly 5 tons of degraded lead azide at its ammunition plant in Middletown, Iowa.

Two explosions could be heard in the vicinity of the site, according to a video feed.

A senior official within the Trump administration told Fox News that the planned explosion "exceeded all expectations" and that "maximum mitigation" was achieved. That was followed up by a short Truth Social post from President Donald Trump himself, in which he said early reports showed mitigation efforts had been successful.

Missouri Governor Mike Kehoe confirmed that the explosion had taken place about 15 minutes after it was heard. Updated Army maps had projected the blast's plume could drift southwest into the northeastern corner of Kehoe's state.

"Initial analysis shared with us by federal partners and Iowa Homeland Security and Emergency Management indicates that the mitigation measures appear to have been successful and the explosion was well-contained," Kehoe wrote in a social media post.

Ahead of the blast, residents and environmental advocates had warned that detonating the bomb-making materials at the plant risked blanketing the surrounding community in toxic lead dust. To prevent this, the Army built extensive barriers and deployed specialized gels to absorb the fallout.

As of 11 a.m. ET, none of the EPA's four live air monitors in the expected plume path have detected any lead in their readings.

After the detonation, FEMA said that residents within 20 miles of the blast site who have not evacuated "should continue to shelter in place" and follow EPA safety guidance.

FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton told Fox News there was a "sigh of relief" at the Army's command center following the explosion, which he said was likely effective at containing lead particles.

"From what we see now, it doesn't appear that there's a big lead plume that was in the projection models," Hamilton said, adding that the Army's mitigation efforts to contain the blast "were very, very successful."

He said that in the coming days and weeks, various federal agencies will be taking air and soil samples, while officials with the Department of Agriculture will visit nearby farms to carry out impact assessments.

Officials with the Army and other federal agencies had agreed that a controlled detonation of the 68 barrels of lead azide, a highly volatile explosive material, was safer and far preferable than a spontaneous explosion.

Such a blast, officials said, was possible at any moment because the lead azide had crystallized. The crystals are extremely sensitive to heat, shock and friction, and officials warned that freezing temperatures — like those that hit Middletown on Wednesday — could further destabilize the material and trigger an explosion.

The lead azide was first brought to the munitions plant in Middletown at some point in 2009, according to ATF Director Robert Cekada.

The Army discovered that it had begun to crystallize during a routine inspection in March, Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle has said.

The Army initially considered hiring a private firm to neutralize the explosives using chemicals, as well as options for thermal treatment or relocation. However, officials determined that opening the unstable barrels to add chemicals risked triggering an immediate explosion, leading them to pursue a controlled detonation instead.

On Nov. 13, the EPA granted an emergency permit authorizing a detonation through February 11, 2027.

After the Army set a date for Nov. 20, the Iowa Environmental Council and the Iowa chapter of the Sierra Club sued the EPA, which put the detonation plans on hold.

Trump ordered the Army not to proceed without court approval, which it got on Monday, when the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals lifted its stay.
