‘Monster: The Lizzie Borden Story’ Episode 6 Recap: The Houseguest

2026-09-20 17:35

I cried during this episode of Netflix’s Monster: The Lizzie Borden Story. More than once. First I cried when Aileen Wuornos described her childhood of brutal physical and sexual abuse to her hallucinated idol, Lizzie Borden. Then I cried when Bridget and Lizzie shared one last dance to “Smalltown Boy,” Bronski Beat’s mournful dance classic about an ostracized young gay man leaving home, while Aileen Wuornos and her girlfriend Shelby somehow join in.

I found this material deeply affecting. It made me sad to think about how some people have to feel the way these people were made to feel. It made me think how beautiful any moment of escape can be, whether through music or sex. (Murder is just the Monster-verse’s misguided attempt to find that kind of transcendence through violence.) I thought these scenes were beautiful. They moved me to tears.

I’m saying all this up front just so you know if you’re in the right place or not. If you find this kind of true-crime material distasteful, if you find Ryan Murphy (who does not appear to have been particularly involved in this season, certainly not as a writer) distasteful, if these factors are enough to preclude you from enjoying the show, I understand. I also disagree, and that’s the perspective from which I’m writing. Monster is a surreal exploration of life as an outsider as reflected in the lives of famous killers, their victims, and the legends surrounding them all; it moves me to tears, and to me, that’s worth both examining and celebrating.

This episode’s Lizzie-centric material concerns her and Bridget’s attempts to cover their tracks in the wake of the Bordens’ deaths. Some of this she does rather effectively — faking a nervous breakdown so convincingly she’s given multiple morphine doses to calm her, colluding with her uncle John Morse to devise an alibi together in exchange for a loan for his butcher business. Other elements, like the bloody handkerchief she passes off as a feminine napkin and burning her bloody clothes in the oven while the house is full of cops, she pulls off with less aplomb.

For a while, at least, the killers have sexism on their side. An initial examination of the bodies leads the police to conclude from the ferocity and number of axe blows incurred that only a man, maybe even only multiple men, could have committed these crimes. Lizzie’s inconsistent statement about the events of the the day are swallowed hook, line, and sinker, even as Lizzie herself comes to regret her father’s death. (Not Abby’s, though: “Cunt,” she spits at her corpse.)

But the misdirection only works for a while. A gratuitously, gloriously gruesome sequence shows the bodies of Abby and Andrew Borden (Rebecca Hall and Charlie Hunnam are frightfully good sports) getting hacked to pieces, boiled, flayed, scraped, and generally manhandled. It’s disgusting. If you’re the right kind of horror person, it’s awesome.

Whatever the Harvard boys responsible for this forensic examination dig up, it’s enough to pin it on Lizzie, who’s so prepared for her own arrest she already has her things packed. Bridget, meanwhile, has flown the coop — somewhat literally, as the last place she and Lizzie meet is the dovecote where Lizzie cooped up her beloved birds. That’s where they dance to “Smalltown Boy,” to my eternal gratitude. (Ryan Murphy projects know their way around early-‘80s electro-pop.)

This gets us back to the episode’s central conceit and main attraction: an entirely apocryphal visit to the Lizzie Borden house, rendered here as a bed and breakfast, by Alieen Wuornos and Shelby, the show’s name for her girlfriend.

You can tell right away this is a transcendent experience for Aileen. She’s eager to see everything, touch everything, smoke and fuck in every room in the house. The smoking is technically forbidden, not that it stops her, but there’s nothing in the rulebook that says you can’t fingerbang on the legendary fainting couch.

As their visit progresses, Aileen shoots up in honor of Lizzie’s morphine doses, then has Shelby read to her from her Lizzie biography. Shelby tells the story like a tale of ghosts around the campfire, leaning hard on the thrills and chills, as well as the doomed romance. It’s really rather charming.

It builds to the episode’s most moving scene. Stopping storytime to go take a leak (I applaud Monster’s commitment to the aspects of human existence we hide behind closed doors), Aileen encounters and communes with the beatific spirit of Lizzie herself. Lizzie cries single tears as Aileen explains who she is, what she does for a living, whom she’s killed and why, and whom she never had the chance to murder even though they treated her even worse than Lizzie’s did: her family. Everyone she knew beat or raped her, not just her father and stepmom, but her brother, even her granddad. Aileen’s killings are the venting of a lifetime of displaced rage. To this, Lizzie extends a loving, forgiving hand.

When Shelby finds Aileen the next morning, she’s sitting in an empty room, visiting with no one and nothing. It’s a supremely unnerving visual, because it speaks to the depths of Aileen’s mental illness (she mentions a schizophrenia diagnosis and she’s clearly unmedicated); it’s also a sad one, because this momentous occasion in her life happened only in her head.

But then, everyone on that show has that in common. Andrew, Abby, Bridget, Lizzie: All of them are living lives in their heads that they see as superior to the ones they lead in real life. They spend so much time there, in fact, that their relationship with reality becomes poisonous, even violent — a twisted tree, to borrow Andrew’s memorable image for Lizzie. There’s a belief that people improve through adversity, and this is sometimes true, but people worsen through adversity as well. That’s unpleasant to face. That’s Monster.
