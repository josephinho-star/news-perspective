Flight passengers clash over armrest space as shrinking economy seats leave almost no room

2026-11-28 11:00

As economy seats shrink to 17 inches across on some airplanes, flight passengers are clashing over armrests, policies and the space available to them.

Flyers have less patience for declining etiquette and armrest disputes, a recent survey from The Points Guy and YouGov reported.

When taller or wider passengers encroach on a neighbor's space, frustrated travelers are increasingly taking to social media to air grievances.

"The seats are getting smaller and smaller. I've experienced it myself," Florida-based etiquette expert and former flight attendant Jacqueline Whitmore told Fox News Digital.

"I always ask for an aisle seat instead of a middle seat or a window seat because you just never know who you're going to be seated next to," she continued.

A recent Reddit post criticizing larger fellow passengers was upvoted over 4,000 times.

After being seated next to a "larger passenger" on six out of eight flights, one Reddit user had had enough.

"I am not the person who wants to shame or insult anyone’s body, especially not on a flight where we’re all uncomfortable and packed in like sardines," the Reddit user wrote.

"But I am struggling with the fact that there seems to be little done at the policy level to ensure comfort in these situations," the poster said.

"On my most recent flight, I was in an aisle [seat] with a larger passenger in the middle. The [person] encroached completely into my space to the point where my entire shoulder and one of my legs HAD to go in the aisle," the Reddit user continued.

Many Reddit users agreed with the overall sentiment.

"I never say anything because I don’t want to be mean or make anyone feel bad, but I really don’t like a stranger in 1/3 of my seat," a commenter wrote.

Another quipped, "Yeah, this happened to me recently on a 5-hour flight — a very large passenger [was] in the middle seat. I had [the] aisle, but [the] poor girl in the window was so smashed into the armrest."

In July, United Airlines unveiled an economy offering on its new Airbus A321XLR aircraft that will give passengers some extra elbow room access to a shared table across an open middle seat, as Fox Business reported.

The company said it expects it will be the only airline offering this seating option.

"We're investing nose-to-tail across our fleet and giving customers choice and value in every cabin," Andrew Nocella, United's chief commercial officer, said in the company's announcement.

"The XLR is our newest aircraft and not only offers all-aisle access lie-flat seats in United Polaris but now also includes seats in Economy Plus with extra leg and elbow room."

The question is whether other airlines will follow suit.

"We're investing nose-to-tail across our fleet and giving customers choice and value in every cabin."

Whitmore, for her part, urged travelers to remain calm when a situation arises.

"If you end up in a middle seat, and you're sitting next to a passenger who might be spilling over onto you, unfortunately, there's not a lot that you can do," she said.

Passengers who have issues should speak up before the plane doors close, Whitmore said.

"You can just say, 'I'm not very comfortable in my seat. Do you have any other options for me?'" she noted.

If the flight is fully booked, however, seat swaps are rarely an option, Whitmore said.

Published airline policies indicate that several carriers require passengers who cannot fit within one seat with the armrests down to purchase a second seat.

In March, a Southwest seating policy — which had recently been launched — ignited a similar debate.

The policy asks passengers who need extra space to purchase a second seat in advance.

It also allows the airline to decide on a case-by-case basis — citing safety reasons and overall passenger comfort.

"We began communicating changes to our policy — which is in line with industry standards — almost a year ago," Southwest Airlines told Fox News Digital at the time.

Some travelers feel airlines have made seats smaller to sell more tickets and upgrades.

Whitmore said it comes down to real estate.

"They can't move you if there is nowhere to move," she said.

"If you haven't paid for a more expensive seat with more legroom, they're technically not allowed to move you because those seats are reserved for people who have paid the extra money."

Fox News Digital reached out to Airlines for America, as well as numerous airlines, for comment.

Eric Revell of Fox News Digital contributed reporting.
