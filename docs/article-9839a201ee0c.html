No 10 insists RAF Fairford is safe after US withdraws its bombers – UK politics live

2026-10-05 12:58

No 10 insists RAF Fairford is safe after US withdraws its bombers

Downing Street has insisted RAF Fairford is safe after the US withdrew its bombers from the base over new security concerns, the Press Association reports. PA says:

The Pentagon confirmed yesterday that aircraft which were being stationed at the Gloucestershire base had been “redeployed to their home stations in the United States”.

The move follows the arrest of six individuals over the past week in connection with a suspected terror plot targeting the base.

US officials told the New York Times the redeployment of the bombers, which have been involved in carrying out strikes on Iran, had been ordered after more specific details about threats to the base emerged over the weekend.

Asked at at lobby briefing this morning whether RAF Fairford was safe, the PM’s spokesperson said: “Yes.”

He pointed to comments by defence secretary Wes Streeting saying there were “tried and tested approaches to defend our bases around the country and throughout the world”, but he declined to comment further on “foreign nations’ military operations”.

He added: “I would just point you to the president’s words last week, where he praised our police, defence and security services in relation to RAF Fairford.”

The five British nationals, all in their mid-20s, arrested near the base on September 27 have since been released on bail.

A sixth man, a 25-year-old dual UK-Iranian national, arrested on October 1, has also been released on bail.

What impact will Green's party's 'Zionism is racism' vote have on Zack Polanski's byelection prospects?

Earlier I said that yesterday’s Green party vote for the “Zionism is racism” motion would not help Zack Polanski’s chances of winning the Holborn and St Pancras byelection. (See 12.26pm.) A reader has been in touch with a counter view. He says:

I think it’s worth noting that the displeasure of the commentariat/politicians doesn’t necessarily translate to voters - the last few elections have shown that with regard to Gaza/Palestine - and given that this electorate ‘has exceptionally high shares of twentysomethings, single person households, full time students, graduates, middle class professionals, social renters, private renters, residents born abroad’, it’s not unreasonable to assume that those people might be more disposed to vote Green than less.

It is hard to be sure, and I don’t live in the constituency, but I think there are three reasons I think why my assessment is more likely to be right than this one.

1) While there are many voters in the UK outraged by what has been happening to Palestinians in Gaza, and disappointed by what the UK government has been doing in response, the number of people in this group who feel exercised about defining Zionism as racism is far smaller. And while the Greens may have increased their appeal to committed anti-Zionists, they are likely to have alienated progressive pro-Palestinians averse to aligning with antisemitism. There are probably more people in the latter group than in the former – even in Holborn and St Pancras.

2) Zack Polanski did not want to be talking about Zionism in the final days of his campaign. Yesterday, as the Green party debate was taking place, he was tweeting about “rent controls, Thames water nationalisation and keeping oil and gas in the ground” (some of his best issues). Having to answer questions about the Zionism vote is a distraction.

3) The row has made Polanski look weak. He can’t take credit for the motion, because he did not vote for it and backed an an amendment to weaken it slightly. And he can’t take credit for opposing it either, because he hasn’t. People probably like having a party leader as their local MP. But this week Polanski has not looked like a leader.

Readers probably have other views. If you do, please let me know by emailing politics.live@theguardian.com.

Investors criticise Tories for proposing to get rid of regulations making new homes environmentally resilient

The UK Sustainable Investment and Finance Association (UKSIF), which represents fund managers committed to sustainable investment, has issued a statement criticising the Conservatives for proprosing to get rid of regulations that make new buildings environmentally resilient. (See 8.08am.)

In a statement, James Alexander, the UKSIF CEO, said:

The UK’s ageing, inefficient housing stock poses a risk to families’ well-being and the long-term financial stability of the property market.

That’s why investors have backed both strong standards for new homes and plans to upgrade existing properties, which can help to tackle this looming crisis.

Rolling out technologies like solar panels and efficient heating systems can cut energy bills while creating high-quality jobs in supply chains across the country.

Suddenly ripping up regulation designed to enhance property resilience could weaken the financing environment for housebuilding and the industries that support it.

'There can be no economic growth on dead planet' - Greens accuse Tories of ignoring reality of climate crisis

The Green party has accused the Tories of ignoring the reality of the climate crisis. In a response to the measures announced by Andrew Griffith (see 8.08am), the Green MP Ellie Chowns said:

It is completely outrageous that, as evidence of climate breakdown and environmental collapse becomes ever-more impossible to ignore, the Conservatives would prefer to tear up environmental standards, weaken climate protection, and abolish organisations charged with protecting nature, rather than tackle the genuine issues affecting building in Britain.

Head of Jewish Greens says she may leave party over ‘Zionism is racism’ vote

Vivien Lichtenstein, the head of the Jewish Greens, has said she may leave the party after it voted to adopt a policy that equates Zionism with racism and calls for Israel to be replaced by a single Palestinian state, Peter Walker reports.

On Thursday voters in Holborn and St Pancras in London will vote in a byelection to replace Keir Starmer. It has been a safe Labour seat for years, and Sagal Abdi-Wali, who came to the UK as a child refugee from Somalia and who is now leader of Camden council is the party’s candidate to replace the ex-PM. But she is facing a tough challenge from Zack Polanski, the Green party leader.

It is hard to find a better guide to a byelection than Rob Ford, the politics professor and elections specialist, and he has written a long piece about the contest on his Substack blog. It is well worth reading, and here is his conclusion.

Burnham’s extended honeymoon with the electorate, and the Greens’ decline in the polls mean Labour go into this contest as heavy favourites. After all, if the Greens could not take Camden when Labour were hitting rock bottom with Starmer, what chance do they have now? But by-elections have a track record of delivering surprises, particularly when high profile figures are on the ballot. If Zack Polanski and his army of Green activists can convince local voters that the contest represents a low risk opportunity to send a warning shot across the bows of the new Labour leadership, they may yet be able to mobilise lingering distrust of Labour among voters still angry at being marginalised during the Starmer years.

It is rumoured that Dominic Cummings wanted to use the slogan “Tell Them Again” in the event of a second EU referendum. A similar message could yet reignite the smouldering resentment of young progressives still sore at the perceived betrayals of the Starmer years, and help Zack Polanski win against the odds next week. Burnham’s rise changed the political weather this summer. But the weather could yet change again next week.

Although Ford published his article today, it does not refer to yesterday’s Green party vote saying “Zionism is racism”, which will probably be bad for Polanski’s chances. Ford’s analysis is in part based on the fact that the constituency has unusual demographics. “The seat has exceptionally high shares of twentysomethings, single person households, full time students, graduates, middle class professionals, social renters, private renters, residents born abroad,” he says.

Shadow Chancellor Andrew Griffith has told the Tory conference that he would “scrap unnecessary net-zero housing red tape” and cut the cost of “delivering and therefore buying a new home by up to £50,000.”

How would the Tories guarantee that developers pass on these supposed savings to homebuyers?

Andy Burnham has been urged to back compulsory voting as a means of helping to overcome opposition to his proposal to make the pensions triple lock less generous.

The argument is made in a report published by the Fairness Foundation that has been written by Charles White, who runs the Campaign for Compulsory Voting.

In her foreword to the report, Polly Neate, a crossbench peer and former CEO of Shelter, said:

The pensions triple lock is protected by voter power. That’s how politics works, but it isn’t how democracy should work, and it certainly doesn’t give fairness a chance.

Now the prime minister has touched the third rail of British politics by daring to fix this unfairness, he needs voters on side. Compulsory voting would give Burnham the political cover and popular support to deliver. The people most likely to back reform, including younger voters, would be at the ballot box to reward a government that took on intergenerational inequality. Compulsory voting brings everyone into the decision, those directly affected and those who aren’t, encouraging a more considered response to major policy choices.

Burnham has said that he will set up a commission on electoral reform. He has not explicitly said that he wants it to look at compulsory voting, but there is increasing interest in the idea in progressive circles.

Scottish government on course to miss its child poverty targets 'by miles', thinktank says

John Swinney has been warned of the “increasing disconnect” between his words and his actions on child poverty, as the government looks set to miss legally-binding targets on the issue “by miles”, the Press Association reports. PA says:

In a highly critical report, the Joseph Rowntree Foundation (JRF) said a “shocking” 21% of children are growing up in poverty despite the first minister’s defining promise to eradicate it.

The Poverty in Scotland 2026 report warned that without “radical change”, Scotland will miss its legally binding target to cut relative child poverty to below 10% by 2030/31 by a “wide margin”.

The Scottish government previously missed its legal targets for 2023-2024, although the child poverty rate did fall.

The JRF said ministers need a much bolder plan to tackle poverty.

It said the government’s plan to review its targets “risks delaying action and lowering ambitions” as it called for new, stretching ones to be created.

The report said: “We have consistently heard the first minister describe eradicating poverty as his defining mission.

This commitment is welcome, but there is an increasing disconnect between the Scottish government’s rhetoric and the reality of life for families in poverty across Scotland.”

It called for ministers to bring forward a “new, much bolder and properly funded plan to reduce poverty in Scotland”, and reform local government funding to ensure that “services are best where they are needed the most”.

Chris Birt, associate director for Scotland at the JRF, said: “We are on course to miss the legally binding targets by miles, but parliament can choose to up the ante and provide challenging new targets that re-energise government efforts.”

Tories will abolish Climate Change Committee, shadow energy secretary Andrew Bowie says

Andrew Bowie, the shadow energy secretary, has said that the Tory plan to dismantled the net zero legislation will include gettting rid of the Climate Change Committee.

In his speech to the conference this morning, he said:

What is the difference between a grid that prioritises cheap power for Britain over a grid nearly entirely reliant on renewables?

Well we’ve done the work: it’s £320bn.

Conference, cheap power is crucial. For industry, for the British people.

At conference last year we announced the measures we would take to cut bills right now.

Removing VAT from energy bills – saving £94

Axing the Carbon Tax on electricity generation. A cut of £75.

Ditching Ed Miliband’s renewable subsidies - £22.

A total package to slash bills by £200 per year.

And we’ll go further.

We’ll scrap GB Energy.

We’ll scrap the auction rounds for offshore wind that tie us to higher and higher prices.

We’ll abolish the climate change committee that instructs the British public how often they should go on holiday, farmers what they should plant, commuters what they should drive and the government what they should tax.

And we’ll repeal Ed Miliband’s Climate Change Act and net zero 2050.
