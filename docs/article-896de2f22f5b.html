Stressed grad student, 31, fined for crying on playground bench in Germany without a kid: report

2026-11-27 23:29

A stressed-out master’s student in Germany was fined by park enforcement officers — for crying alone on a bench designated for parents and children, according to local reports.

The 31-year-old woman, identified by German outlets Westdeutscher Rundfunk and Westdeutsche Allgemeine Zeitung as Hana, was overwhelmed by academic and financial worries when she headed to Gelsenkirchen’s Glückaufpark to call her aunt and vent on Oct. 25.

“I just wanted to be alone and not be seen by anyone,” Hana told WDR.

But to make matters worse, three officers from the Municipal Public Order Service (KOD) confronted Hana and slapped her with a $27 summons, all because she was sitting on a bench designated for families with kids.

“Where is the humanity in that? When you see someone crying, you could at least ask first: ‘Are you okay? Do you need help?’” she asked.

“And then they could have politely explained that you’re not allowed to sit there without children.”

Officers asked her if the children playing nearby were hers, and when she said no, they demanded her ID.

She was also issued a written warning — or “Verwarngeld” — on Oct. 26, a day after the incident.

“Laws and humanity shouldn’t be mutually exclusive,” Hana told WAZ, which initially reported the ordeal.

Other residents in Gelsenkirchen — located in the northwest German state of Nordrhein-Westfalen — told WDR that the municipal ordinance was excessive and made no sense.

“I find it absurd to fine a woman who just wants to rest for a moment,” a pedestrian in Glückaufpark said.

“I’ve lived here for 64 years and have always sat on that bench,” adding that “nobody came by to check.”

Under local municipal laws, everyone over the age of 14 caught resting on park playgrounds faces fines of up to nearly $60. Signs on the playground area reportedly do not specify the age limit.

“Children’s playgrounds are for children. Therefore, adults without children are generally prohibited from staying there,” Gelsenkirchen City Council member Simon Nowack of the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) party told WDR in defense of the rule.

While Hana, who is of Syrian descent, acknowledges that public safety regulations are necessary, she insists the current enforcement crosses the line.

“You hear about children being abducted all the time,” she said.

“That’s why rules like this are very important. But the way they are enforced is even more important.”

She blasted the penalty as a cash grab rather than a legitimate safety measure.

“Making people aware of misconduct and deterring it is okay, I think, but it shouldn’t serve as a source of revenue,” she said.

Left-leaning council factions also took aim at the strict enforcement, arguing a simple verbal warning would have sufficed.

“We must not reach a point where an overly rigid interpretation of rules restricts the quality of life in public spaces or criminalizes citizens over trivialities,” Wiebke Wexeler of the town’s Green Party council faction told WDR.

“This isn’t just about spitting out seeds, it’s about littering in our city, and we think it’s completely right to take action against that.”

A spokesman for the city council told WAZ that the rule is “a comparatively mild and proportionate measure.”

The Gelsenkirchen KOD and city council did not immediately respond to The Post’s request for comment.
