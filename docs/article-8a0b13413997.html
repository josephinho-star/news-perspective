Medicare for All sounds free. But wait until you see your huge tax bill

2026-11-20 10:00

Imagine a politician knocking on your front door with the greatest financial offer in American history.

No more health insurance premiums. No more deductibles. No more copayments. And everybody gets healthcare courtesy of Uncle Sam.

Sounds fantastic, doesn't it?

There's just one tiny problem.

Your doctor isn't working for free. Your hospital isn't operating for free. And the government doesn't have a magical money tree growing behind the White House.

Welcome to Medicare for All, where the healthcare might be free at the doctor's office, but the bill eventually shows up somewhere else.

Usually in your paycheck.

The $30 trillion elephant in the room

Let's start with some numbers.

The Congressional Budget Office has modeled single-payer healthcare systems that would increase federal healthcare spending by approximately $1.5 trillion to $3 trillion annually by 2030.

That's potentially $30 trillion over a decade at the upper end of that annual range. For perspective, America's entire federal government collected roughly $5.4 trillion in revenue in fiscal 2026.

Think about that.

We're talking about adding a massive new federal financing responsibility to a government already carrying more than $40 trillion in debt.

Supporters correctly point out that Americans would no longer pay many private insurance premiums and out-of-pocket costs. But eliminating your insurance premium doesn't eliminate the underlying medical bill. It just changes who sends you the invoice.

Congratulations! Your premium is now a tax

The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget examined what financing approximately $30 trillion in additional federal spending over 10 years might require.

The Congressional Budget Office has modeled single-payer healthcare systems that would increase federal healthcare spending by approximately $1.5 trillion to $3 trillion annually by 2030.

One illustrative option was a 32% payroll tax. Another was a 25% income surtax.

Those aren't official Medicare for All proposals. They're examples of the scale of taxation potentially required if either tax were used alone. And here's where the political sales pitch gets interesting.

A family might save thousands in premiums and deductibles but pay substantially more in taxes.

Another family could come out ahead. The result depends on income, healthcare needs and the financing structure.

You can't determine whether Medicare for All saves your family money until somebody tells you exactly how they're paying for it.

Your insurance card doesn't create another doctor

Here's another inconvenient detail.

If millions more Americans can obtain medical services without worrying about bills, demand for care could increase. That's potentially good for people who currently go without treatment.

But who's providing all those additional appointments? We already have shortages of healthcare professionals in many communities.

Expanding coverage without expanding medical capacity can create longer waits for some services. And if Washington attempts to control spending by reducing reimbursements, hospitals and physicians face another challenge.

You can promise everybody a doctor's appointment. You can't legislate another 100,000 doctors into existence overnight.

What happens to the insurance you already have?

Nearly half of Americans are expected to receive employer-sponsored health coverage in 2026. A comprehensive Medicare for All proposal would fundamentally replace that system.

Some Americans would welcome the change. Others might lose coverage arrangements, provider networks or benefits they currently prefer. Employers would face major adjustments, too.

We're talking about adding a massive new federal financing responsibility to a government already carrying more than $40 trillion in debt.

And don't forget that Medicare itself isn't free today. Beneficiaries commonly pay Part B premiums, face deductibles and often purchase supplemental coverage.

A proposed Medicare for All system with no premiums or deductibles would actually be more generous in important respects than traditional Medicare.

That generosity comes with a price tag.

The government can't repeal economics

Here's what bothers me most about the Medicare for All debate.

Politicians talk about healthcare as though the primary challenge is deciding who deserves coverage. The harder financial question is how America delivers affordable, high-quality care without bankrupting families, overwhelming taxpayers or destabilizing hospitals.

The United States spent approximately $5.3 trillion on healthcare in 2024.

We absolutely need to address prices, administrative waste, prescription drug costs and access to medical care.

A single-payer system might reduce some of those expenses. But the savings aren't guaranteed, and neither is a painless transition.

The real question isn't whether Americans deserve healthcare. Of course, they do. It's whether Washington can finance and administer an enormously expanded system without creating a different set of financial problems.

Because there's one financial lesson every American should understand. When a politician tells you something costing trillions of dollars will be free, don't ask where to sign up.

Ask where they're hiding the bill.
