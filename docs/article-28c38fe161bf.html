Joyce calls Wilson a ‘clown’ after Liberal MP denies power-sharing talks

One Nation’s Barnaby Joyce has branded Liberal Tim Wilson a “clown” after the latter said discussions about a power-sharing agreement between the two parties were a “figment of Barnaby’s imagination”.

Speaking to News24, Joyce sarcastically said:

I just made it up, I get bored, mate … clown. Of course we’re having conversations.

Joyce said there was “no prospect” either the Coalition or One Nation could form government judging off polling, but ruled out any official coalition between the two parties.

We do not want to be in a coalition with Tim [Wilson] to be quite frank.

He’s supposed to be the [shadow] treasurer it’s a shame he can’t count … how are you supposed to get supply and confidence Tim, if you don’t have the numbers?

Joyce said he wouldn’t confirm which senior Liberals he said he had spoken to.

Albanese denies digital duty of care laws are about government control

Prime minister Anthony Albanese has denied Australia’s proposed digital duty of care laws are about government control.

Ahead of a meeting with Donald Trump at the UN general assembly in New York on Tuesday, the US administration warned Australia that its digital duty of care laws risked becoming “viewpoint-based censorship”, calling for American platforms to be exempt.

Speaking to reporters after the assembly, Albanese said he would “stand up for Australia’s national interest”:

The digital duty of care is something that we have foreshadowed for some period of time. It is not about giving government control, it’s about giving people back control over what they received on their devices. Which is why it is framed in that way …

We listened and we participate respectfully and I look forward to immediately after this in a few minutes going to the reception held by president Trump, I look forward to engaging with him there.

The US intervention will embolden the Coalition, which opposes the duty of care laws on the grounds they would hand too much power to the government to regulate online content.

Asked if he was disappointed that Trump had “forcefully rejected the need for international safeguards on AI”, Albanese responded:

We will wait and see what comes out of the meeting between president Trump and the Chinese president, it is good they are meeting and it is on the agenda.

Qantas announces 2028 launch of Sydney-New York direct flights

Qantas says its 18-hour direct Sydney-New York flights will start in mid-2028, predicting it will cut travel time between the two cities by three hours.

The ultra long-haul flights, part of the airline’s Project Sunrise, will follow the launch of Sydney-London direct flights in October 2027.

The company has ordered 12 specially designed Airbus A350-1000ULRs with extra fuel tanks. The first is expected to arrive in Australia in April 2027.

The planes were designed to fly up to 22 hours nonstop for Qantas, to complete some of the longest commercial flights in the world. Qantas said a test model had completed a 24 hour, 24 minute flight from Melbourne to Airbus’ manufacturing facility in Toulouse, France, believed to be the longest-ever commercial flight.

Tickets are set to go on sale in August 2027, Qantas said.

Its chief executive, Vanessa Hudson, said:

Conquering the tyranny of distance has been part of who Qantas is from the very beginning. Reaching New York non-stop completes a vision we set out many years ago, and it’s a moment of enormous pride for the thousands of our people who have been working hard to make Project Sunrise a reality.

Wilson says Bragg ‘more than entitled’ to put forward novel housing ideas

Tim Wilson, the shadow treasurer, says his colleague Andrew Bragg is “more than entitled to raise important issues” around housing.

Wilson spoke to News24 this morning, saying he always believed “it should be home first, super second, in terms of the structure of priority”:

The problem at the moment is the Labor party wants a nation of people who are retired renters. They don’t want homeowners with a superannuation balance. The intergenerational report outlined there’s been a 10% increase in the number of Australians going to retirement renting. That’s a huge jump, and frankly, the Labor party’s answer to that is to say keep renting, we want your money.

When pressed if he supported Bragg’s idea to use superannuation as collateral for a house purchase, Wilson said he wasn’t there yet:

The key point that we’ve always believed is that home ownership is central to a dignified retirement, as is a superannuation balance as an income stream, and the fall back of a pension, and we need to get the hierarchy properly in order.

Joyce speaking with ‘very, very senior Liberals’ about power-sharing deal

Barnaby Joyce says he’s been in talks with “very, very senior Liberals” over a power-sharing arrangement should One Nation win enough seats at the next federal election.

Speaking to News24 yesterday afternoon, the Nationals-turned-One Nation MP said there would be an expectation that the Coalition and the minor party would work together if they had the numbers to beat the Albanese government at the polls.

I think we’ve been pretty clear on this. We’ve said our job is to get rid of the socialists. So either we would offer supply and confidence to the Coalition … on the premise of solid policy outcomes that we would want for that to happen, and we would want there to be clear acknowledgment that they are our policy outcomes, and we’d have a regime then of looking at legislation that came forward … we’d expect the same deal if we had greater numbers than the Coalition.

Joyce said he’d had discussions with “very, very senior Liberals” as recently as Tuesday morning on a possible deal.

He added “we’re now cutting dice, and we’ll find out where we end up”.

Bragg says proposal to use super for mortgages not 'controversial'

Andrew Bragg, the shadow housing minister, said he believes in “big debates” about housing after pushing his new proposal yesterday to allow people to use their superannuation as collateral to get a home loan.

The policy hasn’t been adopted by the opposition, and leader Angus Taylor said yesterday it wasn’t Coalition policy.

Bragg told RN this morning the housing system needed a revamp, and lawmakers were responsible for hosting those ideas:

We’re a great country, and we should be able to have big debates, as we had in the 80s and 90s about national savings and housing and the like, which led, in fact, to the super guarantee. …

It’s very important that we look to calibrate the retirement and housing and superannuation systems to make sure that they’re getting the best outcomes for the Australian people.

I don’t think that’s controversial. I think that’s just our day job.

Labor says no intention to exclude US tech platforms in digital duty of care

Daniel Mulino, the assistant treasurer, maintained Labor plans to put in place “appropriate” protections on big tech after warnings from the White House against any “censorship” of protected speech.

The US warned Australia overnight its plan to boost online safety through digital duty of care laws risked becoming a method for “viewpoint-based censorship”, saying US platforms should be exempted.

Mulino told RN Breakfast this morning the duty of care shifts are meant to combat “what can at times be addictive algorithms, algorithms that can create harm”.

When pressed if Australia would consider a US carve out considering most of the big tech companies are based in the US, Mulino said the digital duty of care proposals would apply regardless of country of origin.

I would expect … that we would apply this to digital platforms no matter where their origin is, and that’s the right position to take when it comes to regulating technology in a way that is achieving the right policy outcomes.

Over 200 homes under construction for Sydney’s essential workers in inner west

The NSW government has begun construction on 220 new build-to-rent apartments for Sydney’s essential workers in the inner west.

Paul Scully, the state minister for planning, and Rose Jackson, the minister for housing, said the units would be available at discounted market rates for health workers, teachers, police officers, firefighters and others “so they don’t have to choose between an affordable home and a long commute”.

The building sits in Annandale, about a 15 minute bus ride from the Sydney CBD.

The NSW government will retain ownership of the properties, which will be built on a vacant site left empty following construction of the WestConnex tunnel.

The building is expected to see its first residents by 2029.

Jackson said in a statement:

Every essential worker priced out of the community they serve is a failure of the housing system we inherited, and this is what fixing it looks like.

The Joinery Annandale means a nurse or a teacher in the Inner West does not have to choose between living near their job and being able to afford rent.

Smartglasses ban on Sydney’s eastern suburb beaches on the cards

Smartglasses could soon be banned across Sydney’s eastern suburbs beaches Coogee, Clovelly and Maroubra under a motion put forward to Randwick council.

Councillor PhilipaVeitch told the council meeting on Tuesday that the devices created a “gross violation of privacy and a safety risk for children and women” on beaches.

The council has already banned the technology at the Des Renford leisure centre, citing “growing community concerns” about their potential misuse.

Veitch suggested provisions for those with significant vision impairment or other disabilities.

It follows City of Sydney councillors voting to ban smartglasses at council gyms, childcare centres and pools on Monday.

Anthony Albanese has told the UN general assembly that “retaining sovereignty must have a digital dimension” as he spruiked his teen social media ban, opt out algorithm and AI statement.

In a speech to the coalition for children’s rights and protections in the age of AI, in New York, the prime minister said Australian parents were legally empowered by the digital safety reform.

The conversations they have had together confirm the common concerns they have about their children, that social media is robbing them of their childhood, addicting them to algorithms [and] encouraging anxieties, eating disorders and other harms.

Put simply, we must give children back their childhood.

In this digital age, retaining sovereignty must have a digital dimension.

‘Super intelligence’: Trump says AI should be renamed and rejects calls for further protections for it

Donald Trump has lashed out at countries seeking to rein in artificial intelligence, even suggesting the technology’s name should be changed to lessen anxieties, Australian Associated Press reports.

Trump’s broadside came at an awkward moment for Anthony Albanese after he urged further safeguards against AI the day before, and as his government was warned about “censorship” by the Trump administration over plans to curb the algorithms of big tech.

In an address to the UN general assembly overnight, the US president said AI should be renamed as “super intelligence”, rejecting calls for further protections for it.

He said AI had a potential to be bigger than the Industrial Revolution or the internet.

The use of the word artificial makes intelligence sound fake. It is not fake, it’s actually amazing,

The United States also totally rejects any attempt to construct a globalist scheme to control the artificial intelligence being spoken of.

He said the use of the technology should be actively encouraged, instead of being curtailed.

‘Culture of secrecy’ and ‘inadequate factchecking’ at Four Corners criticised by ABC watchdog

A culture of secrecy and inadequate factchecking at the ABC’s leading investigative program has been criticised by an independent watchdog, Australian Associated Press reports.

The ABC ombudsman yesterday found two episodes of the Four Corners program led by a bikie turned journalist had breached editorial standards.

Mahmood Fazal was sacked in May and has denied any wrongdoing after questions were raised regarding the anonymous sources used in the Cocaine Nation and Meth Highway episodes aired during 2023 and 2024.

Both were removed by the ABC due to accuracy concerns.

ABC chair Kim Williams elevated the allegations to ombudsman Fiona Cameron who found the national broadcaster had failed to meet its own editorial standards for accuracy, fair and honest dealing, and independence and integrity.

Audiences were given a misleading impression of who key contributors were, alleges the report from Cameron.

In both episodes, a masked figure who purportedly dealt cocaine to Sydney’s elite was featured.

Media Watch later reported Sydney-based rapper Charissa Habkouk played the role as a favour to Fazal.

Cameron confirmed this was “materially misleading” to the audience and recommended a full investigation in the use of anonymous and confidential sources.

Crack down on big money donations to parties, Grattan Institute says

Major parties should get less money to spend at elections, and new independents should get drastically more, says the Grattan Institute, urging a rethink of donation caps to prevent an election spending “arms race”.

Labor and the Coalition negotiated a new set of donation laws that independents called a “stitch up”, giving major parties a cap of $90m each to spend, and placing a per-electorate cap of $800,000.

A new Grattan Institute report says the major party cap should be more than halved, to $40m. It argues the $800,000 seat cap should remain for incumbents and established-party candidates, but new entrants should get an extra 50% to spend.

Grattan says the Australian Electoral Commission and Australian Bureau of Statistics spend around $30m on big campaigns such as getting Australians to enrol to vote – so they argue $40m for big parties is pretty generous.

And to give you a sense of what $90m buys – Clive Palmer spent $89m during the 2019 election.

Big-money elections mean politicians spend more time and effort on fundraising, and political parties become reliant on major donors to fund their campaigns. This creates fertile conditions for wealthy interests to have undue influence over policy making. Capping electoral expenditure puts a ceiling on money in politics.

Political parties collectively declared almost half a billion dollars in expenditure in the year leading up to the 2025 federal election. And this was not a one-off. They declared expenditure of more than $400 million in the lead-up to both the 2022 and 2019 federal elections as well.

Under the government’s newly legislated rules, the taxpayer funding for parties increased from $3.38 for every first preference vote for parties and independents that achieved at least 4% of votes, to $5 – which Grattan argues won’t be necessary if the overall spending cap was reduced, saving public money.
