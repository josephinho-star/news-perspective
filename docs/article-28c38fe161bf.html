Police offer $1m reward for information about missing boy Gus Lamont

A $1m reward for information about missing four-year-old Gus Lamont is on offer, as police say they have found no evidence to suggest he is still alive.

It has been almost a year since four-year-old Gus went missing from his family’s enormous sheep farm in the South Australian outback.

He was last seen playing outside at 5pm on Saturday 27 September. Half an hour later, he was gone.

His disappearance sparked one of the biggest searches the state has ever undertaken, with new searches launched as new information or theories came to light.

Volunteers, police, emergency service and defence personnel, along with dogs and divers and drones scoured the 60,000 hectare Oak Park Station, about 300km inland from Adelaide.

One of the biggest moments in the saga came in February this year, when the South Australian police declared his disappearance a major crime, and said they had identified a suspect.

Det Supt Darren Fielke said at the time they had effectively ruled out Gus wandering off or being abducted, and that they were now investigating someone in his household.

The suspect was not one of his parents, Fielke said.

The SA premier, Peter Malinauskas, said they stand ready to do “everything they can” to help police. He said it would go to anyone who had information leading to a body or a conviction. He said it was a “crystal clear” message:

If you know something you’ve gotta speak.

The disappearance of Gus Lamont has deeply shocked and saddened all South Australians. I cannot imagine the anguish that Gus’s parents Jess and Josh are going through, and my thoughts remain with them.

Fielke said the police would do everything they could “to locate Gus and reunite him with his family”.

Police arrest Melbourne man who allegedly pledged allegiance to Isis

Counter-terrorism police have arrested a Melbourne man, who they allege acted as an Isis propagandist.

The 65-year-old was arrested during a raid of a home in Carlton this morning.

Police allege he pledged allegiance to Isis and has played a significant role in spreading propaganda for the terrorist group, sending about 10,000 files of violent extremist material.

The man is alleged to have shared information about avoiding detection from authorities and instructions on how to plan and conduct mass casualty attacks.

AFP acting assistant commissioner counter-terrorism, Paula Hudson, spoke to reporters just after midday.

We will allege the videos, photographs, audio files and documents contain messages relating to current content, being executions, mass murder, torture and propaganda ….

Let me be clear, terrorist propaganda is not harmless online content. Terrorist organisations like Isis rely on online platforms to distribute propaganda and recruit supporters.

Hudson added that police launched an investigation after Australian Border Force officers found suspicious material on electronic devices seized as the man flew out of Melbourne.

She said the man was a “principal distributor of Isis propaganda within the group”.

The man lives with his wife and is an Australian citizen, according to police.

Wong warns nuclear test ban under ‘serious strain’, condemns Russia at UN

Foreign minister Penny Wong has said the global norm against nuclear testing is under “serious strain”.

Wong criticised Russia’s decision to pull back from the test ban treaty, while speaking at a UN meeting marking 30 years of the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT). She called on remaining nations to ratify the agreement “without delay”.

Any nuclear explosion – of any yield – could have irrevocable consequences …

In this context, we must make it clear that Australia strongly opposes Russia’s revocation of its ratification of the CTBT.

That Russia would even countenance a return to nuclear tests is an affront to all who want a world without nuclear weapons.

Australia and partners continue to call on Russia to reverse this course of action.

Wong also welcomed Tonga’s recent ratification, marking full treaty coverage across the Pacific.

Hume backs ‘Wilson’s version of events’ over Joyce’s claim of power-sharing talks

If you were following the blog earlier this morning, you would have seen the spat between One Nation’s Barnaby Joyce and Liberal Tim Wilson.

A quick recap: Joyce claimed he had been speaking with “senior Liberals” about a potential power-sharing arrangement should Pauline Hanson’s One Nation win enough seats at the next federal election.

Wilson rebuked this, and now Liberal senator Jane Hume has also poured cold water on Joyce’s claim.

Appearing on Sky News this morning, Hume was asked if she had any knowledge of Joyce’s supposed negotiations. “No, I’m afraid I don’t,” she said.

Pretty sure that I guess [Angus] Taylor doesn’t. Pretty sure that Matt Canavan doesn’t either, but Barnaby may persist and we’ll see what happens …

Corruption not a ‘crime without victims’, counsel assisting royal commission says

Counsel assisting the Big Build royal commission, Chris Young KC, said while corruption has often been described as a “crime without victims”, that is “not what we have been told”.

He cited several examples, including the directors of a small Indigenous-owned labour hire business who wrote to the minister responsible for these projects and to the premier in April 2022, saying they had been “threatened with violence and banned” from a state government project. Young said:

They said they had recordings. They asked for help … They got a reply a year later, which referred them elsewhere. By then, their business was all but finished, and it was closed a few months after.

He said a woman working in traffic management on a public hospital was “locked in a storeroom by a man recently released from prison for stalking and threatening to kill a woman” who “smoked ice and blew the smoke in her face”.

She reported it. She says that what happened next was that she lost her job … There are others. Workers were reportedly told they no longer had jobs on a state tunnel project because they belonged to the wrong union. Some subcontractors say they lost work because they would not pay. Some people were seriously injured. Others had their property destroyed.

Big Build royal commission to pursue ‘every person’ involved in corruption

Commissioner Chris Kourakis says the royal commission’s job is to bring those responsible for delivering the Big Build – the Victorian government’s pipeline of more than 180 major road and rail projects – “to account publicly”.

It’s also the job of this commission to pursue every person who is reasonably suspected of being complicit in the abuse of entrusted power for private gain and to expose and to refer for prosecution whoever is implicated by the evidence the commission receives. The commission will do so from those who carried the highest level of responsibility down and without exception

Kourakis also provided a warning to “all those who have done wrong” that the commission will leave “no stone unturned in search for whatever is hid or whomever lurks underneath it”:

Anyone who has an ambition to remain in the industry will be better served by early voluntary disclosures to the commission than by waiting to be found out.

Big Build royal commissioner says closed hearings already held, detective appointed

Commissioner Chris Kourakis said that while this is the first public hearing of the inquiry, work began immediately after it was stood up in August. He says this work has included appointing Victoria police detective supt Daniel Ryan to lead the commission’s investigation team.

He said prior to joining the commission, Ryan worked in professional standards in Victoria police and had earlier worked in the homicide squad, the joint organised crime taskforce, and the criminal proceed squad.

Kourakis also confirmed the commission had already held a number of closed hearings:

They were closed because their purpose was to obtain sensitive information to guide the commission’s investigations. Early disclosure of that information would have compromised the commission’s ongoing investigations.

He said it would continue to hold closed hearings when necessary, but it would also “deliver on the just expectation of the Victorian public that there will be full disclosure of all matters touching on the integrity of the infrastructure projects the commission is charged to investigate.”
