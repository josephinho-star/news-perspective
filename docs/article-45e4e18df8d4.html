California home where top Theranos scientist died by suicide quietly sells for $5M

2026-11-02 20:44

A Silicon Valley home tied to one of the darkest chapters of the Theranos scandal has changed hands.

Rochelle Gibbons, widow of Theranos chief scientist Ian Gibbons, sold the couple’s longtime ranch in Portola Valley, Calif., for $5 million in May 2025 — a deal that slipped by largely unnoticed. It’s the same house where the British biochemist died by suicide in 2013, as he faced mounting pressure to testify about the startup’s technology.

They had purchased the three bedroom, 3.5 bathroom home for just $785,000 back in 1990, property records obtained by The Post show.

Buyers didn’t hesitate.

The property at 831 La Mesa Drive came to market on April 21 with a $4.65 million price tag, landed a buyer within 10 days and closed on May 19 for $5 million, a cool $350,000 over ask.

Rochelle had spent a few years renovating the property before putting it on the market.

The single-story spread spans 3,074 square feet on a lot of roughly a quarter acre in the leafy Ladera enclave. A top-to-bottom overhaul delivered heated concrete floors, a chef’s kitchen anchored by an oversized island and a walk-in pantry, and walls of glass that pour in sunshine.

Two secondary bedrooms share a Jack-and-Jill bath, while a flex room could serve as a den, an office or extra sleeping space. Out back, there’s a pool ringed by mature greenery.

The backstory is far more grim than the brochure.

Gibbons, a Cambridge-educated scientist with more than 60 US patents to his credit, was hired in 2005 as Elizabeth Holmes’ first seasoned scientific recruit. He’d come recommended by Theranos board member Channing Robertson, for whom he had done consulting work.

It didn’t take long for the cracks to show. The miniature finger-prick machines Holmes was hyping to investors simply didn’t deliver, and Gibbons confided as much at home. He “told me nothing was working,” Rochelle once told the Wall Street Journal.

Already battling cancer, he felt trapped.

“Ian felt like he would lose his job if he told the truth,” she later said to Vanity Fair. Holmes, meanwhile, was listed as a co-inventor on patents built on much of his work.

In 2013, Gibbons was subpoenaed to testify in a patent dispute involving the company. He was reluctant to answer questions about how the technology functioned, in part because he believed the hardware wasn’t ready for the public.

Gibbons took an overdose of medication. His wife found him unconscious on their bathroom floor the next morning. He was rushed to the hospital and died of liver failure a week later on May 23, 2013 at 67.

The company’s response stunned his family. Holmes’ secretary passed along condolences, but Holmes herself never reached out. Instead, Theranos called the widow insisting she return any confidential company materials, and its lawyers later threatened her for speaking publicly.

Rochelle, a scientist and patent attorney in her own right, didn’t stay quiet. She became a key voice in the reporting that unraveled the $9 billion company, and after Holmes’ 2022 fraud conviction, she remembered her husband in glowing terms. “For me, he was as near to perfection, as a human could be,” she told CBS News.

The tragedy reached a wider audience when Stephen Fry portrayed Gibbons in Hulu’s 2022 miniseries “The Dropout.”

Holmes, now 42, is still behind bars on an 11-year sentence, though she’s slated to move to a Texas halfway house in 2027. She’s also back in the spotlight thanks to a buzzy new documentary, “You Can See Everything,” that premiered at Telluride. That film, with Nathan Fielder, captures her and partner Billy Evans pitching potential partners on a so-called “Theranos 2.0” venture Evans intends to build while she serves her time.
