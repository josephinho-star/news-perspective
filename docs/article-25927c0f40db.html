Is Alice Huang a spy or not?

2026-11-26 20:00

The central, still-unproven claim in the Nvidia scandal is that Alice Huang, a former executive at the data-center company Megaspeed, is a Chinese spy. She had an affair with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who abruptly resigned on Nov. 12 after acknowledging it and was just as abruptly reinstated on Monday of this week.

What’s publicly known is murkier than the headlines suggest. U.S. intelligence has reportedly tracked Alice Huang’s contact with an officer of China’s spy agency, but officials haven’t offered public evidence that she was commissioned to target Nvidia’s chief. In the past week, after President Trump successfully pushed to bring Jensen Huang back, the administration has played down the allegations it once circulated internally. Whether that reflects new intelligence or political pressure is the big question.

Undisputed reporting shows the claim emerged from the White House itself. It first appeared in a highly classified memo written in March under the oversight of Secretary of State Marco Rubio and circulated to White House officials ahead of Trump’s trip to China in May. The memo formed the partial basis for the New York Times’ initial reporting on the scandal, published Oct. 29 and its existence was first reported on Nov. 8.

The memo did not definitively say, according to the Wall Street Journal, that Alice Huang was a Chinese spy. CNN, by contrast, has reported that the memo concluded she was a Chinese intelligence asset.

What’s better established is that Alice Huang was in touch with Chinese intelligence. The Ministry of State Security (MSS) is Beijing’s civilian spy agency, responsible for both foreign intelligence and domestic counterintelligence. Last weekend, the Times, citing people with knowledge of U.S. intelligence reports, reported that Huang was in contact with an MSS officer from the spring of 2025 until this fall, a period that overlaps her relationship with Jensen Huang. (The Chinese government has called the spy claims a “smear,” and Megaspeed says Huang left the company in August 2025 and that its business is legitimate.)

Top Trump officials have nonetheless all but discredited the espionage claims, along with the suggestion that Nvidia fell prey to spying or foreign influence.

Attorney General Todd Blanche, who was Trump’s personal defense lawyer before taking over the Justice Department, told journalists on Tuesday: “I have no reason to stand up here and say Nvidia is anything but a good corporate citizen.” On the same day, Trump said a pardon was an option for Jensen Huang but that he did not think one would be needed. Senior intelligence officials reportedly told Congress that the government apparently has no actual evidence that the MSS directed Alice Huang to pursue an affair with Jensen Huang.

None of those statements necessarily exonerates Alice Huang as an intelligence agent. Instead, they support the alternate theory that the Chinese government didn’t tell her to begin the affair and that Jensen Huang broke no law by having it. Not being directed to have an affair isn’t the same as not being a spy, though.

From a certain angle, the circumstantial evidence surrounding Alice Huang looks quite suspicious. She was a top executive at a company investigated for illicitly channeling Nvidia chips to China, and her husband, Kang Wei, is said to have even closer ties to the MSS, with some sources saying he worked for the agency.

The timing of the government’s reversal is at the heart of the matter. Trump has said he lobbied the board “directly and indirectly” to reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO, so it wouldn’t be surprising if officials who serve at his pleasure were recasting the case in terms that satisfy his agenda. That wouldn’t necessarily make their assessments wrong. But unless new intelligence has actually resolved the question, and none has been publicly identified as yet, that backdrop is critical to keep in mind. CBS has also reported that Rubio, whose office oversaw the original memo, has privately voiced concerns about aspects of Jensen Huang’s return.

Doubts persist elsewhere, too. Earlier this month, CNN reported that the intelligence community was still working to understand Alice Huang’s relationship with Jensen Huang and that the intelligence collected on her to date does not prove she was acting as a spy for Beijing. After a briefing by top intelligence officials earlier this month, Rep. Jim Himes (D-Conn.), the incoming chair of the House Intelligence Committee, said he was unsure whether she was a spy or a “useful idiot.” And this week, the Wall Street Journal pointed out that under Chinese leader Xi Jinping, top corporate executives routinely maintain ties to the country’s intelligence service without being formally deputized as assets.

The crush of conflicting claims, most from anonymous government sources, obviously leaves large questions unanswered as to what actually transpired. Some of those questions that naturally spring to mind:

- Alice Huang is said to have been under U.S. surveillance for over a year. If the government suspected her of espionage, why wasn’t Jensen Huang warned earlier (and privately, so as not to cause the stock-market-destabilizing maelstrom that followed the more recent disclosure)? One explanation that has been given is that alerting the Nvidia chief might have tipped off Huang that her cover had been blown.

- If Alice Huang was a spy, what information did she pass to the Chinese government, and what influence did she actually have?

- Most critically, how did the allegation reach the public at all? The likeliest answer is a leak. A source told CNN that Rubio briefed Trump on the intelligence in May, yet Trump has since said he only recently learned of the concerns, and in October he presented Jensen Huang with the National Medal of Science. Officials who felt they could not get through to the president may have turned to reporters instead, which would explain how the contents of a memo whose existence has never been disputed found their way into print. What remains unclear is who leaked it, and whether they anticipated the fallout.

There are answers to all of these questions, of course, but when the public will get them is anybody’s guess. Jensen Huang is due to appear before the Senate Banking Committee next week, and by the look from the statements of some of its members, he’s likely in for a rough ride. Unfortunately, congressional hearings, particularly public ones, rarely unearth secrets that haven’t already been disclosed.
