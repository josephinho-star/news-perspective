The ICJ Just Recognized the Genocide of the Rohingya. What’s Next?

2026-11-19 13:02

On August 25, 2017, when I was 14 years old, the Myanmar military, led by Min Aung Hlaing, conducted operations to kill Rohingya like me. They burned our villages to the ground, executed thousands, and forced the rest of us to flee to Bangladesh. My family and I lived for six years in refugee camps in Cox’s Bazar. It was the third time that my grandmother had been forced by the Myanmar military to run from her home and live in Bangladesh.

The genocide of Rohingya Muslims, which was formally recognized today by the International Court of Justice (ICJ), began when my grandmother was young and continues to this day. Successive military regimes in Myanmar stripped us of our citizenship, our land, and our dignity during a 40-year campaign aimed at destroying my people.

I am one of the fortunate ones who was able to escape and create a life beyond the confines of barbed wire camps and the existential urgency of the next meal.

For the past three and a half years, together with Rohingya colleagues, I’ve served as a connection between Rohingya communities in Bangladesh and Myanmar, diplomats, and the international community, advocating for them before the U.S. Congress, the European Union, and the United Nations. In pursuit of a more just future, I work to empower young people, women, and girls in the camps to prepare them to participate in a future democratic Myanmar through Refugee Women for Peace and Justice, which I lead.

The question now is whether that just future, featuring a democratic Myanmar, will ever come.

The first two times my grandmother was forced to flee her home, in 1978 and 1992, Bangladesh negotiated with the perpetrators, the Myanmar military, for the Rohingya to return. The Myanmar military faced no consequences for its brutality and we Rohingya were ushered back to our homes without rights or recourse to protect against the next onslaught. The violence of 2017 was, therefore, as inevitable as the atrocities that came before it.

But 2017 felt different. We were told that the international community was motivated to help us. Each foreign delegation to the camps raised our hopes that our situation would change soon. Things did actually change on November 11, 2019, when The Gambia filed a case at the ICJ on behalf of and in solidarity with the Rohingya against Myanmar. It took over six years for the court to open a full public hearing in January 2026, but the wheels of justice were turning and we believed that “never again” might finally mean something.

The ICJ has never before found a state responsible for committing genocide. It has now. This historic ruling offers a moment of vindication and solace for the nearly 2 million of us who fled our homes in 2017, and for those who continue to languish in tiny shelters in refugee camps, hoping for their rights to be restored, justice to be served, and to return home. For many of us, this is the most important day of our lives. I am grateful to The Gambia, the host country Bangladesh, the countries that intervened, the legal teams, and the human rights groups that documented the violence. I am most grateful to the survivors who had the courage to tell their stories to the court.

Although the ruling is a powerful indictment of the Myanmar military and Min Aung Hlaing, it alone does not end our genocide. The ruling must be enforced. In 2020, soon after the case was accepted by the court, the ICJ ordered provisional measures that the Myanmar military stop committing violence against the Rohingya, and to regularly report to the court about the situation on the ground. The military repeatedly defied that order with impunity. The international community has done virtually nothing to hold them accountable and we fear the military will get off once again.

Just a year after the ICJ’s provisional measures, the military seized power from the elected government of Myanmar in a coup. When the public resisted, the military opened fire, sparking the most widespread violent conflict in the country’s history. The reviled military regime lost vast parts of the country to resistance forces, including virtually all of Rakhine State, where hundreds of thousands of stateless Rohingya live in conditions of apartheid. In response, the Myanmar military has bombed Rakhine State incessantly, killing Rohingya and other ethnic communities, and forcibly conscripting Rohingya into their ranks. Meanwhile, the international community has done virtually nothing. Instead, the chief architect of our genocide and the 2021 coup, Min Aung Hlaing, has been welcomed with a red carpet by nine countries since he appointed himself as “president” of Myanmar in April of this year. This impunity must end.

Unlike the provisional measures, the ICJ’s judgement must be more than a piece of paper. The Security Council, U.N. member states, and all relevant stakeholders must implement the judgement immediately. They must use this judgement to hold the Myanmar military accountable for its crimes and pursue every possible means to protect and aid Rohingya.

When implementing the ruling, the international community must also recognize that the well-being of the Rohingya is inseparable from the well-being of Myanmar. Outsiders often disengage from Myanmar, complaining that Myanmar’s conflict is too complicated to resolve. This is partially true, but one thing is not complicated: the Myanmar military is the primary agent of instability and Myanmar will be at war as long as the military is in power.

The Rohingya genocide is unique in its systematic nature and intent, but other Myanmar ethnic groups face similar violence at the hands of the military regime. The ICJ’s ruling is, therefore, for every ethnic group in Myanmar that is fighting for a just, peaceful and democratic Myanmar. We Rohingya, the poorest, courageous, and most marginalized ethnic group in our beautifully diverse country, brought our stories to the world’s court to end our suffering but also to serve our brothers and sisters from other communities who have suffered similarly at the hands of the military regime.

Peace talks that treat the regime as one party among equals will repeat the mistake my grandmother has lived through for decades. Bangladesh sent us home in 1978 and 1992 under deals with the military that guaranteed us nothing. The killing resumed each time. A third return on those terms will end the same way. A lasting settlement must end military rule and give Rohingya not only citizenship but a voice in the political decisions that affect our lives.

My grandmother still lives in a refugee camp. When I visited her earlier this year just two months ago, she asked me when she could go home. I had no answer. I still don’t. The answer belongs to the governments with the power to enforce the ICJ’s ruling and to the Myanmar people, who will decide if the Rohingya belong in whatever comes after military rule. My grandmother has been running from this military for four decades. The world can help her stop.
