Russia warns of ‘preventive nuclear attack’ as Medvedev claims Ukraine, NATO crossed line

2026-11-24 07:47

Published Nov. 24, 2026, 2:47 a.m. ET

See more of our coverage in your search results.

Russia warned Monday that it could carry out a “preventive nuclear attack” under certain circumstances, with former President Dmitry Medvedev claiming Ukraine and NATO allies have already crossed certain thresholds that could push Moscow to consider using nuclear weapons.

“The massive use of non-nuclear means that cause catastrophes makes a preventive nuclear attack by the Russian Federation possible,” Medvedev, deputy chairman of Russia’s Security Council, wrote in an article published Monday in the Moscow Journal of International Law and excerpted by the state news agency TASS.

Medvedev also warned that “secret or hidden supplies of nuclear weapons from the West to Kyiv will provoke a legitimate preventive nuclear attack by Russia.”

“Kyiv and its allies must remember that the Russian Federation not only has the right, but in various situations has the obligation to use nuclear weapons,” he wrote.

He argued that Ukraine and its Western allies have already created circumstances under which Russia could consider a nuclear strike, though he said Russian President Vladimir Putin carefully weighs the consequences before making such a decision.

“Formally, in a number of cases, the parties collectively opposing us (Ukraine, NATO countries, and several Western countries outside the alliance) have already created situations in which Russia considers the possibility of launching a nuclear strike,” Medvedev wrote in the article, titled “State Sovereignty and Nuclear Weapons: International Legal Aspects of Ensuring National Security”.

Despite those warnings, Medvedev wrote that Putin “is always guided by a comprehensive analysis of all factors and the proportionality between the actual damage inflicted and the retaliatory measures.”

Medvedev also said Russia has not used nuclear weapons during the war with Ukraine because it has not considered them necessary, though he warned that calculation could change.

“Obviously, so far the course of events in the Ukrainian crisis has not created a state of direct necessity to use nuclear weapons,” he wrote.

“The decision on Russia’s use of nuclear weapons during the special military operation was not taken due to the lack of a direct necessity to do so,” he added, using the Kremlin’s preferred term for the war in Ukraine.

Medvedev argued that while Russia’s nuclear doctrine does not explicitly authorize preventive nuclear strikes, certain scenarios — including catastrophic attacks using conventional weapons or the transfer of nuclear arms to Ukraine — could justify preemptive action.

He said any final decision rests solely with Putin.

“In making such decisions, the Russian president is always guided by a complete analysis of all factors and the proportionality between the actual damage caused and the retaliatory measures,” Medvedev wrote.

“The Kyiv regime and its sponsors must not harbor dangerous illusions,” he warned.

“If the scenarios mentioned above were to occur, the head of state not only has the right, but also the obligation to use nuclear weapons. All those who are currently fanning the flames must fully realize this”.

Medvedev’s remarks came within days of President Donald Trump meeting with Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping on Nov. 19 in Shenzhen, China, where the leaders of the three nuclear powers agreed to pursue talks aimed at reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation.

While the summit produced no major breakthrough on arms reductions, the leaders reaffirmed in a declaration that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought.

To support his argument, Medvedev cited a 1996 International Court of Justice advisory opinion, contending that international law does not categorically prohibit the use of nuclear weapons when a state’s survival is at stake.

The court concluded that the threat or use of nuclear weapons would generally violate international humanitarian law, but it did not definitively resolve whether such use would be lawful in extreme circumstances of self-defense.

Medvedev, who served as Russia’s president from 2008 to 2012 and later as prime minister under Putin, has become one of Moscow’s most outspoken voices on nuclear escalation since Russia invaded Ukraine.

Medvedev accused NATO members of undermining nonproliferation efforts, criticizing Germany’s participation in nuclear deterrence arrangements and arguing that NATO’s nuclear-sharing policies do not represent a universally accepted interpretation of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT)

On Monday, he also accused the U.S. of applying double standards toward Iran and said Washington used nonproliferation fears to justify military action against Tehran while following its own objectives.
