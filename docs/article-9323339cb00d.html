After 80 years in Oxford, Naga ancestors are finally going home

2026-11-24 07:30

The lacerating legacy of colonialism survives in many spaces, particularly in museum collections and private holdings across the western world. And it’s not just limited to inanimate artefacts or cultural objects.

For almost 80 years, human remains were displayed on crowded glass shelves at Pitt Rivers Museum at Oxford University, one of the world’s most celebrated institutions.

Many of them were, in fact, skeletal remains taken from Nagaland brought to the museum by two British colonial administrators, John Henry Hutton and James Philip Mills between 1910 and 1940. Now, after six years of consultations, negotiations and a concerted effort to build consensus across Naga communities, 39 skeletal remains are finally coming home. In early November, the museum handed custodianship to a Naga delegation of leaders and scholars. The repatriated remains include those of two children, a woman and a village chief.

“This is the first such repatriation of human remains to India,” Dolly Kikon, a Naga anthropologist and professor at the University of California, Santa Cruz, told me. She had contacted the Pitt Rivers Museum in 2020 after reading in the Guardian that the museum was removing human remains from public display following an ethical review, setting the repatriation process in motion. In a statement, the museum’s director, Laura Van Broekhoven, acknowledged that it has been a “long journey”, adding that “hope is possible as we work towards reconciliation and healing”.

The review was part of a broader reassessment of the museum’s display practices and how it presented human remains from communities across the world. Among its most contentious displays were the shrunken heads known as tsantsa, from the Shuar people in South America. Pitt Rivers – one of the world’s leading anthropology and ethnography museums – has acknowledged that its display reinforced racist stereotypes about the culture. The Naga remains were displayed alongside artefacts and human remains from a number of communities, including from Nigeria and Papua New Guinea.

Naga academics have challenged the perception that the remains were exclusively associated with the practice of headhunting. While Naga warriors traditionally collected the heads of enemies killed in raids, the anthropologists say that British collectors acquired Naga human remains under duress during colonial expeditions.

For the Naga community, that was just the beginning of a long journey. India, unlike countries such as Australia, Canada and the US, has no national laws for the repatriation of Indigenous ancestral remains, says Kikon. “We had to ensure the process was community-led, in the absence of a policy and make sure the focus remained on healing and reconciliation. It was all unprecedented.”

The Forum for Naga Reconciliation, a collective working toward peace and reconciliation among Naga Indigenous communities, facilitated the repatriation, and a voluntary research group, Recover, Restore and Decolonise (RRaD), was formed to build wider community awareness. Its volunteers travelled to urban centres and villages, talking with students, elders and intergenerational audiences about the human remains kept at the museum and what the community wanted to do with them.

The museum still has around 178 objects that contain, or may contain, human hair. It also has 6,500 Naga objects, including clothing items, weapons, vessels and musical instruments, many from Mills and Hutton, while some items, like weapons, were bought from auction houses. “The Pitt Rivers Museum has one of the largest collections of Naga objects, artefacts and human remains in the world. What we have focused on for now is the skeletal remains, but this is only a first step in thinking more broadly about these collections,” said Arkotong Longkumer, a professor of anthropology and modern Asia at Edinburgh University.

The question of restitution of Indian artefacts and cultural heritage items has long occupied a prominent place in public discourse. At its heart is the Koh-i-noor diamond, which passed through Mughal, Persian and Afghan hands before Britain wrested it from the 10-year-old king, Maharaja Duleep Singh. Today, it is part of the British crown jewels, a symbol of the plunder of the empire and, for many in India, a theft that continues to rankle. Last year, an Indian parliamentary committee recommended establishing a dedicated heritage recovery taskforce to pursue the return of cultural objects held abroad, particularly those taken during the colonial period.

In the case of the human remains, the question is about the right to honour the ancestors and give them a dignified resting place. “How we imagine a shared future depends on how we understand our history. We have to look back and make sense of the past in order to think about the kind of future we want to create,” Longkumer told me.

“The Naga repatriation addresses a very deep colonial violence,” Kikon says. “It is about the dehumanisation where the remains of human beings were stored in museums and exhibited as exotic objects.”

The return prompted Kikon to reflect on the contradiction between the longstanding reverence for Oxford University by Indians, many of whom have aspired to study there, and the institution’s history of exhibiting the remains of their ancestors. “Repatriation is not just about bringing back bones. It was about starting a process of deep introspection,” she says. “Ultimately, it leaves us with a greater responsibility to reflect on how we live our lives.”

The remains will be buried at Naga Solidarity Park in Kohima, the capital of Nagaland.

A recommendation from someone with great taste

Writer Gayatri Rangachari Shah, a founding member of the Women Leadership Circle, recommends an inspiring model of rural development. “If there is an institution that captures an alternative vision of India, it’s the Barefoot College in Rajasthan. Set up by Sanjit ‘Bunker’ Roy in 1972, it has spent more than five decades putting rural communities at the centre of their own development. Its Solar Mamas programme has enabled women of all ages who had never been to school to learn to make and install solar lights. Its other initiatives include digital learning centres for young women, night schools for those working during the day, menstrual-friendly toilets in government schools, rainwater harvesting, waste management and recycling.

Roy died on 10 November but he passed on his work to a younger team.”

India’s top wildlife body approved the clearance of ecologically sensitive land and wildlife corridors covering an area of at least 99,000 hectares (244,530 acres) – an area about a third larger than Singapore – between 2014 and 2026, an analysis by Article 14 concluded. Defence projects, mining and irrigation were the top sectors for which the board granted clearances at the expense of forests and wildlife habitats, according to the investigation by Prakriti Srivastava and Prerna Singh Bindra.

Grand Theft Auto VI, billed as the biggest cultural event of the year, has smashed sales records. It has also helped propel a little-known 1982 Indian disco track to global fandom. Aaj Shanibar by the Bengali singer Rupa Biswas features on Worldwide FM, one of the radio stations in the game.

The song was part of an album called Disco Jazz that Biswas recorded during a visit to her brother in Canada. The album never made a splash and faded into obscurity, but years later her son discovered the track had found a following in DJ sets and was reissued by the US label Numero Group. Now, with GTA VI, the song has reached a generation who were not even born when the original was released 44 years ago.
