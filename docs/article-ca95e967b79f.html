US army to detonate 68 barrels of toxic lead in Iowa on day before Thanksgiving

2026-11-25 00:33

The US Army is set to detonate 68 barrels – five tons – of lead azide at an ammunition plant in rural Iowa on Wednesday morning, after delays and conflicting information about how far the toxic lead dust plume could travel after the explosion.

That controlled blast has been scheduled for 10am ET on Wednesday, the day before the Thanksgiving holiday.

“Inaction is no longer an option,” Army Maj Gen Eric Shirley, the commander of the operation, said at a press conference in Middletown, Iowa, on Tuesday. “It would be irresponsible for us to not take the determined, consensus-based course of action moving forward.”

Federal officials on Tuesday said mitigation measures had been put in place, including barriers around the blast zone to capture much of the dust, but warned that residents in the vicinity should be prepared to evacuate.

“We don’t believe that the public should be in a panic,” said Adam Telle, acting secretary of the army. “Our goal is to keep as much of it as we can here on our Iowa Army Ammunition plant to prevent it from traveling.”

Telle said weather conditions on Wednesday morning were expected to be “ideal to protect public health and safety across the region” and stressed that a “controlled, mitigated, detonation” was “far preferable to an uncontrolled spontaneous detonation” of the unstable material.

State officials, environmental groups and residents have sharply criticized federal authorities over what many have described as a “communication failure” about the operation.

In a statement, Iowa senator-elect Josh Turek welcomed the army’s “long-overdue press conference” about Wednesday’s operation and vowed to “continue asking questions about how we got here” when he joins the chamber in January.

“There is no excuse for such a failure of transparency and communication, especially when the health and safety of the public is involved,” said Turek, who was born with spina bifida, which he attributes to his father’s exposure to Agent Orange during the Vietnam war. “Iowans should have had a full and clear understanding of the health implications of this operation, especially as children, pregnant women, and those with chronic illness are being advised to stay away from the affected area.”

Earlier on Tuesday, the army released new maps showing the projected plume path moving south-west of the Iowa army ammunition plant in Middletown, stretching through a south-eastern portion of the state before extending into northern Missouri.

The army sent an emergency notification to cellphones on Tuesday confirming the time of the controlled explosion – and warning that some people within 5 miles south-west of the plant may be evacuated.

The “imminent threat alert” told those up to 20 miles away to remain indoors with windows closed “to avoid airborne lead dust, noise and vibration”.

On Tuesday, the outgoing governor, Kim Reynolds, issued a proclamation of disaster emergency for Des Moines, Lee and Van Buren counties. Additionally, Reynolds said, she was deploying up to 100 soldiers from the Iowa national guard to support with public safety efforts.

Earlier in the day, the governor announced her support for the planned detonation after speaking with military leaders, including US defense secretary Pete Hegseth.

“I trust the army will do everything in its power to protect the people who could be affected,” she said in a statement.

The army said it had set up additional “blast barriers” around the bunkers where the lead azide will be detonated. It also said it would apply ballistic gels and foams to absorb the lead dust and douse the explosive materials with thousands of gallons of water.

The decision to proceed came after a federal appeals court cleared the way for the detonation to take place after the date had been changed three times after an outpouring of public concern. Environmentalists sued to stop the detonation, arguing that the planned explosion had not been issued permits correctly.

An attorney for the environmentalists emphasized that their legal challenge centers on the validity of the Environment Protection Agency permit – not the army’s decision to move forward with the detonation under the emergency provision.

The army said it needs to blow up the stockpile to prevent a possible chain-reaction explosion, which would be more dangerous.

The order to go ahead came after an extraordinary intervention by Donald Trump, who said on social media that in addition to the court’s go-ahead, he wanted Rob Sand, Iowa’s governor-elect, to approve the detonation.

Sand, a Democrat who does not take office until January, has said the decision is fully up to the president’s administration.

Evacuated residents will be notified by phone once they may return home, the Des Moines Register reported.

The army and the Iowa department of health and human services are recommending that those in the area take protective measures. Those include closing all windows and exterior doors; turning off or placing heating, ventilation, and air conditioning systems in recirculation mode; avoiding unnecessary travel; and cleaning indoor and outdoor surfaces that may be contacted by children.

The Associated Press contributed reporting
