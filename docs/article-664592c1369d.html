Pete Hegseth, the defense secretary, used his remarks at the Pentagon ceremony to link the 9/11 attacks directly to the ongoing US war with Iran, framing both as part of a continuing religious and civilizational struggle. “The struggle against evil continued, and it continues now. It will continue until Judgment Day. Our only answer here on Earth is eternal vigilance,” Hegseth said.

He also claimed the threat extended into US borders, warning that “enemies plot to curb our power and kill our citizens, including from Islamic terrorists abroad and at home.”

He went on to credit Trump with dismantling Iran’s military capabilities, saying “the United States killed their leaders” and vowed that “we’re still sending terrorists where they belong to hell, and tankers to the bottom of the ocean where they belong.” He added that “we are ensuring that Iran will never have a nuclear weapon.”

Hegseth closed by invoking scripture, telling the audience that “the world will always have war and rumors of war”, before calling for a new generation of “strong, brave warriors who will say when it matters most, send me”.

My colleague Ramon Antonio Vargas has also been reporting on the group 9/11 victims’ families who say it has “taken way too long” to hold Saudi Arabia accountable for its alleged support of the terrorists who carried out the September 11, 2001 attacks.

Terry Strada – the national chair of 9/11 Families United – said she felt as if it had “been several lifetimes” for there to be progress in the effort “to achieve a modicum of justice through our court system” in connection with the deadliest act of terror against the US.

Strada, whose husband, Tom, was killed in the attacks, subsequently became an advocate for people who lost loved ones on September 11.

One of the 9/11 families’ most visible attempts at accountability for the attacks has been a years-long civil lawsuit alleging that Saudi Arabia supported the al-Qaida terror group operatives who carried out September 11.

Attorneys for Saudi Arabia have denied the lawsuit’s merits, maintaining that the kingdom has helped the US combat al-Qaida, its late founder Osama bin Laden, and terrorism in general.

While political speeches are prohibited at the 9/11 memorial ceremonies in New York, the nephew of Lisa Marie Terry, one of the victims of the attacks on the World Trade Center, insisted that “the deep state has hidden the truth about what happened” for 25 years.

He went on to urge Donald Trump to “release the unredacted files that implicate Saudi Arabia while what’s left of the victims families are still alive to see it”.

At the Pentagon, Dan Caine – the chair of the joint chiefs of staff – is now speaking.

“Today is not just a story of loss; it is also a story of great American courage, selfless service, and those who’ve made the choice to carry those memories forward,” Caine said. “The courage of those extraordinary Americans in Pennsylvania, in New York, and right here on these hallowed grounds did not end when the smoke cleared and the fires went out, because survivors and families have carried it forward through service, through sacrifice, and through remembrance.”

US inflation remained high in August as military conflict with Iran continues

US consumer prices remained stubbornly high in August as the end of the ceasefire between the US and Iran pushed energy prices up, according to data from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics released on Friday.

The annualized inflation rate was 3.4%, the same as it was in July. The most recent peak was seen in May, when the inflation rate hit a three-year high at 4.2%. Core inflation, which strips out volatile energy and food prices, increased to 2.4% in August.

Despite dipping from its most recent peak, inflation remains above levels that were seen before the war in Iran, largely because of higher energy prices.

He saw the first one on September 11 itself. “Missing”, said a hastily designed poster on the window of a bar in Tribeca providing food and water to first responders. It was a plea for information about a person who had vanished in the deadliest terrorist attack in US history.

“I was like, ‘Wow, this is going to be …’” recalls Phil Buehler, an artist and photographer who had watched the twin towers collapse from his office window. “But you knew that there would be very few survivors. You knew it then.”

The missing posters kept coming in the days after 9/11, and Buehler kept photographing them. But having amassed more than 500 images, he put them away in a box on a top shelf because he could not bear to look at them.

As the 25th anniversary of the atrocity neared, he decided to revisit them and will put a selection on public display for the first time this week. Countenance: The 9/11 Missing Posters, an installation at Most Holy Trinity – St Mary’s church in Brooklyn, places 144 pictures across 24 translucent vellum panels, in vertical pairs reminiscent of the twin towers.

The World Trade Center was always a favourite subject for Buehler. He had been photographing it since 1973, when he was 16. On September 11 2001, as an advisory board member for a tech startup, he had been invited to a conference at the Windows on the World restaurant at the top of the north tower, but turned the offer down (a friend intended to go but was delayed).

“The guy that invited us died that day and I think my friend feels guilty about it,” he says quietly by phone, recalling the day on which nearly 3,000 people were killed by hijacked airliners in New York, northern Virginia and Pennsylvania. “You realise how close – everybody had a story.”

He has told the story hundreds of times, but it never seems to fade. If anything, it only becomes more vivid. “Oh, gosh,” exclaims Stanley Praimnath, his voice cracking with an “ooooooh!” as if bearing a weight. “It’s like reliving that day.”

Nearly 3,000 people died in the terrorist attacks on New York City and Washington DC on September 11 2001. But Praimnath and another office worker, Brian Clark, were two of only four individuals known to have survived from above the impact zone in either tower of the World Trade Center. The two strangers met in the smoke and fire of the 81st floor of the south tower, and walked down 1,620 steps into the rest of their lives.

Their split-second decisions that day made all the difference. Over the next 25 years, both men moved from middle age into old age and retirement. Both enjoyed family weddings and watched children and grandchildren grow. Both saw US presidents come and go as old wars ended and new wars began. They remained friends through it all.

They also told their story – countless times – in churches, schools, TV studios and elsewhere. “It could be 800, 1,200, a number like that,” Clark estimates.

Praimnath, in a separate interview, muses: “People refer to me as ‘the 9/11 guy’. It doesn’t feel repetitive. It is like unloading baggage from my shoulder, like washing my soul with tears. It is comforting to know that people out there still want to hear.”
