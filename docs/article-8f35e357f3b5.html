Travel remains heavily disrupted across Florida, Alabama, Georgia and Mississippi on Saturday as Isaias continues to stall regional transportation networks.

Airports throughout the Gulf coast are bearing the brunt of the storm’s impact.

Pensacola international airport in Florida remains closed to all commercial traffic as officials conduct safety inspections and assess physical damage after Friday’s shutdown.

Destin-Fort Walton Beach airport in Florida has reopened, but flight operations are severely delayed, with the majority of departing flights canceled on Saturday, according to FlightAware data.

In response to the rolling closures and cancellations, major carriers – including American Airlines, Delta and United Airlines – have issued travel waivers for affected passengers.

A person died in Birmingham, Alabama, after a tree fell on their home overnight, according to local reports citing the Birmingham fire department.

A tree reportedly fell on a home during the night, trapping two people inside. One of the people trapped in the home died, officials said.

This marks the second fatality over this weekend due to fallen trees resulting from the storm. The other reported death took place in Escambia county, Florida, local officials said.

The “significant damage” to homes, businesses and public buildings in Monroe county, Alabama, will take days to repair, the county’s emergency management agency said in a Saturday morning statement.

Monroe county officials also said in a social media post there was “crippling damage to communications and power infrastructure”. They added there were downed trees and power lines throughout the county with several impassable roads.

“Please be patient. There was significant damage to the power grid,” the agency said. “Repairs will take days.”

Around 843,000 people are still without power in the US south-east, according to PowerOutage.com, as Isaias continues to move inland.

“A tree fell on a house and it unfortunately took the life of a lady,” Simmons said in a video published on social media. “And our thoughts and prayers are obviously with that family.”

Florida’s governor, Ron DeSantis, said during an interview with Fox News on Saturday morning that they had not received any reports of fatalities yet in the state.

DeSantis attributed this to likely heightened preparation and previous experience with other tropical storms and hurricanes.

“We’re a peninsula in the middle of a tropical part of the world – we’ve gotta deal with these things,” DeSantis said.

No search and rescue requests after Isaias a ‘good sign’, DeSantis says

Florida’s governor, Ron DeSantis, said no requests for search and rescue assistance had been received following Isaias, which he described as “cautiously, a good sign”.

Speaking to Fox News, he said:

I think that may be a sign that people heeded the warnings, took proper precautions. We sure would love to say we have not gotten a report of a fatality yet, and we’ll see what happens.

There’s also a lot of hazards post-storm that people should be careful of, but so far we’ve had a lot of assets in place that have not been called on, which is a good sign. I’d rather be over-prepared, and not have to use them, than not have what you need.”

He added that emergency crews have been working overnight to clear roads and bridges, restore power and assess damage.

One significant effect of the stronger El Niño is fewer hurricanes in the Atlantic. In the 30 years to 2020, on average the first hurricane arrived by 11 August, the second by 26 August and the third by 7 September.

Isaias’s October arrival marks a stark contrast from this trend.

At the start of the season, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) predicted a quiet year, anticipating between three and six hurricanes, well below the average of seven. The August forecast cut the expected number to between two and six. Until this year, the latest date for the first Atlantic hurricane was 11 September, in 2002 and 2013.

El Niño is marked by comparatively warm sea surface temperatures in the Pacific and a weakening of the seasonal trade winds. One side-effect is that it increases wind shear – the change in wind speed with height – over the Atlantic. This makes it less likely for tropical storms to evolve into hurricanes, as these require relatively stable air to form over a wide area.

But while things have been mostly quiet on the Atlantic side of the Americas, the eastern Pacific has seen plenty of activity, with 10 hurricanes so far.

As a hurricane approaches a coast, the churning winds force ocean water up on to land; atmospheric pressure from the storm also helps squeeze the water ashore. The shallower the continental shelf, the higher the threat of a dangerous surge. The water may take a couple of days to fully subside.

Water is heavy – about 1,700lb, or 770kg, to a cubic yard (0.76 cubic meters) – and it can move fast in a surge, sweeping people to their deaths, throwing about boats and vehicles, and pulverizing structures. Six inches of fast-moving water is enough to knock over an adult, the National Hurricane Center (NHC) says. Surges become even more dangerous as they coincide with high tide.

Hurricane Katrina in 2005 caused storm surges of over 25ft on the Mississippi coastline. The NHC says many of the 1,500 people killed lost their lives, directly or indirectly, due to the storm surges.

The storm is expected to move across Alabama and into the Tennessee Valley later today and Sunday, the National Hurricane Center (NHC) said in its latest advisory.

“Maximum sustained winds have decreased to near 35 mph (55 km/h) with higher gusts. Slow weakening is forecast today, and Isaias is forecast to dissipate on Sunday,” the center said.

The NHC warned heavy rainfall across the south-east increases the risk of flash flooding, with southern Alabama, the Florida Panhandle and south-western Georgia expected to be among the hardest-hit areas, while landslides are also possible in the Southern Appalachians.

Isaias, first Atlantic hurricane of 2026 season, batters US Gulf coast

More than half a million people were without power after hurricane Isaias wreaked havoc on the US Gulf Coast, with Alabama and the Florida Panhandle being hit the hardest by the storm.

The category 2 hurricane made landfall near Destin, Florida, late Friday, with maximum sustained winds of 105 miles per hour. It weakened to a post-tropical cyclone by early morning, with winds of 65 mph, the National Weather Service said.

More than 750,000 people in Florida and Alabama have been left without power, according to poweroutage.us, with outages also reported in Georgia for tens of thousands of customers.

“Things are flying really fast, and I’ve never seen wind this fast before, so it’s intense, it’s scary,” Jonathan Ramos, a 40-year-old government employee, said from his hotel in Pensacola, Florida, where the power went out.

“I don’t recommend people coming down here for fun to do this.”
