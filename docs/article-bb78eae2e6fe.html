Turkey joins Artemis Accords as 71st signatory

2026-09-01 13:34

Turkey signed the Artemis Accords in Washington on Monday, becoming the 71st country to join the US-led framework for peaceful and responsible space exploration.

Turkish Industry and Technology Minister Mehmet Fatih Kacır signed the accords on behalf of Turkey at NASA headquarters, where NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman hosted the ceremony. Turkish Space Agency President Yusuf Kıraç and US Deputy Assistant Secretary of State for Space and Environment Connor Tomlinson also attended.

Kacır said Turkey’s accession marked a new period in its space program and that Ankara aimed to maintain a sustained presence in space while supporting the peaceful use of space. He also linked the decision to growing cooperation between Turkey and the US on space projects.

Isaacman welcomed Turkey as the 71st signatory and said President Donald Trump had directed NASA last year to accelerate its return to the Moon and establish humanity’s first enduring presence on another world. He said the Artemis Accords would help support that effort and that NASA had invited signatories to contribute hardware, scientific payloads, technology demonstrations and other capabilities to future Artemis missions.

Turkey plans to send its first lunar spacecraft, AYAP-1, to the Moon in 2027. Kacır said the mission would use a hybrid propulsion system and collect data from lunar orbit before a planned impact on the lunar surface.

AYAP-1 is being developed by TÜBİTAK Space Technologies Research Institute (TÜBİTAK UZAY) under Turkey’s Lunar Research Program. The spacecraft is designed to orbit the Moon at an altitude of about 100 kilometers for roughly three months before making a controlled hard landing on the surface. Sweden’s Institute of Space Physics is contributing a Lunar Neutrals Telescope to study interactions between the solar wind and the lunar surface.

The lunar mission is part of a broader expansion of Turkey’s space program. Alper Gezeravcı, a Turkish Air Force colonel, became the country’s first astronaut in January 2024, spending about two weeks aboard the International Space Station during Axiom Space’s Ax-3 mission and taking part in scientific experiments. Tuva Cihangir Atasever later conducted a suborbital research mission aboard Virgin Galactic’s VSS Unity.

Gezeravcı has also spoken about Turkey’s plans for the Moon and its spaceport project in Somalia. He said Turkey aimed to become the sixth country to achieve a soft landing on the Moon and described the lunar program as a two-stage process, beginning with a mission involving a controlled hard landing before a later soft-landing mission. He also said the planned Somali spaceport would give Turkey a “critical and key capability” for its own needs and for space operations worldwide.

Turkey first announced the Somali spaceport project during a joint news conference with President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and Somali President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud in İstanbul. The project is being carried out under a cooperation agreement between Turkey and Somalia, under which Somalia allocated land for the facility. Kacır has said Somalia was selected after feasibility studies identified it as the most suitable location.

Turkey completed feasibility and design studies for the spaceport in December 2025. The facility’s location near the equator would give Turkey access to a launch latitude that can offer efficiency advantages for certain orbital missions because of the Earth’s rotation.

Turkey will also host the International Astronautical Congress in Antalya in October, with dozens of Artemis Accords signatories expected to attend. NASA said the congress would provide an opportunity to discuss the future of peaceful and transparent space exploration.

The Artemis Accords were established in 2020 by NASA and the US State Department together with seven founding countries as a set of non-binding principles for civil space exploration. They build on the 1967 Outer Space Treaty and cover issues including peaceful exploration, transparency, scientific data sharing, emergency assistance, registration of space objects and preventing harmful interference between space activities.

The accords also set out principles for the use of space resources and exploration of the Moon, Mars and other celestial bodies. Turkey’s accession brings its lunar program into a US-led international framework that has expanded from its original group of signatories into a broader coalition focused on future lunar and deep-space exploration.
