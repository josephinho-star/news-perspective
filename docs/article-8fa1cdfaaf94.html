Sylvester Stallone endured broke, lonely days cleaning lion cages before Henry Winkler changed his life

2026-11-26 20:00

Before Sylvester Stallone became a Hollywood heavyweight, he was a broke, lonely actor living in a cramped New York City walk-up apartment with few friends to lean on — until Henry Winkler came along.

In his memoir, "The Steps," the "Rocky" icon recalls crossing paths with the future "Happy Days" star on the set of 1974's "The Lords of Flatbush." Though Stallone joked they were polar opposites — Winkler, a Yale graduate, and Stallone from "the college of what's the matter [with] you" — the two forged an instant bond.

For Winkler, it didn't take long to see Stallone's fierce determination to make it in Hollywood. It was a drive he witnessed firsthand when he visited his co-star's home on Lexington Avenue for the first time.

"He was living there with his first wife and their bull mastiff, who had more slobber than the Atlantic Ocean," Winkler recalled to Fox News Digital.

"He painted his windows black so that he would never be bothered by what time of day it was. He wouldn't be influenced by time. Instead, he just wrote and wrote. And from the minute we met on the set of a movie in which we made no money whatsoever, you could see him. You could see his drive. You could see there was something so special, so funny, so articulate coming out of this, 'I'm going to eat you for breakfast.' And it only continued."

"If you listen to Sly Stallone, you quickly realize he's a philosopher," Winkler shared. "You would never put the two together, but it's true. And he's so open. His upbringing would put any man or woman down. It would put them in a corner shaking for the rest of their life. But his motto is 'keep on punching' and it's true. He kept on punching. And it's been an honor to be his friend."

In his book, Stallone described how Winkler had to "step over a homeless guy while walking up five flights of stairs" just to see his "shabby place and get a glimpse into my world." Instead of being horrified, Winkler was impressed by the library of tattered books and a shelf holding about 15 screenplays. Stallone had been keeping busy writing.

When Winkler picked up one of Stallone's scripts, he was stunned. Winkler urged Stallone to keep writing.

Stallone told Fox News Digital he never forgot Winkler's kindness and encouragement during a time when hearing "no" was the norm.

"You cannot get two people more different in every aspect," Stallone said with a laugh. "We were, as I wrote, an 'odd couple.' And yet, he gave me such confidence. He's an intellectual, and I didn't think of myself that way. I didn't see myself that way at all. When he saw the writing, he said, 'You wrote all this stuff?' I said, 'It's not good.' He then said, 'That's not the point. You did 15, 15 scripts. It's harder to make it as a writer than an actor. This is impressive.'"

Stallone wrote, "We were an odd couple. He was an intellectual Jewish kid from the Upper West Side who graduated from the Yale School of Drama — the real deal. I had to learn everything the hard way. We were anomalies in each other's lives. To see us together was like watching a zebra hang out with a buffalo. Still, we clicked, because Henry is a genuinely wonderful human being."

Years later, after scraping by in New York and taking odd jobs like working at a deli, ushering at a movie theater and cleaning lion cages at the Central Park Zoo, Stallone headed west. He and his then-wife, Sasha Czack, made the cross-country trip to California in hopes of finally getting his big acting break. But they knew no one on the West Coast and didn't have a place to live. Stallone wrote, "We had barely $500 left of the $830 we departed New York with."

But there was one person Stallone knew he could call: Winkler. By then, the actor had already moved to California and become a star on "Happy Days."

"When I came out to California, he was the only guy I knew," said Stallone. "It was comforting because, again, he was very complimentary and welcoming. He kept saying, 'Working with you on "Lords of Flatbush" helped me with The Fonz, playing a tough guy.' I kept telling him, 'No, man, you did that all on your own.' But he saw something in me, even before ‘Rocky.’ Yes, this was a long time ago, but that's how great of a guy he was. He still is."

"Really, our relationship goes back to 1972," Stallone said with a smile. "It's amazing. Again, we're so different, yet there's a bond there that he actually instigated. I was a loner, no doubt about that. And yet he would insert himself, talk to me, be very outgoing and witty. He wanted to be my friend. And he was from Yale. He didn't have to be my friend. But he genuinely cared. We bonded and it's terrific."

Stallone and Czack eventually settled into a rental in Encino for $300 a month. The house was barely 500 square feet and there was no grass or air conditioning. Still, Stallone said he was determined to make it big in Hollywood.

In the book, Stallone described first arriving at Hollywood and Vine. He had no idea that the nearby Walk of Fame would one day bear his name.

"That I would have a star there less than 10 years later, and only a few blocks away, still boggles my mind," he wrote.

Winkler said he never doubted that one day, his friend’s dreams would become a reality.

"I called him one day and I said, 'You're like a fine wine. You were great, and now you have blossomed.' He just kept punching. Isn't he amazing? He punched his way to the top."
