Pentagon has no department-wide plan to address munitions shortages, watchdog report finds

2026-11-19 19:24

Despite a munitions shortfall exacerbated by the Trump administration’s war in Iran, the Pentagon does not have a department-wide plan to address the issue long-term, a federal watchdog said in a new report released Thursday.

The Government Accountability Office found that while the Defense Department has taken steps toward increasing its munitions inventory, it “doesn’t have an overarching plan that identifies long-term goals, strategies, or ways to measure its progress.”

As part of the problem, the Pentagon does not have a dedicated office with authority to improve munition inventories, making it harder for the department “to know whether its munitions production efforts are really addressing department-wide needs.”

The status of U.S. munitions reserves remains murky at best, but what is known is that Washington blew through a significant portion of its long-range cruise missiles and interceptors in the beginning of the Iran war, which began on Feb. 28.

In March, for example, it was reported that U.S. forces burned through at least 850 Tomahawk missiles in the first month of the conflict known as Operation Epic Fury. It’s estimated the Pentagon has just a few thousand left.

And in August, Reuters reported the Army had used up “virtually all” of its global stockpile of long-range missiles such as ATACMS and Precision Strike Missiles.

While top administration officials have insisted the U.S. military has the weapons needed for an intense military offensive against Iran should fighting resume, Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao on Tuesday revealed this week that his service recoded and fired missiles he described as “expired.”

“A lot of the munitions we used in Epic Fury, we were able to recode those,” Cao said during a Senate Armed Services Committee hearing on his nomination to become Navy secretary.

“They were code echo, code foxtrot, which means they’re expired. But, you know, I came from a poor family, right? Things like bread doesn’t expire. You just rub some water on it, microwave it for 15 seconds and it’s good to go,” he said.

Reports also have suggested that weapons shortages are a factor in persuading President Trump to hold back on escalating attacks against Iran.

“A key part of military readiness is having a reliable supply of munitions, including bombs, missiles, and torpedoes. But the Department of Defense has faced persistent munitions shortages,” the GAO wrote in a summary of its new report. “The 2026 conflict with Iran only reinforced the challenges with the munitions inventory.”

“DOD has taken steps toward increasing its inventory, but could do more,” the watchdog adds, noting that in lieu of a plan, the Pentagon and the military services “can be reactive to global events rather than driven by total requirements.”

And while DOD has a department-wide munitions data tool to track munitions, the GAO found that it has limited oversight over this instrument has not regularly conducted inventory check ins with the services.

The watchdog recommended that the Pentagon come up with an overarching plan to improve inventory levels that links building munitions and funding to department requirements and include long-term goals, strategies to achieve them and ways to gauge progress.

In addition, it suggests the Defense secretary identify an office to implement the munitions plan and set regular timelines to check munitions inventories.

The Hill reached out to the Pentagon for comment on the report.
