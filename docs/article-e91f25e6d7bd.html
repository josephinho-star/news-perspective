Trump administration delays Medicaid work requirement enforcement

2026-11-13 23:19

The Trump administration is delaying the implementation of controversial Medicaid work requirements, giving states an extra six months to prepare.

The work requirements, which Republicans established in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act last year, will still technically go into effect Jan. 1. But states will have an optional “hold harmless” transition period that will run through the end of June, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) said Friday.

States that implement the hold harmless period will assess whether individuals meet the work requirement or are excluded from it, but they will not deny applications or disenroll otherwise eligible individuals solely for failure to meet the requirement, the CMS said.

The delay will also give states an opportunity to learn from the early months of implementation to “identify and address workforce training needs and improve the beneficiary experience,” the CMS said.

Once the requirements are enforced, millions of Americans will have to start proving they work, volunteer or attend school at least 80 hours per month — or risk getting kicked off their health insurance.

“States have made real progress, and they’ve been clear with us about what it takes to get this right for beneficiaries—at scale and for the long term,” Dan Brillman, deputy administrator and director of the Center for Medicaid and CHIP Services, said in a statement.

“If the state can use data to show that someone is working or qualifies for an exclusion, that person should not have to track down paperwork to prove it,” he added. “And when someone needs support, they should know exactly what to do, when to do it, and where to go.”

The CMS has been facing pressure from states and Democratic lawmakers to delay the effective date.

States said the implementation was rushed from the start, as the law gave less than 18 months from enactment to the go-live date. They said they weren’t given enough time to set up the verification systems needed to enforce the new requirements.

Advocates said the rushed implementation risked millions more people becoming entangled in paperwork and administrative red tape and losing coverage.

According to the Congressional Budget Office, about 5 million fewer people are expected to be enrolled in Medicaid over the next 10 years because of the work requirements; most of those people are expected to be uninsured.

Last month, a federal judge put more uncertainty into the process when he ruled the CMS was too strict when it outlined how people can be considered too “medically frail” to work.

Democratic-led states had sued the CMS over its regulation, charging it went further than what Congress intended.
