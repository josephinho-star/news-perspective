Why the EU is failing to act on growing public criticism of Israel? - analysis

When the British government announced sweeping sanctions on trade with illegal Israeli settlements and condemned “ethnic cleansing” in the West Bank by state-backed “settler terrorists”, some EU diplomats hoped this dramatic shift in UK government policy towards Israel would have a galvanising effect on the rest of Europe. So far, it has not.

Although France and Canada joined the UK in pledging to ban goods from illegal settlements and impose sanctions on “those who facilitate them or profit from them”, there has not yet been a wider ripple effect.

A meeting of EU foreign affairs ministers on Monday is likely to showcase the bloc’s stasis in policy towards Israel – a stalemate that is at odds with rising public criticism in Europe of Israel’s government.

A survey of 10 European countries, including Spain, France and Sweden, found large majorities of people had a negative opinion of Israel. Views had become more negative compared with the previous year, according to the Pew Research Center, mostly after the start of the US-Israeli war on Iran, which unleashed a new crisis in the Middle East and a global economic shock. In Poland, negative views jumped eight points to 70% of respondents, in Italy they increased by nine points to 75% and in Germany they also rose by nine points to 73%.

Israel is facing ever deeper isolation in the theatre of public opinion. Five countries chose to boycott the Eurovision song contest this year over Israel’s participation, after organisers declined to exclude it over its conduct in the Gaza war and rising violence in the West Bank. Organisers have now decided that broadcasters will no longer be eligible to host the song contest if their state is involved in an armed conflict or sensitive geopolitical situation.

More than 74,000 people, including at least 21,200 children, have been killed in Gaza since Israel launched its war in retaliation for the Hamas attack of 7 October 2023, in which the terrorist group killed 1,209 people – including 36 children – and took 251 hostages, inflicting a nationwide trauma.

Gaza remains a humanitarian catastrophe, with people facing acute shortages of water, nutritious food and medical equipment and medicines, while contending with disease, unemployment, economic collapse and the threat of death or injury. Israel’s airstrikes continue: the death toll includes 1,400 Palestinians killed since a ceasefire was declared a year ago.

The EU talks on the Middle East will focus on the conflicts in Iran and Yemen, but ministers will also discuss the situation in Gaza and the West Bank in the context of elections in Israel on 27 October and the first legislative elections in Palestine in 20 years, which are expected by late November.

The discussion is expected to expose familiar fault lines in the 27-member EU.One group of countries are vocal supporters of the Palestinian cause: Spain and Ireland were the first to call for a review of whether Israel’s conduct in the Gaza war was in breach of the human rights requirements of its association agreement with the EU, which covers the €68bn (£58bn) trade relationship, cooperation in research, environment and energy.

A second group consistently opposes targeting Israel’s government: Germany and Austria are close allies for historical reasons, while Czechia and Italy are ideologically aligned with Benjamin Netanyahu’s administration.

Among a third, larger group of countries in the middle are several, such as France and the Netherlands, that are increasingly critical of the Netanyahu government’s conduct in Gaza and its plans to build the vast E1 settlement, which threatens to critically damage the two-state solution.

President Pavel returns from holidays as Czechs go to polls

Czechs go to the polls on Friday at the start of two days of voting to fill one-third of the seats in Parliament’s upper house, the Senate, and elect local representatives, AP reported.

The elections are the first test for populist billionaire Andrej Babiš since he returned to power after a decisive victory in parliamentary elections in October 2025.

His centrist ANO (YES) movement hopes to make gains in the 81-seat Senate, which is controlled by pro-western opposition parties. Twenty-seven Senate seats are up for grabs in the two-round election, with runoffs scheduled for next week. First-round results are expected on Saturday.

Babiš has formed a governing coalition with two small political groups whose agenda includes reducing support for Ukraine and rejecting some key European Union policies.

Unlike the government, the opposition and president Petr Pavel remain firm supporters of Ukraine following Russia’s invasion.

But Pavel – an avid biker – attracted a lot of attention in the build up to the vote as it emerged that he would miss the vote due to a long-planned motorcycle trip in Morocco. After days of relentless pressure, he eventually cut the holiday short and reported to vote, acknowledging his original “error” in scheduling.

“Some mistakes can be corrected relatively easily, others with more difficulty, and some cannot be corrected at all.

Fortunately, a mistake in planning can be remedied by changing the plan. Admitting a mistake is not easy, but if a politician values people’s trust, he must prove it.

I know what responsibility is and that any trust is hard to earn, but terribly easy and quick to lose. You pay for mistakes, and I have learned from this one of my own.”

EU grants temporary trade preferences to Armenia after Russian sanctions

The European Union is granting temporary trade preferences to Armenia following trade restrictions recently imposed by Russia.

In a move which shows how flexible the EU can be, it said the temporary trade preferences “will help alleviate the difficult situation faced by Armenian producers and exporters and support the resilience of Armenia’s economy by facilitating access to the EU market.

The measures, which will enter into force tomorrow for a period of two years, temporarily allow 80% of Armenian exports to the EU, including almost all exports of fresh fruit, vegetables and flowers affected by the Russian measures, as well as over 91% of exports of beverages and spirits.

Armenia is not an accession state, but has been singled out for support as part of the EU a broader solidarity support package

Schröder's cosy encounter with Putin sparks outrage in Berlin

The Kremlin’s release of photos showing a cosy fireside encounter between former German chancellor Gerhard Schröder and Russian president Vladimir Putin on the occasion of Putin’s 74th birthday, reportedly at his home in Moscow, has sparked outrage in political Berlin.

We understand that Schröder travelled to Moscow for his latest lucrative business role in Russia. In addition to his positions on the advisory boards of various gas companies – which he has retained, refusing to distance himself from Putin despite his punitive war on Ukraine – Schröder, 82, has now reportedly taken up a position as an advisory board member of the Russian retailer Globus, advising on strategy since September and drawing on his influence and extensive network of contacts.

The pictures, emphasing the political and personal alliance between the two men, jarred with many in Berlin.

Schröder had said in early 2025 that he was too ill to attend a parliamentary inquiry committee into the construction of the Nord Stream 2 gas pipeline between Russia and Germany, citing “burnout”. This is the latest of numerous controversial trips Schröder has taken to Russia, even following the 2022 full invasion of Ukraine.

Katrin Göring-Eckardt of the Greens said she was appalled that Schröder had been “too sick” to attend a commission into the Nord Stream 2 pipeline in person, but was able to fly to Moscow. She called the revelation a “shame for Germany” and a “betrayal of Europe”.

Marie-Agnes Strack-Zimmermann, who leads the European parliament’s Committee on Security and Defence called Schröder “Germany’s most brazen pensioner”.

According to the Kremlin, Putin only learned of Schröder’s presence in Moscow via media outlets, and invited him over to celebrate.

The two pictures posted by the Kremlin show the two pals in open-necked shirts and jackets – Putin unusually casual in blue jeans – warmly shaking hands in front of a blazing fire, a beaming Schröder placing his left hand on the small of Putin’s back. It’s the height of gemütlichkeit.

Mawkish fascination draws the eye to the Russian autocrat’s interior design of which we rarely get a glimpse: a traditional ceramic stove, miniature golden rocking horses dotted about the room, a gleaming samovar, soft furnishings in striking traditional patterns, colourful drapes, and intimate niches.

These details, along with Putin’s almost shy, sheepish grin in the picture, belie the terror he has come to represent. The image is a PR coup for both, helping Putin prove that Russia is certainly not fully isolated, and showing Schröder’s complete indifference to the fact that his proximity to Putin has shattered his domestic reputation.

(Schröder did eventually testify via video link to the Nord Stream committee in October 2025, where he staunchly defended the pipeline’s construction and the formation of the controversial Climate Protection Foundation but denied Russia had exerted undue pressure on either project or on him.)

US imposes sanctions on international criminal court, hours after former judge Pillay wins Nobel

US president Donald Trump’s administration imposed sanctions on the international criminal court on Friday, hours after one of its former judges won the Nobel Peace Prize, putting Washington on a diplomatic collision course with European allies, Reuters reported.

The ICC’s deputy prosecutor, Nazhat Shameem Khan, condemned the sanctions and said they would not halt the tribunal’s work.

The Trump administration has long been hostile to the court and has previously imposed sanctions on its prosecutors and judges. Friday’s move would go far further, by potentially punishing companies that provide services to the court itself.

“President Trump will never allow the international criminal court to prosecute Americans,” secretary of state Marco Rubio said in a statement reviewed by Reuters ahead of the official announcement.

“That is why, today, the United States is sanctioning the international criminal court. We will ban transactions with this rogue court, cutting off their resources and crippling its ability to operate.”

ICC Deputy Prosecutor Khan told Reuters the sanctions “must be condemned“.

“The sanctions are intended to undermine the rule of law internationally and the work that an independent judicial institution is doing,” Khan said. “Our work will continue across all our situations, our investigations and our prosecutions.“

Washington has long been angry over ICC arrest warrants issued for Israeli officials including prime minister Benjamin Netanyahu, and over a past investigation into actions of US soldiers in Afghanistan which the ICC has since closed.

The court, founded in 2002 to prosecute war crimes, crimes against humanity and genocide, counts 125 countries as member states, including all EU countries and all members of the Nato alliance apart from the United States and Turkey.

It has received strong public backing in the past from member countries including major US allies, and is based in the Netherlands, an EU and Nato member.

Navi Pillay, the South African jurist who won the Nobel Peace Prize on Friday for her work in international criminal justice, served as a judge on the ICC’s Appeals Chamber from 2003-2008. In its citation, the prize committee said it had sought to “issue a reminder” at a time when “judges and institutions of international law are under political attack“.

Rubio said the sanctions had been structured to give the court and its member states an opportunity to address US concerns.

He did not provide further details, but a senior state department official said the US Treasury would issue a set of licenses “to protect American interests“, an apparent reference to temporary exemptions to some sanctions.

US sanctions typically bar all US-based companies from any work with a sanctioned organisation, forcing banks to shut accounts, insurers to end coverage and software companies to halt cooperation.

The Trump administration previously imposed targeted sanctions on court officials during his first term, which were rescinded under President Joe Biden. Washington announced new sanctions just days after Trump returned to office in January 2025.

Court officials have been preparing for months for the prospect of sanctions on the court itself, shifting office computers from Microsoft software to open source equivalents and making new arrangements for banking and health insurance.

The court’s registrar Osvaldo Zavala Giler called earlier this week for the EU to be prepared to use a so-called “blocking mechanism” that lets it shield European countries from the extraterritorial effects of sanctions.

EU reaches deal with China to 'moderate' sales of hybrid electric cars, Šefčovič says

The EU says it has reached a landmark deal with China to “moderate” sales of Chinese hybrid electric cars in the EU.

It is global first, EU’s trade commissioner Maroš Šefčovič told reporters.

The deal “opens the prospect of cutting China’s export [of hybrids] by more than a half”.

“It is the first time that China has accepted to moderate its exports without going through the prior” trade investigations, said Šefčovič at a press conference in Beijing just now.

Šefčovič said the deal on hybrid cars was “very, very hard to accomplish these results”.

“This is the result of very intense, work. And it reflects also the appreciation of the situation on both sides. The European car industry is under a lot of stress. And therefore this step was, absolutely crucial. But I think that what we agreed is fair, we, are working in a full compliance, with, WTO, methodology,” he has just told reporters.

China reaches 'an understanding' with the EU to stop trade tensions

China has reached an “understanding” with the EU, to avert deterioration in trade tensions after two days of talks in Beijing, according to a Chinese statement.

EU trade commissioner Maroš Šefčovič is due to hold a press conference around 3pm CET, but China says the two sides have agreed a 16 point agreement allowing for a relaxation of restrictions on rare earth exports from China to the EU in return for concessions on electric vehicle exports.

China is “willing... to continue facilitating the approval of export licenses for rare earths and permanent magnets destined for the EU”, a statement from the Chinese ministry of commerce has said following two days of talks in Beijing.

While they acknowledged “differences” between them, the two sides were committed to maintaining trade and to hold a further meeting in March.

China said both sides would adhere to “procedures concerning company price undertakings” of hybrid cars, Beijing’s commerce ministry said, citing a joint statement, suggesting a deal to find minimum prices for hybrid cars exported to the EU.

The talks follow months of rising tensions between the 27-nation EU and the Asian powerhouse that threatened to escalate into a trade war.

Maroš Šefčovič said earlier in the day that the EU’s trade deficit with running at around £1bn a day (€1.18bn) every day was “unsustainable”.
