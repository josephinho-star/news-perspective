US army calls Iowa lead detonation a ‘huge success’ and says threat of spontaneous explosions has been ‘eliminated’ – live

2026-11-25 16:30

Army calls detonation a 'HUGE success' and says 'the threat of an uncontrolled spontaneous detonation has been eliminated'

In a statement online, Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said that that “the Army’s controlled, mitigated detonation exceeded all expectations and was a HUGE success. The threat of an uncontrolled spontaneous detonation has been eliminated.”

“I am so proud of our SOLDIERS for their courage, training, mitigation, and precision! They put it on the line for all of us just like this every day!” Telle said. He also thanked President Trump and his administration “for mobilizing an unprecedented whole of government approach to fix this problem once and for all tor the great people of Iowa.”

The Army said that it is also monitoring the EPA’s live lead particle tracking operation, and that as of 11am ET “there are no readings so far above 0.0.”

“To the people of Iowa, we are with you all the way” Telle added. “We will update the public with more information throughout the day.”

The EPA says that "real-time air monitors" are reporting 0 detection of airborne lead throughout the region

In a post on social media at 11:15am ET, the EPA said that all “real-time air monitors are reporting ZERO detection of airborne lead throughout the region” and that the EPA monitors “never stopped collecting data throughout the event”.

Iowa Governor Kim Reynolds has echoed what other state and federal officials have said, and said that the initial feedback is indicating that the detonation “was successful”.

“We remain in close contact with authorities on the ground, while state agencies continue to monitor incoming data from the State Emergency Operations Center” Reynolds said. “We will keep Iowans informed as details become available.”

Reynolds also said that the Army is set to hold a press conference at 11 am CST, which is Noon ET.

Lee Zeldin, the EPA Administrator, said that he has been told that the blast “caved inward perfectly”.

“When the army was planning its mitigation efforts, there were a number of mitigation actions that were taken that that included an effort to get the detonation to go go an explosive effect downland and toward unoccupied land within the Iowa Army ammunition plant” Zeldin told CNN. “This message that I just received … first off, the air monitors are showing a remaining zero detection, and as far as the blast itself, it caved inward perfectly.”

“The decision point that that the army was facing was you either do this controlled, mitigated detonation, or you end up with a spontaneous detonation, and the spontaneous detonation could take place at a moment where you don’t have optimal weather conditions” he said. “That detonation could be going upwards into the air, causing more of an issue as it relates to the dispersion of lead and the goal of the Army working with the interagency partners and others experts from outside the government, was instead of having this detonate upwards into the air, was to take mitigation efforts to have this detonate down into the ground.”

“As they were applying more and more mitigation efforts and talking to the experts, they were continuing to drive home the goal and the hope and predictability that the detonation would be driving this downwards into the ground rather than upwards into the air” he added.

FEMA has urged residents in the local area who did not evacuate to shelter in place and continue to follow guidance issued by the EPA and others, and to wait for the all clear to go outside by officials.

FEMA also said that it has provided safe lodging to those who evacuated.

Lead azide, which typically appears as a white powder or needle-like crystals, is a explosive chemical compound of lead used mainly in detonators, National Center for Biotechnology Information.

The US army said that a routine inspection in March at the Iowa facility found that “decades-old, stored lead azide had partially crystallized, making it increasingly sensitive to friction, shock, and cold winter temperatures.”

The army said that the deterioration had “created a new and growing risk of spontaneous, uncontrolled detonation.”

The Army described lead azide as a “highly sensitive explosive” which becomes “increasingly unstable as it deteriorates”.

A resident of Burlington, Iowa, told the Associated Press that he was disappointed by the blast’s size.

The resident, Tim Brandt, told the news outlet that he wanted to see a big boom and feel the ground shake, but that instead, the detonation reminded him of the noisemakers that crack when thrown on the ground.

“It was nothing,” he said. “It seemed like government put fear in a lot of people over nothing.”

Illinois governor JB Pritzker said that Illinois is expected to be “minimally affected” by the detonation in Iowa, but that the state still remains on alert.

“Protecting our people’s safety and wellbeing is our top priority as we continue to monitor the conditions” he said on Wednesday. “Stay safe and stay informed, Illinoisans.”

EPA official says that initial lead levels near the blast 'are coming in so low it is equalling to no detection'

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin also told CNN on Wednesday morning that that he is being told that “lead levels are coming so low that it is equalling to no detection and that’s coming from some sensors closest to the detonation.”

“What I have directed my team to do … we’re gonna get this stuff to the lab, when we collect these samples … as expedited as possible, we will be posting it as quickly as we can” he said.

Zeldin also told CNN that it is an “extraordinarily good sign when the early numbers come in so low that it equals essentially a non-detect, a zero detect.”

The EPA’s air monitors around the ammunition plant did not report any lead in the air during the first 15 minutes after the blast, according to the Associated Press.
