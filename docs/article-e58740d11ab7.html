These funds tout hefty income tied to the tech trade – if you’re willing to deal with the complexity

2026-11-08 11:50

A new type of exchange-traded fund offers attractive coupons tied to the performance of the tech trade, but the recent rout in artificial intelligence names underscores investors' need to perform their due diligence before getting in. Autocallable ETFs aim to give retail investors access to a strategy that's typically available to the institutional investor set. The funds tout weighted average coupons that exceed 10%, but the drivers of these rates can be complicated to grasp. "It's a tricky area of the market," said Brendan McCann, senior associate manager research analyst at Morningstar. "They're not the most simple funds; they're not the most simple instruments. There are lots of variables." These autocallable ETFs are also fairly new. The oldest offering on the books, the Calamos Autocallable Income ETF (CAIE) turned a year old in June and has about $1.4 billion in net assets as of Nov. 5. Several offerings have put a twist on the theme, tying the yields they offer to the performance of tech-related assets, be they single stocks like Nvidia , indexes like the Nasdaq-100 or portfolios of growth-oriented names. These so-called reference assets are being put to the test in this latest bout of volatility as investors question the AI trade after Democrats' resounding Election Day victories . South Carolina Democrat Annie Andrews won a Senate seat after calling for a moratorium on new data centers and dubbing her opponent Sen. Darline Graham "Data Center Darline." The Nasdaq Composite , which dipped into correction territory Thursday, notched a weekly loss of 3.4%. The State Street Technology Select Sector SPDR ETF (XLK) posted its worst week since April 2025, down 8.2%. A complex way to generate portfolio income Banks issue autocallable yield notes, which are set to mature by a stated term. These instruments use embedded options and are linked to the performance of an underlying index or asset. The note pays a coupon if this asset meets a set of conditions, such as staying above a stated level. Depending on the strategy, the notes may take up to five years to mature. The issuer can call them back after a year if the index is at or above its initial level. There is also some element of downside protection from these notes: They pay a coupon if the underlying index doesn't fall below a set percentage. This is known as a barrier. Autocallable ETFs generally use a portfolio of these notes, but the structure can vary from one fund family to the next. Though the possibility of hefty yields and potential downside protection may sound attractive, these ETFs aren't risk-free. For instance, a note may not make its coupon payment or your principal may decline under certain circumstances. That can happen if your underlying asset slides sharply below the negative barrier in a lengthy downturn. Further, autocallable ETFs that track a limited portfolio of stocks – or even just one stock, as some funds do – have another element of risk. "With individual stocks [as reference assets] you have greater fluctuations day to day, so it's much more likely you're going to have missed coupons," said McCann. "It's not unheard of for a few stocks within the portfolio to be below the coupon barrier." A few beat-up tech shares Investors in tech-focused autocallable ETFs should expect to be along for the bumpy ride in that sector. The ARK Active Autocallable Income ETF (ARKY) , just launched in August, brings the firm's tech focus to the space, but one of the reference stocks in its portfolio has fallen just below the coupon barrier as of Nov. 5: Oklo, a nuclear power AI play that's down 67% in 2026. The note tied to that stock is at risk of missing its coupon, but ARKY has a "memory" feature that allows investors to recover the payment if the reference stock rebounds and climbs above the negative barrier before maturity, according to Tom Staudt, chief operating officer at ARK. "You're getting paid because these names are volatile," he said. "If these were lower volatility equities, you'd have lower income payments associated with the names." ARKY's portfolio also contains 45 autocallables, which Staudt noted offers the benefit of diversification in the event one of the reference assets fell below its barrier. There's also a slate of single-stock autocallable ETFs that follow the performance of Palantir Technologies , Super Micro Computer and Nvidia – and these are especially risky for income-seeking investors. "The stocks that these single-stock ETFs target tend to be more volatile stocks," said McCann, adding that he "wouldn't rely" on income from single-stock autocallables. Do your homework Investors who are curious about autocallable ETFs should expect to perform a lot of due diligence. They will need to understand the underlying asset, said Matt Kaufman, head of ETFs at Calamos Investments. "How does it behave through difficult markets?" he said. "How many times do you receive the coupon? How many times does it turn off?" Kaufman added that diversification is another consideration. "If you have three notes, 10 notes, that's not a very well-diversified experience," he added, noting that losing a payment on a coupon in that case could be a "big hit." Fees should also be top of mind, as they can easily top 0.8%. "It's important to educate yourself on what they are before you buy in, without looking at the yield and saying 'that's great,'" said Morningstar's McCann.
