Australian politics live: Pauline Hanson offers qualified apologies to Albanese and Indigenous Australians over podcast comments

2026-09-15 02:42

Hanson offers qualified apologies to Albanese and Indigenous Australians

Well about 10 minutes after her interview on News24, Pauline Hanson has done somewhat of an about-turn and apologised for her comments about Indigenous Australians and about Anthony Albanese’s mother – both which have been widely condemned.

Speaking to a very large crowd of journalists squeezed into the press gallery corridor, Hanson says her comments were “not distasteful” towards Albanese’s mother but then apologises.

I don’t think my comments were derogatory towards his mother at all. I was referring to housing commission at that time. So if he’s taken offence to it, well, I’m sorry for that.

When asked about her comments on Indigenous Australians, calling them the “most primitive race” she also eventually apologises.

All I’ve asked is for accountability from our … governments and from our politicians to address these concerns and stop using it as a political football, and I will continue to talk about this.

If I’ve offended people by using that [word], yes, I will apologise for that.

‘We never spoke about a gas reservation policy’: Hanson denies Guardian report

Pauline Hanson says her party has “never” spoken about a gas reservation policy, after my colleague Sarah Martin published a story this morning that One Nation quietly dropped the plan due to their patron Gina Rinehart.

She tells the press gallery her policy was to adopt a Norwegian-style tax on gas exports.

Unfortunately for Hanson, the internet exists.

Here she is in March introducing a bill for a 15% gas reserve – in a social media post on One Nation’s account.

And even a quick look at One Nation’s own website shows it was once on the table:

One Nation has previously considered an East Coast gas reservation policy. However, through consultation with industry and stakeholders, it became clear that it fell short of our policy objectives.

Hanson tells the press gallery:

We never spoke about a gas reservation policy. We spoke about the government having a, like Norwegian gas over there, that they play a part in the exploration plus the development of the gas. The gas reservation policy that the government is advocating at 20% is not going to work.

But that is still under consultation with these companies to ensure that we utilise our resources to the best of our abilities, without the multinationals taking a lot out of this country, which both political parties have allowed to happen, and we have not had our returns on our resources.

She looks physically uncomfortable as she answers and then shortly after ends the press conference.

Tuvalu’s climate minister says ‘pushing people to move is not a solution’ for island nation

Tuvalu’s climate change and environment minister has said migration should not be considered the solution for the low-lying nation as it deals with the effects of global heating.

Speaking at The Australia Institute’s climate integrity summit in Sydney, Dr Maina Talia said the reality of the climate crisis for the small island nation “keeps me awake at night”. He said:

Every time we think of the climate impact in Tuvalu, we are not just simply talking about a climate rescue plan. We are thinking of the … people that [do] not want to leave.

Australia has offered up to 280 visas to Tuvaluans annually under a world first agreement as rising seas threaten the low-lying nation.

In October, world leaders will visit Tuvalu as part of preparatory events leading into Cop31. Talia said addressing Tuvalu’s “economic vulnerability” resulting from the climate crisis needed to be a priority.

It’s scary. Even with this issue of migration attached to the climate change narrative, it’s scary. And it’s blurry because we don’t want to encourage people to move … It’s a matter of choice.

People have the freedom to leave but that should not be the solution for climate change. Pushing people to move is not a solution.

We should not trade life with migration … that’s immoral.

Pauline Hanson is not happy about her old comments being resurfaced by the media, and says everyone is trying to drag her party down.

Her comments on Indigenous Australians were from a podcast released in September last year, while comments about Anthony Albanese’s mother from another podcast in February came to light this week – both received wide condemnation.

She claims that no other politicians have been treated the way she has.

Someone’s out there digging up whatever dirt that I said how many years ago … Everything that’s happening, through my office and other staffers [who] have worked in other political parties’ offices, said we’ve never, ever, ever seen anything like this. Of what they’re trying to put on to One Nation, drags down all the time. Wake up to yourselves because the real enemy there is the Albanese, Labor, Greens government.

‘I’ve always put my foot in my mouth’: Hanson refuses to apologise over comments on Indigenous Australians

Pauline Hanson has refused to apologise for her comments calling Indigenous Australians the “most primitive race”, and has doubled down on the claim.

She concedes she has always “put my foot in my mouth” but then tells News24 that she said in a podcast that Indigenous Australians “were” the most primitive race when Australia was colonised, and backs that comment now.

I’ve always put my foot in my mouth and why I come out and say things. I’m not the most eloquent speaker and how I deliver something, what I say.

Host, Keiran Gilbert asks if Hanson is proud of the thousands of generations of knowledge of Indigenous communities in Australia, the oldest continuous culture on Earth.

She says it’s part of Australia’s heritage but that the “victimisation” needs to stop. She says she’s worried about Indigenous communities living in “third world conditions”.

Eventually she says she “regrets” the comments but still won’t apologise:

Well, then I regret saying that because I don’t want to upset people. That’s not my intentions. That wasn’t my intentions to hurt people. My intentions are keeping people together. I want to address the rape that’s going on. The Indigenous, most people in their prisons, about 37% in their prisons. We’ve got alcohol abuse. We’ve got domestic violence abuse. We’ve got kids in poverty.

Hanson denies migration plan will send country into recession

Pauline Hanson has pushed back against criticism that One Nation has released a migration policy without costings or detail, and says she doesn’t believe the plan will punt the country into recession.

Speaking to News24 in a live interview – her first time talking about the policy since it was released yesterday – Hanson says she’s “sick of listening” to bureaucrats and big business.

Hanson says Australia is in a per capita recession – which is true – but can’t explain how her plan of removing migrants from the country will fix that. She adds that she doesn’t trust economists.

Do you trust these economists what they come out and say? Because I don’t. I don’t believe they always get it right as well.

By taking the migration figures back to 2017, did the economy crash at that time? No, they didn’t … I’m concerned about the 150,000 plus people that are living homeless in their cars, in tents, couch surfing, and all the rest of it.

Victorian Labor struggling to turn election tide under new premier, new polling shows

Support for Victoria’s Labor government securing a fourth term remains stuck at one in four voters, according to the latest Resolve Political Monitor conducted last week and published by Nine Newspapers on Tuesday.

With Victorians headed to the polls in November, Labor and the Coalition have been duking it out in recent weeks over potholes, road and train upgrades, a secret public transport levy, crime and free dental clinics.

Labor’s primary vote was steady at 25%, the Coalition’s pushed up one point to 27% and One Nation remained at 23%.

With state net debt forecast to hit almost $200bn by 2030, Victorians (72%) more commonly expressed worry about debt levels than residents in other states such as NSW (67%) and Queensland (63%).

Almost half of respondents preferred to pause Suburban Rail Loop East rather than keep tunnelling, while 35% backed Labor’s $230m free dental care plan compared with 43% who reckoned it was not worth the cost and should not proceed.

The poll showed primary support for the Greens was 12%, while independents scooped up 8%.

The Victorian Coalition needs to hold all of its current lower house seats and win at least 16 more to govern alone.

Anthony Albanese is headed to the US this week, and has told Labor MPs he will launch Australia’s bid for a seat on the United Nations security council in New York.

Other honorees in the awards include Finland’s president, Alexander Stubb, the chief executive of Formula One, Stefano Domenicali, and Cynthia Erivo, the Wicked star and Academy Award-nominated actor.

Independent MP and former Winter Olympian Zali Steggall has joined the chorus of criticism against an ad for a US sports wagering company featuring actor Sydney Sweeney.

Steggall tells News24 she thought the ad was pretty disgusting, and rejected assertions from host, Laura Jayes, that it’s “ironic” that Sweeney, a woman, is copping all the flak for it.

I thought it was pretty disgusting. I mean, clearly Sydney Sweeney hasn’t done a real day of hard work in sport in her life, I would argue. And at the end of the day, she’s pushing that old trope to lure men into gambling … it brings women’s sport back to the sexualisation and the objectification, rather than celebrating women who are strong, fierce, brave, powerful, and do amazing things in the sporting arena.

Keep in mind, my understanding is she’s a shareholder and has a proprietary interest in the gambling advertising company that she’s promoting. So not only is she putting her body cheaply on the line, but she is also ultimately luring men into losing vast amounts of money through gambling advertising.

Steggall says women have been battling for decades to get more equality in media attention, respect, and pay conditions in sport, so “every time women’s sport is objectivised and trivialised in this way, it is really frustrating”.

Queensland to ban ‘green groups’ from objecting to mining projects

“Activists” and “green groups” will be banned from objecting to Queensland mining projects, under new legislation to be introduced into parliament today.

Queensland’s minister for natural resources, Dale Last, announced the move this morning.

He said it will amend the objection process so that “activists, often based thousands of kilometres away in green groups, many funded by foreign overseas entities,” will no longer be eligible to have a say under the act. Last told Queensland parliament:

We made a commitment that we would streamline approvals, restore confidence, and provide certainty – certainty for proponents, communities, local landholders, businesses, and workers. It’s about getting to a yes or a no faster, and we’re doing just that.

The bill will also put a stop to vexatious claims and remove regulatory duplication between mining and environmental legislation, he said.

National framework for defining affordable housing supported by advocates

Advocacy group National Shelter has welcomed a move to establish a nationally consistent definition of “affordable housing”, but says a definition won’t solve the housing crisis.

On Monday, the member for Bradfield, Nicolette Boele, introduced a bill that would tie the definition of affordable housing to what households actually earn, and attach it to commonwealth housing programs and funding agreements.

CEO of National Shelter, Jackson Hills, said the organisation supported the bill:

For too long, affordable housing has been defined differently across programs, jurisdictions, and policy settings. Establishing a consistent framework would strengthen policy development, improve transparency and support more effective measurement of housing outcomes over time.

While a nationally recognised definition is an important foundation, definitions alone will not solve the housing crisis. Affordable rental housing delivered at scale can reduce pressure on overstretched social housing waiting lists by providing viable housing options for low- and moderate-income households.

Over the next two decades, Australia must move beyond definitions and accelerate the policies, programs and investments needed to grow Affordable Rental Housing at scale across the country.

Queensland’s state government has flagged a new crackdown in the state’s parole system.

The legislation is set to be introduced into state parliament today by the minister for corrective services, Laura Gerber.

Gerber said it will expand the type of people who can be covered by the state’s restricted prisoner laws, which permit a person serving a life sentence to be banned from release for a decade.

At the moment the parole board can only issue a ban to a person who murders more than one person, or a child. Gerber told parliament they will now apply to all people sentenced to life, an unlimited number of times.

Under our laws, the rights of victims and community safety will be the paramount consideration for all parole decisions, as it always should have been.

The parole board must be empowered to deliver decisions in line with community expectations that put victims and the safety of Queenslanders first, and that is exactly what these laws will deliver.

The legislation comes on new legislation making it more difficult for some people accused of crimes to get bail.

Australia rises as US plunges in world democracy report

The rise of independents and teals and an underage social media ban have helped boost Australia’s ranking in one of the world’s leading democracy indexes, AAP reports.

At the same time, the United States’ standing has plummeted, with the Trump-era ransacking of institutions and standards dropping one of democracy’s leading lights to the ranks of also-rans.

The annual Global State of Democracy report, released on Tuesday, rates Australia very well across its assessment matrix.

Sweden-based organisation International IDEA charts nations across four main categories – representation, rights, rule of law, and participation – to assess their democratic health.

Overall, it’s ugly reading – autocracies and repressive forces appear to be winning. For the 11th straight year, the report finds more countries recorded declines rather than improvements.

The most extensive global decline was in freedom of expression, with drops in a quarter of the 173 surveyed countries.

Judicial independence is at a 36-year-low globally, while press freedom and access to justice are at their lowest levels this century.

People over 40 the largest single age group in share house accommodation

Share housing has stopped being a stage people pass through, with people in their early 20s now in the minority, according to research conducted by Homerun, an app that manages share house expenditure.

The study of 3,562 Australians living in or hosting a share house revealed that the largest single age group in Australian share accommodation is people aged 40 and over, who make up 44% of respondents.

17% already own a home and let rooms in it, rising to 26% of the over 40s.

35% have shared for five years or more, and 18% for more than 10.

69% did not expect, at 20, to still be share housing at their current age.

31% never expect to own a home. A further 11% put ownership more than a decade away.

75% say the high cost of housing has extended how long they will keep sharing.

Scott Mckeon, founder of Homerun said:

Sharehouses are underrepresented and underserviced. Co-living with people you are not related to comes with its own problems, and very little is built for them.

Running a household is work, and it is harder between independent adults. Splitting the finances, agreeing on what clean means, keeping the shared things stocked, and finding someone new when a housemate moves out.

Drug dogs on Oxford Street causing risks for LGBTQ+, advocates say

Redfern Legal Centre has condemned repeated raids by NSW police on Oxford Street venues as alleged harassment of the LGBTQ+ community that fails to disrupt drug supply while causing real social harm and health risks.

Over the weekend, police with drug-detection dogs were seen at several venues with people asked to remove clothing and questions about why people were in bathrooms.

RLC’s police accountability practice lead, Rian Terrell, said:

NSW police’s repeated decisions by NSW Police to deploy drug dogs to target patrons of venues on Oxford Street amount to [alleged] targeted harassment of the LGBTQIA+ community.

This is particularly concerning given the police watchdog, the Law Enforcement Conduct Commission, directed NSW Police to reopen a misconduct investigation into its actions targeting Sydney LGBTQIA+ venues just last month.

That NSW Police chose to raid Oxford Street again, when that investigation has not concluded, raises further concerns about NSW Police lack of accountability.

Use of drug dogs, wide scale strip searches and searches do little to disrupt drug supply and deter drug use.

The 2024 NSW Drug Summit made several recommendations, including related to the use of drug dogs, many of which have not been accepted or implemented by the NSW Government.
