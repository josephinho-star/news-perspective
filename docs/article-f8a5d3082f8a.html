‘Vampire’ so feared she was locked in booby-trapped grave was teen girl who met horror movie fate: experts

2026-09-06 20:35

The “real-life vampire” who was so feared that she was buried in a padlocked, booby-trapped grave was actually a teenage girl whose death remains shrouded in mystery, experts said.

The girl, nicknamed “Zosia” by researchers, was unearthed in 2022 in an eerie, 400-year-old unmarked burial site in Poland where 16th century villagers put the remains of dozens of people they believed were vampires or demons.

Zosia was buried with a sickle set up across her neck in a Rube Goldberg setup that experts believe was intended to behead her if she became reanimated and tried to emerge from the grave.

Now researchers say Zosia was actually a girl between the ages of 17 and 19 who did not die by violence or disease — but was likely blamed for a local famine or virus outbreak, according to a new study published in the Journal of Archaeological Science.

The girl was initially thought to have been sacrificed over that fear, but the new research determined there is no evidence to back up how she died before she met her horror movie fate.

Researchers had previously determined she was initially buried with a padlock fastened to her toe, which researchers said was a common practice at the time due to irrational fears from villagers that the dead could rise from the grave and harm the living nearby.

The iron sickle came later — likely added after villagers dug up the body, potentially after a disease or famine likely swept through the area, researchers said.

Research into her bones and teeth also found an abnormality in Zosia’s breast, a physical deformity that caused her great pain and may have “marked” her in a negative way and sparked fears that she had supernatural powers.

But despite the fear she may have instilled into the hearts of her neighbors, the girl was buried in a coffin wearing a silk cap trimmed with real silver and gold threads — a sign researchers said means she likely came from a wealthy or noble family and was not an ordinary villager or peasant.

Her DNA also showed a distant connection to Scandinavia, though evidence suggests she actually grew up in the area, with researchers dating her death to the mid-1600s.

Though the modern concept of vampires came later, Eastern Europeans initially became fearful of the monsters in the 11th century, believing that “some people who died would claw their way out of the grave as blood-sucking monsters that terrorized the living,” according to Smithsonian magazine.

By the 17th century, “unusual burial practices became common across Poland in response to a reported outbreak of vampires,” Science Alert reported.
