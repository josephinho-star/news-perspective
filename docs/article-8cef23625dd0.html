Tuesday briefing: Europe thinks Russia is preparing to test Nato’s limits

2026-09-22 05:40

Good morning. As the UN general assembly kicks off in New York later today, we anticipate a heavy focus on the ongoing conflicts in Iran, Ukraine and the Gulf – as well as the inevitable body language analysis of Andy Burnham’s first in-person meeting with Donald Trump.

But another related conflict is likely to dominate informal discussions: the intensifying acts of sabotage on European states that are believed to trace back to Russia but deliberately fall below the threshold for military response – a so-called hybrid or “shadow war” with the singular purpose of disrupting support for Ukraine.

As Vladimir Putin consolidates his grip on power in tightly controlled Russian elections and the Kremlin welcomed further success for the far-right AfD in German state polls yesterday, European intelligence officers have shared with the Guardian their starkest warnings yet about potential Russian attempts to test Nato resolve with an attack on European soil.

For today’s newsletter, I spoke to Shaun Walker, our special correspondent for eastern Europe, Ukraine and intelligence matters, about whether Europe is already at war. First, this morning’s headlines.

Yemen | The UK has agreed to provide defensive air support to Saudi Arabia to help counter Houthi fighters and restore stability in the Middle East while driving down the cost of living at home, Andy Burnham has said, ahead of his first meeting with Donald Trump.

Environment | The “jaw-dropping” El Niño currently heating up the planet has burst through the highest temperature ever recorded for the phenomenon, several months before its expected peak.

Gaza | Israeli and UK military commanders could be requested to give evidence to an inquest concerning three British aid workers killed in Gaza when aid vehicles they were travelling in were hit by an airstrike.

US | After President Trump’s snap decision to ban CNN from the White House, the four other networks that comprise the White House’s TV pool decided they would cease their pooled coverage of the president.

Slavery | Bones of the enslaver Edward Colston are likely to remain interred in a church in Bristol if and when the currently unused building is given a new lease of life as a community resource, it has emerged.

In depth: The line between peace and war

In a series of interviews with Shaun Walker in Prague and Riga, European intelligence bosses from the Czech Republic, Latvia and Sweden raised the possibility of an imminent Russian test of Nato, with one suggesting a potential attack could come in a matter of “months, not years”.

These sources were speaking days after Polish prime minister Donald Tusk told his parliament that intelligence assessments suggested Russia might launch an attack with drones or missiles on Nato territory in the coming months. French president Emmanuel Macron has also summoned party leaders for an emergency meeting to discuss similar warnings.

When I catch up with Shaun in a taxi on the way to Warsaw airport, he’s still assessing the gravity of these warnings. “One thing you always have to bear in mind when reporting on intelligence is that – because the way spy agencies gain information usually remains secret – you often don’t know if a source is talking about a very specific intercept of a plan that’s almost certainly going to happen, or if it’s less concrete chatter that analysts have put together.”

His view is that these concerns are not at the level of early 2022, when American intelligence repeatedly warned Russia was preparing to invade Ukraine and had hard evidence of the Kremlin’s plans (as Shaun revealed here).

What did surprise him was his sources’ willingness to be quoted: “Normally I go into these interviews assuming that we’re going to talk off the record. All of these people were happy to be on the record, which does suggest they want to get a message out.”

(Quick note, readers: “off the record” is a phrase that reporters in books, films and even real life throw about with abandon, but what does it mean? Say your source tells you about seeing a piece of fruit in a bowl. On the record means you can report that Jill Bloggs told the Guardian exclusively: “There’s a banana in the fruit bowl.” Off the record allows you to use that information to aid your investigation, but you mustn’t reveal where it came from. You can approach other fruit bowl-watchers, to check if they agree with Jill’s sighting. Then you might write: “Several sources with knowledge of the fruit bowl confirmed that a banana was present.” It’s a journalistic convention with no legal consequences, of course.)

What is this hybrid war and how can Europe fight it?

This systematic campaign has developed since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine four and a half years ago, with common features: designed to seed chaos and anxiety among Ukraine’s allies, while it is often difficult to prove conclusively Russian involvement as Kremlin security services pivot to recruiting local foot soldiers online.

Since the beginning of this year, there has been a catalogue of unexplained fires at defence facilities supplying Ukraine in Italy, Bulgaria, Estonia and Slovakia. An explosives-laden drone was discovered next to a Ukrainian cargo plane at Germany’s Leipzig airport. Authorities in Denmark warned Russia is recruiting Danish citizens to sabotage defence firms with ties to Ukraine; in Lithuania, an Ikea was set on fire; and in France, five coffins inscribed with the words “French soldiers in Ukraine” were left under the Eiffel Tower. In the UK, the Rycroft review found online disinformation from hostile foreign states including Russia was warping political discourse when it published in March, while two Russian-linked arsonists who targeted property connected to former prime minister Sir Keir Starmer were jailed in June.

Given the recent escalation, it’s not surprising that Shaun observes some EU politicians “getting irritated” with the term ‘hybrid war’, and arguing that such direct attacks amount to more than “grey zone” activity.

Although European governments have become much quicker publicly to call out Russian involvement, the challenge for these politicians is deciding what effective measures they can take. After the Leipzig incident, Germany expelled more than 150 Russian diplomats and promised tighter border controls for Russians entering Germany. But the Schengen free movement zone makes this tricky: one of the suspects in the Leipzig incident reportedly entered Europe using an Italian tourist visa.

EU sanctions blacklist Russian banks and energy companies, as well as entities in the United Arab Emirates, Thailand and China for helping Moscow evade western restrictions. And there’s a growing willingness among EU nations to clamp down on Russia’s shadow fleet – ships illegally carrying Russian oil and other supplies in the Baltic and North seas.

While there are vocal critics who argue these diplomatic and economic measures don’t go far enough, others worry that even more decisive responses would riskdragging the bloc into military escalation.

“What Russia is working to is that they know even the most hawkish people in Nato countries would really like to avoid having a direct conflict.”

What is the mood in Europe?

For decades, politicians and analysts in those Baltic states most at risk of Russian aggression have warned western Europe to take that threat more seriously. But that mood has flipped of late, with the secretary general of Estonia’s foreign ministry, Jonatan Vseviov, for example, cautioning against “panic” on social media.

These states remain the strongest voices in terms of tangible responses to Russian aggression, but are well aware that the vast majority of Russia’s military capability is tied up in Ukraine. They are also concerned about the impact that a narrative of tanks rolling into Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania has on their societies and economies – which is of course partly the point of concerted Russian psyops.

“Somebody put it to me recently – which I thought was the perfect description – that Baltic states prefer western Europe to see it as a chronic rather than an acute problem”.

With that in mind, European leaders at the UN in New York this week will be keen to create a united front and wary of interventions from Donald Trump, who has previously dismissed concerns that Russia will strike a Baltic country or other member state to test Nato resolve and repeatedly criticised the alliance.

Is Europe already at war (and what does that mean)?

When Shaun was researching his latest book, The Illegals, about Russia’s deep-cover spies, he waded through archives of KGB documents from the 70s and 80s. They often referred to “the special period”, a Soviet term that described a stage between friendly coexistence and war between communist and capitalist worlds, and was characterised by plans for relentless sabotage.

“It sounded a lot like where we are now”, he says. Like Europe, Russia doesn’t want a direct confrontation with the West but it’s clear, adds Shaun, “that their risk appetite is increasing.”

The scale of sabotage has led to prominent military voices in the UK and across Europe suggesting that the bloc is already at war – although the very definition of ‘at war’ is now liminal – and that governments ought to be more transparent with their public. Does he agree?

Shaun’s one word answer is “no”. The nuance is as follows: “There is a clear campaign from Russia, using methods that are not military, but are pretty aggressive. Some people may consider them as being something close to a war. It’s clearly absolutely nothing like the war in Ukraine.”

He suggests that European governments find themselves in a bind domestically. “If they choose to spend on defence rather than social issues, those social issues get worse and voters move to the populist right, the AfD in Germany or Le Pen in France, then you end up with leaders who are very sympathetic to Russia in power.”

“There’s a careful path between dismissing this as a fake threat and the kind of alarmist rhetoric that we’re on the path to war and every other issue is secondary.”

A bit of good news to remind you that the world’s not all bad

Despite 18 million people in the UK having hearing issues, there is still no dedicated centre for the Deaf community.

Now, Amanprit Arnold, an urban planner and activist, is campaigning to create one. The DeafCity Hub would be a national centre for Arnold’s community, as well as a co-working and cultural space, and a home for Deaf-sector charities. It would be designed using deaf-friendly architectural guidelines called DeafSpace principles, which include wide corridors and staircases so people can walk next to each other while talking in BSL.

This month, the project has landed a year-long “meanwhile” space (a building awaiting redevelopment), which Arnold hopes “will act as an incubator to develop this idea further.”
