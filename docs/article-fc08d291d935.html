Palisades family celebrates Thanksgiving in unfinished home in sign of fire-ravaged town’s comeback

2026-11-27 01:30

The Pacific Palisades’ comeback was on full display this Thanksgiving as one family gathered for a holiday feast inside the bare wooden frame of their fire-ravaged home, refusing to let agonizing rebuilding delays derail a 32-year tradition.

No windows, no finished walls and unfinished plumbing — but the Pascoes weren’t spending another Thanksgiving away from home.

Fed up with waiting, the family transformed the bare wooden frame of their partially rebuilt Pacific Palisades house into a makeshift dining room, refusing to let a painfully slow recovery rob them of a 32-year holiday tradition.

“The big thing is, it is our home,” Sue Pascoe told the California Post. “This is our home even though it’s not there yet.”

The family had celebrated Thanksgiving at the same address since 1994, long before the Jan. 7, 2025, wildfire tore through the coastal community, killing 12 people, scorching more than 23,000 acres and destroying 6,845 structures.

“Every Thanksgiving, we would go eat on the patio of this house, which was lovely, and we would send photos of us eating outside to everyone in the Midwest who was in the snow,” Pascoe said.

Those photographs are gone. But the traditions behind them live on.

The Pascoes’ Thanksgiving table has always had room for more, welcoming friends, out-of-town visitors and anyone who had nowhere else to spend the holiday.

“The holidays are hard for the thousands who are still displaced, but it’s incredible to see so many families returning to the Palisades, in new homes, making new memories and carrying on old traditions,” said Councilwoman Traci Park.

This year was no different. Even in the middle of a construction site, the family welcomed a friend and invited The Post’s photographer to stay for dinner.

“Every year there would be someone new that might have been out of town who was there or someone who didn’t have someplace to go,” Pascoe said. “It just was a grounding place is what it was on Thanksgiving.”

With her adult children coming home for the holiday and little room to entertain in the family’s temporary apartment, Pascoe realized her unfinished house had one thing going for it: plenty of space.

Her husband, John, embraced the idea, arranging for rental tables and chairs to accommodate the Thanksgiving gathering.

On the menu: turkey, dressing, Brussels sprouts, pecan pie and, of course, ugly cake.

Even the family’s oddly named dessert has a survival story of its own.

“It looks ugly, but when people eat it, they love it,” Pascoe said.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Pascoe began experimenting with dessert recipes from different states, stumbling across “ugly cake” in a Lutheran church cookbook.

Packed with apples, raisins and butter, the cake became a family favorite despite its less-than-appetizing name.

The wildfire wiped out Pascoe’s entire cookbook collection, but she knew the recipe by heart and has since printed copies for others to enjoy.

For Pascoe, a longtime journalist who runs the local news publication Circling the News, much of the past 22 months has been spent chronicling the very disaster that upended her own life.

Despite losing her home, she continued reporting on disaster assistance, insurance battles and the agonizingly slow recovery of a community desperate to rebuild.

Behind the headlines, she was fighting her own battle to get home.

Pascoe secured rebuilding permits in July 2025, just six months after the fire. But getting permission to rebuild proved far easier than getting the money to do it.

“At the beginning you have nothing. You really have nothing,” Pascoe said.

The family spent months battling State Farm over insurance payments, forced to compile detailed lists of belongings lost in the fire and provide receipts, photographs and replacement costs.

“They started stalling and stalling, and then they told us we didn’t have the possessions that we had,” Pascoe told The Post.

“We went through seven adjusters,” Pascoe said.

Frustrated by the delays, Pascoe turned to state Sen. Ben Allen and state insurance regulators for help.

The family finally received its full insurance payout in April 2026, nine months after securing rebuilding permits and 15 months after their home burned down.

But no insurance check could replace everything.

“You lose your wedding dress. You lose your kids’ baby books. You lose the cedar chest your dad made you,” she said.

“The most devastating thing is the memories you lose.”

The Pascoes’ insurance battle unfolded as complaints over State Farm’s handling of wildfire claims drew scrutiny from state regulators.

In May, the California Department of Insurance launched legal action against State Farm after identifying 398 alleged violations across 114 of the 220 wildfire claims investigators examined.

State Farm has denied systematically mishandling claims, reporting in May that it had paid more than $5.7 billion on approximately 13,700 fire-related claims.

Meanwhile, the gap between rebuilding permits and completed homes underscores just how far the recovery still has to go.

Los Angeles has issued more than 2,100 residential rebuilding permits but just 61 certificates of occupancy, according to the state’s wildfire rebuilding dashboard.

For the Pascoes, however, Thanksgiving offered a chance to celebrate how far they had come rather than dwell on how much work remained.

Pascoe’s daughter Shelby, who helped clear debris and dig up plants in the aftermath of the fire, found comfort in seeing familiar pieces of her childhood home take shape.

“It’s cool to see how much progress has gotten done,” Shelby said, pointing to familiar features, including the kitchen nook and the three children’s bedrooms.

“There’s different elements that have that home feel, which is really nice.”

When the family went around the Thanksgiving table sharing what they were grateful for, her brother Sawyer offered a particularly fitting answer.

“I’m thankful we’re together and have a roof over our head. A construction roof, but we had a roof over our head,” Sawyer said.

For Pascoe, much of her gratitude was reserved for the man who made the homecoming possible.

“I am very thankful for my husband, who went through all the effort to get the rentals and everything set up so we could celebrate here,” she said.
