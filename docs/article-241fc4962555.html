How America can win the AI race without burning voters

2026-11-16 09:50

One of the biggest losers on Election Day was the AI industry. Across the country, voters delivered a clear warning about the rapid expansion of data centers powering artificial intelligence. Americans want technological progress, but not at the expense of their communities, electricity bills, water supplies or quality of life.

According to Ballotpedia, 46 of the 47 data-center-related ballot measures decided in 2026 favored greater limits on development. The message is unmistakable: The industry is losing public confidence, and political leaders who ignore these concerns do so at their own peril.

America needs more data centers, not fewer, to maintain its AI leadership. But technology companies cannot expect communities to welcome projects when residents believe they are absorbing the costs while corporations reap the benefits.

We believe there is a better way forward.

In our new report, Beyond the "Wild West" and Moratorium: A Bipartisan Framework for Smart and Responsible Data Center Investment, we outline a practical approach to preserving America's AI leadership while treating host communities fairly. Our perspectives are bipartisan: Robert Wolf served as an economic adviser to President Barack Obama, while Stephen Moore served as a senior economic adviser to President Donald Trump.

AI is becoming one of this century's defining technologies. Data centers support cloud computing, advanced manufacturing, scientific research, national security and future AI applications. A multitrillion-dollar investment wave also creates opportunities for American workers, skilled tradespeople and domestic manufacturers.

The industry has an opportunity to change course. Developers should treat residents as partners, replace vague promises with measurable commitments and pay the incremental costs their projects create.

But economic benefits must be measured honestly. Construction jobs can be substantial, while permanent employment at operating facilities is often considerably smaller. Communities deserve transparency about lasting jobs, tax revenues and economic benefits.

Residents also have legitimate concerns about electricity demand, utility bills, water consumption, noise, traffic, pollution and lost open space. These concerns must be addressed, not dismissed.

Two extremes that won't work

The debate has become divided between two approaches.

The first is the "Wild West": allowing development with minimal safeguards or community input. While speed matters in the global AI race, dismissing local concerns is creating the political resistance that threatens future investment.

The second is the moratorium approach: pausing or halting construction until stronger regulations are established. Although the concerns are understandable, broad freezes risk delaying investment and innovation without addressing underlying problems.

The backlash is growing. New York has pursued pauses on certain data center approvals, California has enacted oversight laws, and Texas has increased scrutiny of the industry's demands on its electric grid.

Public opinion reflects these concerns. Pew Research Center found that the share of Americans who believe data centers are mostly bad for the environment increased from 39 percent in January to 54 percent in August 2026. The share believing they are mostly bad for home energy costs rose from 38 percent to 50 percent.

A September Fabrizio, Lee & Associates survey found that nearly 65 percent opposed new data center construction, while 61 percent supported construction with conditions. Americans are not necessarily rejecting development. They are demanding reasonable protections.

A bipartisan path forward

The choice between unchecked development and blanket moratoriums is false. America needs predictable rules that protect consumers and communities while allowing responsible investment.

Our report proposes five principles:

1. Protect electricity customers.

Data center developers should pay the full incremental costs of the generation, transmission and distribution infrastructure their projects require. Residential customers and small businesses should not subsidize investments primarily serving technology companies.

The bipartisan Ratepayer Protection Act, which passed the House 417–3, demonstrates support for this principle: Those driving new electricity demand should pay their fair share.

2. Protect grid reliability.

Developers should disclose projected electricity demand and demonstrate how projects will affect grid reliability. Plans should address new generation, dedicated energy resources and storage without shifting costs or risks onto existing customers.

3. Safeguard water and the environment.

Communities should have access to assessments of water availability, cooling requirements, emissions, noise and environmental impacts before projects are approved. Monitoring and mitigation should protect local resources and residents.

4. Require transparency and community engagement.

Residents deserve early notice and meaningful opportunities to participate before major decisions are made. Developers should disclose projected jobs, tax revenues, public incentives, electricity demand and water consumption.

Public incentives should be tied to measurable commitments, with reviews to ensure companies deliver on their promises.

5. Preserve local flexibility and accountability.

The federal government should establish baseline protections while allowing states and localities to adapt requirements to local circumstances. Communities should retain meaningful control over siting and land use, while predictable permitting standards provide developers with investment certainty.

The goal is not to dictate where every data center belongs. It is to establish clear rules for responsible development.

The opportunity in the backlash

There is a difference between opposing artificial intelligence and opposing how its infrastructure is being developed. Election results should not be interpreted as a rejection of innovation, but as a demand that technological progress benefit ordinary Americans.

The industry has an opportunity to change course. Developers should treat residents as partners, replace vague promises with measurable commitments and pay the incremental costs their projects create.

This is not an argument for less innovation. It is an argument for sustainable innovation.

America cannot afford to fall behind in artificial intelligence. But the industry cannot afford to lose the public support needed to build the infrastructure AI requires.

A responsible, bipartisan framework can protect ratepayers, respect communities, provide certainty for developers and keep America competitive.

Election Day delivered a warning. The question now is whether the AI industry listens—or continues down a path of growing delays, moratoriums and political resistance.

The future of American AI leadership may depend on getting this right.

Stephen Moore is a former senior economic adviser to President Donald Trump and chairman of Unleash Prosperity.
