Turkey advances permit rules for Booking.com, Expedia and Airbnb

2026-10-07 14:22

A Turkish parliamentary committee on Tuesday approved a bill that would require foreign booking platforms such as Booking.com, Expedia and Airbnb to obtain permits and appoint representatives in Turkey, while capping commissions on domestic bookings at 17 percent, Euronews reported.

The proposal still needs approval from the full parliament and would take effect January 1, 2027, if enacted.

President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan’s ruling Justice and Development Party (AKP) submitted the bill in August, following complaints from Turkey’s travel agencies about competition from platforms based abroad.

The legislation aims to protect consumers, support businesses operating under Turkey’s licensing rules and prevent unfair competition, Deputy Culture and Tourism Minister Nadir Alpaslan told the committee.

Platforms would need a two-year permit from the Ministry of Culture and Tourism, with the fee set at 5 million lira, about $101,000, and adjusted annually.

They would have to register for Turkey’s digital services tax, have no outstanding tax debt and provide an address in Turkey for official notices.

A representative in Turkey would be responsible for responding to court orders, government requests and consumer complaints.

For bookings made from Turkey for services in Turkey, platforms’ commissions and other charges would be limited to 17 percent of the price before taxes.

Charging above the cap could trigger a fine of 10 times the amount collected unlawfully, with repeated violations leading to permit cancellation.

Platforms could not prevent hotels from offering different prices through other channels or punish them in search rankings for filing complaints with public bodies or courts.

The companies would also pay a levy of 0.075 percent of the revenue used to calculate their digital services tax to the Turkey Tourism Promotion and Development Agency, which finances tourism promotion.

Licensed platforms could sell bookings for ministry-certified accommodation and homes licensed for tourism rentals, along with airline tickets.

Other services reserved for travel agencies would have to be sold through licensed agencies, while car rentals would require authorized rental companies.

Accommodation listings would have to display permit numbers after the platforms verify them under ministry rules.

Turkish law would govern disputes arising from activities covered by the permits, and Turkish courts would have jurisdiction.

The bill would also allow permit cancellation for activities deemed contrary to Turkey’s interests, damaging to public confidence or harmful to tourism, according to parliament’s account of the legislation.

The ministry could order access to platforms blocked if they operated without a permit or their permit expired or was canceled.

Blocking orders would have to be submitted to a judge for approval within 24 hours and would lapse if the judge did not issue a decision within 48 hours.

Platforms operating in Turkey when the law takes effect would have three months to apply for permits, with access blocks for those that fail to apply or whose applications are rejected.

The proposal follows a dispute between Turkey’s travel agencies and foreign booking companies that predates the current bill.

An İstanbul court ordered restrictions on Booking.com in 2017 after the Association of Turkish Travel Agencies (TÜRSAB), an industry body representing travel agencies, accused the company of unfair competition.

Booking.com stopped offering accommodation in Turkey to users booking from Turkey, while continuing to serve people booking Turkish accommodation from abroad and users in Turkey booking trips abroad.

Turkey also enacted a law in 2023 requiring permits for homes rented to tourists for 100 days or less, creating a licensing system for properties advertised through platforms such as Airbnb.
