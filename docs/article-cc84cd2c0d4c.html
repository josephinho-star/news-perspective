Integrity body warns government algorithms risk repeating robodebt mistakes

The independent research institute the Centre for Public Integrity has warned that federal government agencies are deploying opaque automated decision-making tools as part of an opaque system, creating major accountability gaps potentially depriving people of fundamental review rights.

In a submission to the government inquiry on Artificial Intelligence, the Centre wrote that it is concerned the increasing use of algorithms is creating “significant accountability gaps” requiring new legal frameworks. The submission said:

At the most basic level, individuals may not know whether automated decision-making has been used at all in a decision affecting them.

Even where its use is known, there may be insufficient information about the particular system involved and the material upon which it operated, including relevant algorithms, code, datasets and, in the case of generative AI, prompts and other instructions.

This opacity stops meaningful scrutiny of whether government systems produce discriminatory, biased or otherwise unfair outcomes, the submission says, and this may “substantially impair an individual’s capacity to understand and challenge a decision”.

It comes as Guardian Australia has reported the government’s implementation of an algorithm to assess the entitlement of older Australians to government-funded aged care support at home could not be overridden when it made wrong decisions. It has left older Australians without the care and support they need, leading to worsening health and earlier entry into aged care.

Centre director Dr Catherine Williams said:

We have been here before.

Robodebt showed what happens when automated systems are allowed to make decisions affecting people’s lives without adequate transparency, accountability or review rights. To prevent piecemeal reform like we have seen with the Aged Care Bill, a single, principled framework must be implemented.

The submission calls for an audit and registry of all automated tools across commonwealth agencies.

Shadow health and aged care minister Anne Ruston said Freedom of Information (FoI) documents reported by Guardian Australia show the government “shut out its own staff and frontline assessors from a decision that directly affects older Australians”.

You cannot tell Australians a qualified human is in control, while designing a system where an assessor cannot override an algorithm-generated result.

The federal government stripped frontline health workers of the power to override an algorithm used by aged care assessors to determine how much government-funded home support older Australians are entitled to, forcing them to accept the algorithm’s outcome even when it under-assesses peoples needs.

FoI documents show the department’s own staff warned removing the ability of assessors to override wrong outcomes was contrary to assurances they had provided to assessors and in public statements, and would likely break the trust of the sector.

Technology can support an assessment but must never replace professional judgement or stop an assessor responding to an older Australian’s individual circumstances.

Government to face Senate scrutiny over compulsory BasicsCard scheme after broken election promise

Last week, the government broke an election promise to “scrap the discriminatory Cashless Debit Card and make the BasicsCard voluntary” by repealing a clause that would have ended the compulsory nature of the scheme in July this year.

Tomorrow, the Senate Community Affairs References Committee will be hearing from groups about the government’s changes to the policy, which blocks purchases like alcohol and tobacco until 2033,

Data from the Department of Social Services shows there are 33,061 people on income management programs, known as the BasicsCard and SmartCard. More than 82% of participants identify as First Nations, with the vast majority on the scheme living in the Northern Territory.

Ahead of the inquiry tomorrow, Accountable Income Management Network Simon Schrapel said:

The Government’s proposed changes aim to shut down future Parliamentary review of a regime that has proven to be ineffective, discriminatory, costly and harmful for many participants.

And yet here we are with a government having no road map to end compulsory income management. Instead they have pushed through legislative changes to remove self-repeal dates and the periodic review of income management by the Parliament, backtracking on their promise to end these policies.

Income management disproportionately targets Aboriginal people and communities. It undermines basic human rights, creates stigma, is fundamentally racist and has consistently shown to deliver minimal if any benefits for participants forced onto the scheme. The Accountable Income Management Network continues to call on the government to end compulsory income management.

If the Government was serious about implementing its policy commitment to ending mandated income management then this latest proposal to remove legislative self-repeal dates is a funny way to go about it. It will only serve to make the Government less accountable for a policy that has failed at every hurdle.

Queensland passes laws to establish outback boot camps for youth criminals

Queensland’s government has passed legislation allowing it to establish outback boot camps for young people convicted or accused of crimes.

The Youth Justice (Circuit Breaker) Amendment Bill 2026 passed into law this afternoon.

The Liberal-National party government made an election promise to establish two camps run by non-government organisations in rural or remote locations in the state for up to 60 children. Under the act, children can be sent to the remote prisons for up to six months either as a condition of bail or as a sentence for a conviction.

Experts compared the facilities to the boot camps created under the government of Campbell Newman. A KPMG report later found they weren’t an effective way to reduce recidivism and children should not be detained in remote locations.

The Youth Advocacy Centre warned there was a risk children could be mistreated in the camps, as the new centres will have less oversight than Queensland jails. Minister for youth justice Laura Gerber said the law would give courts “a powerful new tool to deal with repeat youth offenders by removing them from the community they have caused harm to and ordering them to Circuit Breaker Sentencing, an intensive rehabilitation program”.

Failed NSW anti-abortion bill an attempt to ‘chip away’ at women’s rights, critics say

A failed New South Wales bill has been labelled “a familiar strategy from the anti-abortion playbook”.

The Libertarian upper house member John Ruddick had introduced a bill that claimed to ban sex-selective abortions. Experts say such a practice is not occurring, has already been banned within the health regulations, and that the attempt to “ban” it is part of a broader strategy to recriminalise abortion.

The bill was defeated 21-16 on Wednesday night.

Family Planning Australia’s chief executive officer, Sue Shilbury, said the decision underlined that abortion is healthcare. She said:

It is reassuring that measures which could recriminalise abortion care and undermine safe, equitable and timely access were rejected.

Criminalising any aspect of abortion care is an attack on women’s health and the doctors and nurses caring for them.

This bill was a familiar strategy from the anti-abortion playbook attempting to chip away at women’s reproductive rights bit by bit.

One Nation’s NSW leader, Mike Newman, spoke at a rally for the bill organised by controversial anti-abortion campaigner Joanna Howe, where he claimed the party wanted to “stop” abortion – a claim he later said was a mistake.

Abbott says Australians don’t want frontier wars or ‘drug wars’ in War Memorial

Tony Abbott has said the Australian War Memorial should not be dedicated to the frontier wars between Indigenous people and settlers, just as it should leave out the “drug wars” in suburban Sydney.

The former prime minister said the massacres should not be considered a war between nations, nor even a civil war, addressing the “Imperialism and Resistance” conference at the memorial today. He said:

What about the fights between First Nations which were frequent and often deadly? How could events such as the Eureka Stockade or even the apprehension of the Kelly Gang be excluded? ...

It would be a big stretch to ask the Australian people to describe as war all acts of politically motivated violence, as it were, or of violence between significant groups of people. I mean the tabloids might say there’s a drug war in western Sydney, but I don’t know whether we would want to put that into the Australian War Memorial for instance, however serious.

The Australian War Memorial has no separate gallery on colonisation but is considering more work to reflect the conflicts after the first fleet’s 1788 arrival.

Abbott said there were “lots of terrible things that undoubtedly happened” and singled out a court case over the 1838 Myall Creek massacre as a “regrettably rare show of British justice”. He accused colonial Queensland’s native mounted police of “indiscriminate killing” which went “unrecorded as a matter of policy”. But he downplayed an academic’s massacre map and their estimate of 11,000 deaths from conflict as a “guesstimate”.

Researchers this week estimated there were roughly 2.2 million Indigenous people before 1788, with fewer than a tenth thereafter. Abbott declined to comment on the study but said it was “way, way, way larger” than previously recognised estimates. He said:

Obviously, population decline from let’s say 300,000 to 100,000 is a catastrophic decline.
