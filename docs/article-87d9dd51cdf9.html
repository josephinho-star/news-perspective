Supreme court allows Trump to resume deporting people to third countries for now while justices weigh policy

The supreme court has allowed the Trump administration to resume deporting people to countries other than their own without offering them the chance to show the harms they may face, bolstering a tool Donald Trump has used for rapid expulsions as part of his immigration crackdown.

The justices granted the DOJ’s request to halt Boston-based US district judge Brian Murphy’s ruling that the Department of Homeland Security’s policy to remove migrants to so-called third countries is unlawful, while the administration appeals the matter to the supreme court. They also agreed to hear arguments in the dispute in December, with a formal ruling on policy to follow.

Its three liberal justices dissented from the decision.

Federal attorneys said the lower-court decision forced the cancellation of a deportation flight carrying about 70 people bound for three countries.

The justices previously allowed the deportation flights, a core Trump administration policy, to temporarily continue when the case came before them last year. In another win for the administration, the supreme court subsequently lifted limits the judge had imposed to protect a group of men from being sent to South Sudan.

More than 25,000 people have been deported to 29 countries under the policy implemented last year, according to a tally by human rights groups, with the vast majority going to Mexico.

The administration has also carried out third-country deportations to politically unstable South Sudan, which the US state department urges Americans to avoid due to the risks of crime, kidnapping and armed conflict, as well as Uganda, Equatorial Guinea, Liberia, the Central African Republic, and other nations.

A judge has ended the longest-running federal oversight of a police department in history, concluding that monitoring of the Oakland Police Department may end 23 years after it began.

The monitoring began in 2003, after a rookie officer came forward three years earlier to report abuse of power by a group of officers called the Oakland “Riders.” The four officers were charged with using excessive force, falsifying police reports, assaulting people and other crimes in the predominantly Black neigborhood of west Oakland.

“This is the first time in 23 years that OPD has achieved each of the 51 tasks in the (settlement agreement.) No one can say, ‘mission accomplished.’ But I do congratulate the city and OPD for getting this far,” San Francisco judge William Orrick said during a hearing in May.

On Tuesday, Orrick said: “I’m particularly grateful to the men and women of the Oakland Police Department ... for achieving substantial compliance,” adding: “May it ever be thus.”

In an executive order today, Donald Trump called for “artificial intelligence” to be renamed “super intelligence”.

“The terminology used by the Federal Government should reflect the transformative capabilities of these technologies and the limitless opportunities they create for the American people,” the order reads. “Accordingly, the term ‘Super Intelligence’ more appropriately captures the promise, potential, and rapidly advancing capabilities of these technologies.”

The order directs “executive departments and agencies to use the term in “official correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents, and other non-statutory documents within the executive branch.”

The US Senate on Tuesday evening rejected along mostly party lines a Democratic resolution that would have forced the state department to report to Congress on violence in the occupied West Bank, including the killings of nine American citizens by Israeli settlers or security forces over the last few years.

The resolution, led by the Maryland Democrat Chris Van Hollen, would have given the state department 30 days to report to Congress on those deaths and on wider human rights conditions in the territory, including Israel’s detention of Palestinian children.

The resolution failed to advance by a vote of 47 to 51. Pennsylvania senator John Fetterman, a Democrat, joined Republicans in voting against the resolution, while Kentucky Republican Rand Paul voted in favor.

Donald Trump has shared a copy of the document AI executives signed during a White House meeting today on Truth Social.

In a social media post, Trump attached a copy of the one-page document, called the “White House Accord on Super Intelligence,” a term the president favors to artificial intelligence.

Signed by Google’s Sundar Pichai, Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, Meta’s Mark Zuckerberg, OpenAI’s Greg Brockman, xAI’s Elon Musk and Nvidia’s Jensen Huang, the agreement states “we believe every company is responsible for developing its own technology safely.”

The agreement asks AI companies to commit to four layers of internal controls and audits: 1) “implement robust internal controls”, 2) “empower an internal team” to audit those controls, 3) “partner with an independent external auditor” and 4) “designate an independent committee of the board of directors”.

It adds that “the participating companies will meet regularly to establish standards and best practices”.

The United States has withdrawn from a leading international anti-corruption watchdog.

The Council of Europe said today that the Trump administration has decided to leave the Group of States against Corruption, a European-led anti-corruption monitoring body established in 1999. The United States joined in 2000. Only Russia and Belarus have previously left the group.

The Associated Press reports that a US official confirmed the United States had submitted its withdrawal on Monday.

The news of the United States’ withdrawal comes a week after

“The United States has participated in GRECO since 2000 and has been a valuable contributor to its work and to the exchange of experience and good practice in preventing and combating corruption,” the Strasbourg-based Council of Europe said in a statement. “GRECO remains open to resuming cooperation with the United States in the future in support of shared efforts to prevent corruption, strengthen integrity and uphold the rule of law.”

The decision was announced a week after another European organization, the Organization for Security and Cooperation, reported that the United States has declined to allow its members to observe November’s midterm elections.
