Ted Cruz angrily questions Jack Smith, says Javert from Les Mis would look in 'astonishment'

Throughout Smith’s hearing, Republican senators have repeatedly brought up Timothy Thibault, a former FBI agent who was in charge of the Arctic Frost investigation and accused of political bias.

Republican senator Katie Britt asked Smith multiple times to confirm or deny whether Thibault had engaged in political bias, even though Thibault retired from the FBI months before Smith was made special counsel.

Thibault oversaw paperwork to open the election conspiracy case known as Arctic Frost, which has become an increasingly polarizing topic among lawmakers. Senator Chris Coons, a Democrat, asked Smith about his work with Republicans throughout his investigation into Donald Trump’s attempts to overturn the 2020 election.

The former special prosecutor said he found it “very hard” to understand why his probe was considered to be partisan when the “strongest witnesses” were Republicans like Rusty Bowers, a former Arizona state representative, and former vice-president Mike Pence.

“The criminal scheme we investigated was predicated upon Donald Trump preying on party loyalty. He preyed on party loyalty and fear,” Smith said. “The reality is, one of the reasons, our democracy held up. In a back and forth with Durbin, the former special counsel set out exactly what call toll records are – as I set out earlier.

“My office did secure toll records from third parties, third-party telephone companies, in the course of our investigation that were records for members of Congress,” Smith confirmed, and said they “were materially relevant to our investigation”.

Smith said that these records corroborated evidence that Donald Trump and his co-conspirators were seeking to “exploit the violence on January 6 in furtherance of their criminal scheme. They were taking the fact that law enforcement officers had been brutally assaulted at this institution, and using that to seek to delay the proceedings further in violation of the law.”

The former special counsel explained why these records proved useful, despite the fact they didn’t contain any content. For example it allowed investigators to determine how many phones former New York mayor Rudy Giuliani, a Trump campaign official at the time, had and used to make calls.

Smith noted, in response to questions from Chuck Grassley, that when he first became special counsel, he did not have a government phone or government computer.

“In the initial days when I was appointed and I was still living in the Netherlands, I did use a personal device and email to communicate with folks in the justice department,” Smith said. “What I did when I got government accounts is made sure, to the best of my ability, that information was forwarded to government accounts, so it would be within the Federal Records Act. I sought at all times to comply with the Federal Records Act, as I expected my attorneys to do as well.”

During his opening remarks, Smith stood by his investigations and his findings, and also noted that his work – and that of his colleagues – is “often weakened by attacks” from lawmakers and officials.

He noted the effort from the Trump administration to vilify career prosecutors and FBI agents “simply because of their unwavering commitment to the fair and impartial administration of justice without regard for any personal costs”.

“I myself have been threatened with jail by the President of the United States,” Smith added.

The former special counsel noted that he would be unable to discuss the contents of his second volume of his report – which focuses on Trump’s alleged mishandling of classified documents. This comes as a result of a federal judge’s order, that I mentioned earlier, which bars the release of the specific report.

Senator Dick Durbin, the top Democrat on the judiciary committee, said that his Republican colleagues have “spent months spreading baseless conspiracy theories” about Jack Smith’s investigations “instead of hearing from him directly”.

Durbin has long advocated for the former special counsel to defend his work, despite GOP lawmakers on the panel refusing to invite him to Capitol Hill.

“President Trump’s misconduct was egregious, differing in kind and scale from what we had seen by any president before him,” Durbin said. “Investigating misconduct by government officials requires scrupulous care. Thankfully, Mr Smith, who is here today and will be under oath, and his team of nonpartisan career prosecutors had the necessary experience to tackle this assignment.”

Durbin went on to accuse the president and many congressional Republicans of attempting to smear Smith and manufacture conspiracies about his team in order “to whitewash the history of the 2020 election, and the January 6 insidious attack on this Capitol building”.

“They decry Mr Smith for following the facts of the law and say nothing, nothing about a president who continued to break the law in an effort to prevent the truth from coming to light,” Durbin added.

Grassley accuses former special counsel Jack Smith of targeting 'entire Republican party' throughout investigations

Chuck Grassley – the Republican chair of the Senate judiciary committee – is delivering his opening remarks now.

The senator from Iowa alleges that while Smith has claimed his focus was on the January 6 Capitol riots, the former special counsel’s “own conduct contradicts that assertion”.

“When his team subpoenaed records, sometimes the date ranges extended weeks, months, or years before and after January 6,” Grassley said, adding that Smith subpoenaed and obtained information on Republican groups as early as 2019 and as late as 2023.

“You see, that’s what you must do if your case is designed to map out and target the entire Republican Party,” Grassley added.

It’s worth noting that when it comes to the investigation into Donald Trump’s attempts to overturn the 2020 election, Smith legally obtained phone toll records – which were obtained through a grand jury. These records show the date, time, and duration of calls, but not the actual content of the communications.

These are often secured by prosecutors in connection with investigations.
