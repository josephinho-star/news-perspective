Aging means learning how to prevent costly scams targeting older Americans

2026-11-18 10:00

Getting older comes with some unpleasant consequences — aches and pains, worsening vision and even some forgetfulness. But one of the downsides of aging that isn’t discussed enough is that it also puts you at a higher risk of being targeted by scammers and losing money to fraud and scams.

A recent report by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) to Congress stated that the cost of consumer fraud targeting older adults (60 and older) could have been as high as $119.5 billion last year. Plus, the risk of higher median losses also occurred with older adults, with a large disparity for those 80 years old and older.

When it comes to preying on older adults, scammers come from far and wide. The types of schemes and scams are varied, from romance schemes to tech support scams to fake sweepstakes and lotteries, investment scams and all kinds of impersonation scams, whether the scammer is impersonating a financial institution, government personnel or even a family member.

Experts say that there are very concrete reasons that scammers focus on the aging population. Some of those have to do with having lived for decades in a high-trust society, which takes people at their word and generates a culture of being polite and even obedient to authority.

Another reason is that, in the U.S., more wealth has accumulated with the older generations, so the scammers are just following the money.

Other factors include preying on loneliness, exploiting cognitive issues and even a lack of familiarity with technology.

So, given that scammers are trying to manipulate and scam those who are aging, how can you protect yourself or your aging family members from these schemes?

One of my late father’s favorite sayings was, "If it sounds too good to be true, it is." This is a great mantra to remember when it comes to scams. If a young, gorgeous person approaches you for a romantic liaison, be careful, and if they ask you to send them money, you can feel confident that it is a scam. If an investment is promising an outsized return, it’s also likely a scam. Be on high alert for any "luck" that seems to have befallen you and consider consulting some trusted sources for a second and third opinion before making any decisions on these special "offers."

Building on my dad’s mantra, I have created my own — if it sounds weird, it probably is. Details that don’t add up in the real world are a red flag. You are not James Bond, and therefore, you should not be asked to do anything that puts you in the middle of a spy thriller plot. This includes working with government officials, police, the FBI, major companies like Amazon or financial institutions to catch a criminal. None of the above would ever ask a citizen to do that. And, if you are told you need to keep a secret because someone in an authority position may be in on the plot, that is Hollywood writing, not reality!

The same mantra applies to someone instructing you to move money to be "safe" or directing you to change your funds into another form of currency, whether that be gift cards, cryptocurrency or something else. No legitimate law enforcement, financial services or other entities need your help to catch criminals and if your own accounts were actually at risk, no one should or will ask you to undertake spy-level activities. Again, consider consulting some outside sources, including trusted family members or authorities, before you take any action.

Another tactic to keep scammers at bay is to defuse their urgency. Creating urgency is key for scammers. They make you worried and that causes you to panic. You can diffuse the urgency by not picking up calls you aren’t expecting and not replying to any urgent calls to action. Do your research at your own pace and on your timeframe and don’t fall for pressure to act immediately, even if threatened with a bad outcome.

So, given that scammers are trying to manipulate and scam those who are aging, how can you protect yourself or your aging family members from these schemes?

Also, if you have trusted family members or friends, have them check up on aging relatives and, with their permission, their accounts frequently to make sure there aren’t any scammers circling. I say trusted because scams can come from family members too, unfortunately.

And always have a family code word or phrase. This is something known only to family members that isn’t accessible to anyone else. If there is an urgent situation that involves a family member, you can be sure that it is really them by asking for the code phrase for verification. Otherwise, it’s just another scam.

Being aware of scammers’ techniques and anticipating them can help anyone avoid becoming a victim. With so much at stake and increasingly sophisticated techniques, it’s time well spent to go over these prevention measures yourself and with your family.
