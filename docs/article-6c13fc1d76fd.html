How a 1988 Honda Prelude and pet python shaped Commanders DC Daronte Jones

2026-11-23 23:02

ASHBURN, Va. -- The gale-force Chicago wind shook Daronte Jones' car and rattled his nerves. It did not blow the future Washington Commanders defensive coordinator off his path, but it made him question his life choices.

It was March 2002, nearly three weeks after Jones landed a job at Division III North Park University on the city's northwest side, and he was sleeping in his red 1988 Honda Prelude.

At around 11 p.m., with winds reaching as high 50 mph, he called his aunt Lisa Simmons in Maryland.

"She picked up thinking it was an emergency, which it really wasn't, but it could have been, mentally," Jones said. "The car was rocking side to side.

"I remember calling home and naturally the family was like, 'Just come home. What are we doing?' That was a moment where you really had to think about how much you're willing to sacrifice, and is it worth it?"

Jones didn't go home.

That was the first decision in a long list of sacrifices the first-time NFL defensive coordinator made early in his coaching career. He lived out of his car for two months in Chicago. Then, in 2010, while a grad assistant at UCLA, he slept in the cornerbacks' meeting room for most of his year with the Bruins. Two years later, as a University of Hawaii assistant, he slept in his office for a year before moving into an alumnus' pool house.

Along his journey -- often with his pet python, Goldie Gambino, living in his book bag -- he also coached high school for two years and nearly left the profession twice -- attending law school at one point, and applying to the U.S. Capitol Police at another in hopes of becoming a Federal Air Marshal. However, like on that frigid night in the Windy City, he always resolved to stick it out.

"It was like, 'Is it ever going to end?'" Jones told ESPN. "I was like, 'Maybe it's not for me.'"

A turbulent start to his career helped him develop a deep appreciation for future opportunities -- and for finally reaching this point in his career. It taught him patience; it taught him humility.

The big breaks finally came, which have led Jones, who is a native of Anne Arundel County in Maryland, to Washington and Monday night's matchup with the Cincinnati Bengals and their high-powered offense (8:15 p.m. ET, on ESPN).

Jones may have been leaning on the perseverance he forged early in his career to weather Washington's sluggish start on defense this season. After seven weeks, his unit ranked 31st in scoring and 25th in yards. Since, it's seventh in scoring and 19th in yards.

"It can't get any worse than what it was," Jones said. "I'm not sleeping in my car. I have a home to go to. I have a great family. ... It helps put things in perspective. When you're in the storm and you're in the moment, you've got to take a step back and be like, compared to this isn't not as bad. You've got to compartmentalize the storms."

BEFORE ARRIVING IN Washington, Jones had held 14 different coaching jobs, from Division II to Division I-AA, high school, the Canadian Football League to multiple Division I stops and stints with three NFL teams.

But one of the most formative roles isn't included on his résumé: North Park University.

Jones spent six months at the tiny school in 2002, but left before coaching a game.

When he arrived at North Park as a defensive assistant, Jones, fresh off a graduate assistant role at Lenoir-Rhyne University in Hickory, North Carolina, was told he could sleep on a couch in an apartment with other coaches. Jones didn't trust the couch, which, he said, had looked like it had been pulled off the street -- so he slept in his Prelude.

He kept a suitcase in his locker but other clothes in the Honda -- along with his nearly 3-foot pet Albino Burmese python. Jones said he would park his car near the facility in a different spot each night. On cold nights, he said he'd crack the windows for airflow, turn on the heat and try to stay warm.

Simmons often lent him money when she could. But she said Jones told her, "'I'll just work it out; I'll stay in the car.' He said, 'All I need you to do is call me and wake me up.' So I would get up early and call him, 'Daronte, it's time to get up.' We really wanted things to work out for him, and he was willing to make the sacrifice. We would support him the best we could."

Simmons never wavered. Every morning at 4 a.m., Jones would receive a call from his aunt, then head to the facility to shower before others arrived.

Throughout this time, Jones said he had been telling the other coaches on staff he was living in a hotel. When some found out he wasn't, they offered him the pantry in their apartment, which was on the porch and attached to the kitchen with screens that needed fixing. Jones said the room was full of boxes and was so narrow, his double mattress couldn't lie flat -- it was curled on the outside because it wouldn't fit.

His grandfather told him how to fix and seal the screens, and Jones bought a space heater to keep him and Goldie Gambino comfortable. "I'd sleep in jeans, sweats, a hoodie, leather jacket to stay warm," Jones said. "But it was better than the car."

Four months later, he accepted a restricted earnings job at Nicholls State. Though the opportunity at North Park might have seemed to be a waste, having never coached a game for the Vikings, the sacrifices Jones made in Chicago became a key theme throughout the rest of his journey.

JONES NEARLY DROPPED out of coaching -- twice.

The first time occurred in 2004 when he was 25 and coaching defense at Jeanerette High School in New Iberia, Louisiana. It was his first year at Jeanerette but second year coaching high school in Louisiana. Jones had given himself a deadline: become a college Division 1 position coach by age 26 or quit.

He took the LSAT with plans to attend Southern University's law school with an Omega Psi Phi fraternity brother. Then, Mike Lynn, who was Jones' former position coach at Morgan State, where he played offensive line, became the head coach at Bowie State. Their connection led to a job offer. But Jones wasn't sure that he wanted to accept. He could continue teaching in high school, then attend law school classes at night.

Zeb Simon, Jones' boss at Jeanerette, told him it was a no-brainer and issued him an ultimatum.

"I'm going to give you a choice," Simon said. "Either you go or I'm going to fire you.'"

Jones went.

"It took me about a couple weeks to make that decision," he said.

Five seasons into his stint with the Bulldogs as assistant head coach and defensive coordinator -- the last of which saw his defense ranked No. 1 in Division II -- Jones reached another crossroads. The move to Division I wasn't happening. So, after the 2009 season, he applied for a job with the U.S. Capitol Police, hoping to become a Federal Air Marshal. He had received an offer, and they were in the process of doing a background check when UCLA called. "Maybe it's time for me to look at something else because now I'm in my 30s, and I was like: 'I've got to do something,'" Jones said he was thinking at the time.

The Bruins offered him a graduate assistant's job. It meant going from a salary of $64,000 a year to $1,000 per month while living in the Los Angeles area. He first consulted Damon Wilson, his boss in 2009 at Bowie State, about the UCLA opportunity. But it was longtime advisor Lional Vital, who spent part of four seasons in the NFL and also served as a scout and executive for 30 years, who convinced him.

"You've already been homeless," Vital told him.

Unable to afford the cost of living in Southern California, Jones made his "home" in the UCLA cornerbacks' meeting room, where he spent most of the year sleeping on an air mattress alongside another graduate assistant, Chris Polizzi -- now Iowa's special teams coordinator -- who slept on a futon. The arrangement disappointed Wilson.

"He felt like I shouldn't have to do that," Jones said of his UCLA living conditions. "'You've accomplished some things in your coaching career at Division II level. You shouldn't have to go from there back to [when] you were 20-something years old.' In my mind, it was like I'll take a couple steps back to go a couple steps forward."

Jones spent the next year as the defensive backs coach for the Montreal Alouettes before being hired to coach the secondary at the University of Hawaii, where he slept in his office the first year before moving into an alumnus' pool house for his final two seasons on the islands.

"Hawaii had no money," said Norm Chow, Hawaii's head coach at the time. "I [told him] it doesn't pay well and it's expensive to live there. But he wanted to go. He made it work."

Just as he had done at North Park, Jones never let his living circumstances get in the way of opportunity.

"Perseverance is the best word that describes him," said Morgan State coach Damon Wilson, Jones' boss at Bowie State. "He's had to endure a lot of things to get to where he is."

JONES DITCHED THE Prelude years ago. He now drives a 2020 Porsche 911, he lives in McLean, Virginia, a wealthy suburb of Washington, D.C., and is also the face of an impressive turnaround over the past month in particular. For the season, his defense is tied for sixth with 14 takeaways - four more than last year and only three shy of the total two years ago.

But players say Jones' approach never wavered during the tough first five weeks specifically. He said he recognized after "several games" that they needed to scale back the amount of packages being used. They wanted to play faster, more free. By reducing what they needed to focus on, Jones wanted that to result in better technique. As he told his players, "We got 99 problems but a leverage problem can't be one."

But more than anything, players said, he didn't change.

"There was no panic," Washington safety Jeremy Reaves said. "People that have gone through strenuous, tumultuous things have a sense of calmness when it gets hectic because they've been in the fire before. He's one of those guys - no overreacting, none of that."

Jones' diversity of experiences has helped him appreciate it would take time.

"He understood that Rome wasn't built in a day," Reaves said. "Sometimes you have to unlayer to re-layer. He's very self aware. We drew some things back to focus on what we were really good at and then slowly added on once the confidence was built."

Linebacker Sonny Styles said things are starting to click, and defenders are reacting quicker as they increasingly understand Jones' scheme.

"He's still pushing; he still believes in his guys," Styles said.

Performance has turned around, and Monday provides their biggest challenge. But Jones won't be intimidated by that prospect. During his 20-minute drive home each night, he said he often talks to himself, providing a reminder of the journey he's traveled to reach this point. At games, during the National Anthem, he said he closes his eyes and thanks God for putting him in this spot.

And if those gratitude reminders aren't enough, there's one more: a quote that was sent to him when he was in Minnesota with the Vikings -- during another tough period. He said he made it his screensaver and it explains why he could handle the early struggles.

"Just remember," it reads, "five years ago you prayed to be where you are now."
