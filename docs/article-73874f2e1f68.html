How LA homeless agency’s retreat is a sign of sanity

2026-09-18 01:50

No one’s sad to see the Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority partially withdraw from the fight against regional homelessness.

The LAHSA Commission this week retreated from key duties it’s long been entrusted with, including the point-in-time count of homeless people and two systems that track homeless people, services and referrals.

Good.

For this group, fewer responsibilities for public-service provision means fewer opportunities to squander taxpayers’ money.

Besides, why should a basket-case organization, under federal investigation for alleged mismanagement, compete to retain control of LA’s federally funded homelessness programs?

Just this week, federal authorities arrested three people for alleged fraud involving homelessness funds, including money that flowed through LAHSA. Michael Young, founder of the nonprofit Home at Last, allegedly swiped more than $12 million in taxpayer cash and spent it on luxury travel, real estate, vintage-car restorations and more.

Los Angeles needs change, not more of the same.

Kudos to the Trump administration for imposing a measure of accountability on LAHSA and others.

LAHSA has long been blasted for poor financial management; failure to demonstrate results; missing records; lack of transparency, lax oversight and shoddy spending controls; alleged conflicts of interest; and on it goes.

And then there’s the entitlement.

The agency maintains headquarters in the AON Tower in LA –– a posh building with amenities like a helipad, car service and a cold plunge.

How does that look for a group whose mission is (supposed to be) assisting people who dwell in squalid tents?

Skid Row in downtown LA comprises 50 square blocks of squalor.

Why keep doing the same things that haven’t worked?

LA County finally said it’d had enough in 2025, when it voted to pull more than $300 million in funding from LAHSA and take over some of its functions.

Even the city of LA, almost farcically inept on the issue of homelessness, has cut funding to the troubled agency, though City Hall has not yet managed a long-promised study on alternatives to LAHSA.

The entire is mess is driven, in part, by a sordid homeless industrial complex.

Public officials hand billions of taxpayer dollars to nonprofits and other agencies to address homelessness. Those groups enrich their own officers while further entrenching homeless, to keep the tax dollars flooding in. Repeat on a loop.

Holding the out-of-touch, scandal-plagued LAHSA accountable is a step toward sanity.
