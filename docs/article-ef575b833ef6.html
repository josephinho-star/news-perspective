Bangladesh and Mecca pact: Redrawing South Asia’s security geography

2026-09-08 21:05

Bangladesh’s possible participation in the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement raises a larger question than whether another country will join a new military pact. If Dhaka eventually becomes a member, it could alter the geographical and political character of the arrangement itself.

The pact currently brings together Türkiye, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan. Türkiye connects it to the Eastern Mediterranean and NATO, Saudi Arabia anchors it in the Gulf, and Pakistan brings South Asia into the framework. Pakistan therefore makes South Asia one end of the pact. Add Bangladesh, and the agreement gains a second South Asian member sitting directly beside India’s northeastern states and the Bay of Bengal.

South Asia and the Middle East are still often discussed as separate strategic theaters, although the boundary between them has become increasingly porous. Gulf economies depend heavily on South Asian labor, while energy, remittances and maritime trade connect the two regions every day. Defense relations are also becoming more important, particularly as Türkiye expands its military-industrial partnerships across Asia. The Mecca pact gives these existing connections a new security dimension.

The most immediate consequences would be felt in India. Bangladeshi membership would not automatically mean that Dhaka would fight alongside Pakistan in a future India-Pakistan conflict. The pact follows a collective-defense principle, but its exact military obligations and the freedom individual members would retain during a crisis still require clarification.

Even without direct military involvement, New Delhi would have to factor the pact into its calculations during another India-Pakistan crisis. In another India-Pakistan crisis, Pakistan might receive intelligence, weapons, logistical assistance, air-defense support or coordinated diplomatic backing from its partners. Bangladesh would add a particularly sensitive geographical dimension. India shares a long border with Bangladesh, while the country lies beside the northeastern states and occupies an important position on the Bay of Bengal. A security arrangement involving both Pakistan and Bangladesh would therefore be read very differently in New Delhi from one centered on Pakistan and two geographically distant partners.

India is only one part of Bangladesh’s calculation. Critics in the country’s foreign-policy debate have questioned whether joining a formal defense arrangement is compatible with Bangladesh’s long practice of non-alignment. They also point to a mismatch between Bangladesh’s immediate security concerns and those of the founding members, and warn that Dhaka could find itself drawn into disputes originating far from South Asia. Bangladesh has substantial interests with India, China, the U.S., Japan and other partners whose priorities will not always coincide with those of Türkiye, Saudi Arabia or Pakistan.

Such concerns reflect the weight geography has traditionally carried in Bangladeshi foreign policy. India surrounds most of the country’s land borders, and the asymmetry between the two states is impossible to wish away. For many diplomats and analysts, maintaining room for maneuver therefore means avoiding commitments that might transform somebody else’s rivalry into a Bangladeshi problem.

Others in Bangladesh’s foreign-policy debate look at a wider map. Bangladesh is South Asian, but it is also a Bay of Bengal state with growing economic and defense relationships beyond the subcontinent. It is one of the world’s major contributors to U.N. peacekeeping and has long maintained important ties with the Gulf. Its Muslim-majority identity also gives political meaning to relations with countries such as Türkiye, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, even though religious affinity alone cannot determine foreign policy.

This disagreement is partly about what strategic autonomy should mean for a smaller state.

Bangladesh has traditionally used non-alignment as a way of preserving flexibility. For Bangladesh, strategic flexibility has usually meant keeping several options open, not remaining equally distant from every security arrangement. Smaller states can also increase their autonomy by widening the number of partnerships available to them and reducing the possibility that one larger neighbor or great power becomes indispensable.

The attraction of the Mecca pact can be understood in this context. Türkiye has become an increasingly important defense partner, with an industry producing drones, armored vehicles, naval platforms and other systems of interest to Bangladesh. Saudi Arabia’s relationship with Bangladesh is sustained by labor migration, remittances and investment, while relations with Pakistan have warmed considerably since the political transition of 2024. A defense framework involving all three could open further space for training, technology transfer, intelligence cooperation and political coordination.

The same strategy creates a difficulty of its own. Bangladesh may seek membership precisely to gain greater freedom of action, yet formal alliances create expectations as well as opportunities. Diversifying away from excessive dependence on one regional power does not guarantee freedom from new obligations elsewhere.

A serious India-Pakistan confrontation would expose this tension immediately. Dhaka might prefer neutrality, but Pakistan’s status as a treaty partner could generate expectations of diplomatic support, intelligence cooperation or some other form of assistance. Similar difficulties could arise if the strategic priorities of pact members came into conflict with Bangladesh’s relations with China or other important partners. Strategic autonomy therefore depends not simply on having more partners, but on retaining the ability to make independent choices when those partners disagree.

There is also a less visible effect of defense cooperation. Militaries that train together become familiar with one another, while intelligence-sharing and defense-industry links create forms of trust and dependence that accumulate over time. Officials who meet regularly through the same institutions also begin to discuss threats through a more common vocabulary. None of this guarantees political unity, but it can gradually change which crises are regarded as distant and which are treated as matters of shared concern.

The consequences are not confined to military capability. Repeated cooperation can shape how states understand their relationships with one another and, eventually, the boundaries of the security region to which they believe they belong.

It would be premature to call the agreement an “Islamic NATO”: the pact is still young and its members answer to very different external relationships. Bangladesh would make that complexity even greater. It would bring the Bay of Bengal into an arrangement that already connects Türkiye, the Gulf and Pakistan, while forcing Dhaka to reconcile a wider security role with its longstanding preference for strategic flexibility.

For Bangladesh, this makes the terms of membership more important than the symbolism surrounding it. Dhaka needs clarity on the collective-defence commitment, decision-making procedures, burden-sharing, military obligations and, above all, how much freedom a member would retain when a crisis does not directly threaten its own security.
