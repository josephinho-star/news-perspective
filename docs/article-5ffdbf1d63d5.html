Kenyan doomsday cult leader convicted over further 123 deaths

2026-11-27 17:19

A court in Kenya has convicted the leader of a doomsday Christian cult of the manslaughter of 123 of his followers.

Paul Mackenzie was found guilty in Mombasa of ordering his followers, all adults, to fast to death in Shakahola forest near the coast between January 2021 and September 2023.

Earlier this month Mackenzie was jailed for 200 years for murdering 191 people, all but one of them children, in the same mass killing.

Mackenzie had pleaded not guilty to manslaughter and one of his lawyers said he would appeal against the conviction after sentencing, Reuters news agency reports.

The court also convicted 29 others including Mackenzie's wife, Rhoda Mumbua Maweu, of their part in the manslaughter.

Sixty defendants were acquitted. The Director of Public Prosecutions indicated that an appeal would be launched against their acquittal.

The self-styled pastor was arrested in 2023 after the discovery of more than 400 bodies in shallow graves in the Shakahola forest, near the Indian Ocean coast.

The court found Mackenzie had "systematically persuaded his followers to fast until they died", Kenya's Office of The Director of Public Prosecutions said in a statement on Friday's verdict, which it described as "the final conviction" in the cases against him.

"Children were to go first, then women and then men, with Mackenzie himself meant to die last," the office said.

The prosecution argued that Mackenzie and his accomplices had acted together as part of a suicide pact to fast to death in order to "meet Jesus".

Witnesses testified that he had preached that education, medicine and ornaments were sinful.

Those who opposed his teachings were beaten to death, according to the verdict in his trial for the murder of 191 people.

The defendants had claimed that the victims had fasted out of their own free will.

Earlier this week, Mackenzie was also convicted of:

assault causing actual bodily harm and five counts of cruelty to a child

engaging in organised criminal activity

facilitating the commission of a terrorist act

being in possession of an article connected with an offence under Kenya's Prevention of Terrorism Act, 2012.

Mackenzie established Good News International Ministries in 2003. Over the next 16 years, the church grew in popularity to draw thousands of followers in branches across Kenya.

A television station called Times TV would broadcast his sermons from his church in the resort town of Malindi.

His End Days sermons included conspiracy theories that described education as "evil", healthcare as "against the doctrine" and digital identity cards as "the mark of the devil".

After the main church shut down in 2019, Mackenzie moved to Shakahola forest, encouraging his devotees to follow him.

The community was described as mud houses scattered across the vast forest.

"We lived a quiet normal life, fasting for a few days then having a small meal," said survivor Peter Shukrani, who was rescued after falling ill.

When the shallow graves were discovered, local men were hired for the horrifying task of exhuming the remains.

"Every grave we found had at least three bodies," James, one of the gravediggers, told the BBC. "Some of the remains were of children as young as a few weeks old."

"We once found 12 bodies of children in a line inside a grave. It was massive, the bodies were arranged like clothes. One on top of another."

Gravediggers described some of the bodies of children as having had their "hands and legs tied and bodies wrapped in bedsheets".

Post-mortem records showed many of the victims had died of starvation while the remains of others were mostly skeletal, making the establishment of their cause of death impossible.

Stephen Mwiti, 48, said he had lost all six of his children to the cult. His former wife was one of those convicted of murder alongside Mackenzie.

"I'm a happy man, that all persons have been convicted," he told the BBC outside court last month.

"All six of my children died in Shakahola forest. I only found and buried the remains of my son Samuel."

The deaths have shone a spotlight on the lack of oversight of rogue religious activities and how they could have carried on for so long.

The gravity of the case prompted President William Ruto to set up a commission of inquiry, although this was later declared unconstitutional.
