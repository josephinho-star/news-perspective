Inside the gymnastics lore of Bengals superstar Ja'Marr Chase

2026-11-23 23:02

CINCINNATI -- It was a move that looked so improbable that the majority of the 70,747 in attendance inside NRG Stadium didn't seem to process it in real time.

When the Cincinnati Bengals visited the Houston Texans in Week 2, quarterback Joe Burrow heaved a deep ball toward wide receiver Ja'Marr Chase ("F--- it, Ja'Marr's down there somewhere"). As Chase stumbled toward the near right corner of the end zone, he twisted his body, somehow located the ball to make the catch and then dragged both of his feet inbounds to complete a touchdown.

While the crowd in Houston reacted in disbelief as the replay on the videoboards confirmed it was a catch, TV announcer Kevin Harlan extolled the All-Pro receiver while watching the catch in slow motion.

"Oh, the contortion!," Harlan exclaimed. "The body control! The concentration!"

It was almost as if Harlan was talking about a gymnast. Instead, he was talking about a 6-foot, 205-pound dynamic route runner who is one of the best wide receivers in the NFL.

Over the years, Chase has spoken about how he took up gymnastics as a kid, once adding that it was something that never leaves his spirit. Given how well he controls his body and can still backflip from a standstill position, it could seem as if those skills might have helped him become an All-Pro player. But with a bit more digging, the reality isn't that linear.

"In gymnastics, you got to stick the landing," Bengals wide receivers coach Troy Walters told ESPN. "And in football, the more times you stick the landing and not go to the ground and fall down, probably the more successful you're going to be.

"I could definitely see that [connection]."

Chase was a child with energy that needed to be expended. Sometimes, that manifested in activities outside of football, such as skateboarding. But that also meant leaping off things such as a house, garage or garbage can.

Toleah Chase decided that if her son, who was around 9 at the time, was doing all that, it would be done correctly. Chase's older brother, Terell Smith, spearheaded the mission.

"My brother knew somebody [who had access to a gym], and we met there when it was closed, and he taught me how to backflip," Ja'Marr told ESPN earlier this year. "My mom only made me do it so I wouldn't hurt myself."

After Chase came back from learning the backflips, he would call Toleah into a doorway in their home to showcase his new skill.

"He was very excited about it," Toleah told ESPN. "And I was a nervous wreck.

"I always used to tell him, 'Please be careful.' Don't hurt yourself. He was like, 'No, no, I got it, Mom. I know how to do it right.'"

He wasn't exaggerating. In 2019, Chase racked up 1,780 receiving yards in 14 games, and along with Burrow, led the Tigers to a national title. Two years later, Burrow and Chase were reunited in Cincinnati, where Chase has made catches that look straight out of a floor routine.

Teaching gymnastics concepts to football players isn't new. Jay Clark, who led LSU's women's gymnastics team to an NCAA runner-up finish this year, is a proponent of having athletes do some form of gymnastics at a young age because of the foundational skills that translate to other sports

"It's control due to the spatial awareness that they have -- the aerial sense, knowing where the ground is and being able to feel that innately," Clark said. "Some people can be born with it, but the reality is that our sport probably combines every part of athleticism better than any other, whether that's strength, power, quickness, spatial awareness, balance, all of it."

Occasionally, Chase will showcase his "gymnastics" skills in fun ways. The first example was against the San Francisco 49ers in 2023, when he celebrated a touchdown with a backflip in the end zone.

"I just wanted to have some fun," Chase said after that game. "I did gymnastics when I was young."

Two years later, it came up again when asked if he did things to grab his parents' attention while he was growing up.

"I did gymnastics, y'all," Chase said in September 2025. "It can't leave my spirit."

This year, those incredible catches have highlighted another strong season. Along with his catch against Houston, he had a leaping, twisting touchdown grab against the Miami Dolphins when he secured the ball with his shoulder pad before landing back on the field.

After a slow start, Chase is on pace for one of his best seasons. He's coming off a game in which he had 11 catches for 231 yards against the Pittsburgh Steelers in the Bengals' fifth straight win. It was Chase's third career game with at least 225 receiving yards, tying Jerry Rice, Calvin Johnson and Julio Jones for the most in NFL history.

Chase will lead the Bengals against the Washington Commanders on "Monday Night Football" (8:15, ET, ESPN), and it should be no surprise that he excels in primetime. He has 110.5 receiving yards per game in 17 primetime games, the NFL's highest mark since the 1970 merger.

"The way Ja'Marr is able to track the ball down the field and just catch it way off his body -- the hand strength, the tracking ability -- it's pretty special," said Bengals offensive coordinator Dan Pitcher.

Chase's body composition is one of the things that make him unique. Since he entered the league in 2021 as the fifth pick, coaches and teammates noted how Chase had all the tools of a wide receiver but was built similar to a running back. That enabled him to break tackles and pick up yards after the catch.

But as this year has displayed, one of Chase's greatest strengths is his ability to control his frame while making catches.

"A lot of times, guys go up, and when they come down, they're all out of whack," said Walters, who has been Chase's position coach since he entered the league. "They hit the ground hard, the ball bounces out.

"But he comes down where he's able to control his body or contort his body in the air to make an acrobatic catch."

Similar to Walters, Higgins can understand the comparisons to a gymnast, but Higgins sees something more basic: "Just a freak athlete at the end of the day [who] has God-given talents."
