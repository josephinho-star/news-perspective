Mamdani grilled on rhetoric toward Oct 7, combatting antisemitism as Jewish New Yorkers fear current climate

2026-11-19 01:49

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani was grilled Wednesday on his efforts to combat antisemitism during his appearance at The New York Times DealBook Summit.

After greeting his "comrades" at the beginning of his sit-down, Mamdani was pressed by The New York Times' Andrew Ross Sorkin about the importance of "language," specifically in relation to the Jewish community in New York City and growing antisemitism.

Sorkin pointed to Mamdani's controversial X post commemorating the Oct. 7 terrorist attack committed against Israel, which dedicated more words toward the plight of the Palestinians and Israel's military response in Gaza than the roughly 1,200 Israelis who were brutally murdered that day by Hamas.

"The head of the Reform Jewish movement (had) said at the time that they thought that the statement made New Yorkers less safe. And then there was a rabbi who said — they called himself a fan of yours — he said, 'Can we just have one day?'" Sorkin told Mamdani.

"And I'm curious, again, about the language you use. And the reason I mention it is, you know, 53%, I think, of hate crimes in New York City are against Jews.

"And, so, when you use language in this regard — and I know we can have a whole debate about whether condemning Israel gets conflated with antisemitism and this and that — but do you feel like there's an extra sort of responsibility or distinction in the language you use when you use it?"

Mamdani pushed back at the premise of the question, saying his X post "doesn't cover the entirety of the day," which included a vigil honoring the Oct. 7 victims, noting that he was also protested for attending the vigil.

"My politics is one that believes that there has to be room to mourn everyone," Mamdani said.

"And what I sought to do not only in the statement, but also in my physical attendance at that vigil, which I had attended the year before, was to make clear that I will oppose with every bone in my body the violations of international law, no matter who commits them, who conducts them. And that's why I've called Oct. 7 a war crime. And that is why I have also been immensely critical of the Israeli government as well."

The democratic socialist mayor slammed the "objectionable" statistic that Jewish New Yorkers are the victims of more than half of all hate crimes despite being 10-12% of the city's population.

"That, to me, is unacceptable, because in addition to being proud of being the mayor of New York City, I'm also proud to be the mayor of the most Jewish city in the United States of America," Mamdani told Sorkin.

"So much of what so many of us love about New York is tied up in that same legacy. And to me, the responsibility is you have to root out antisemitism. You have to root out any form of bigotry.

"And that there will be, and there are, Jewish New Yorkers, as well as New Yorkers of other faiths or no faiths at all, who disagree with me on my views on Israel and Palestine," the mayor continued. "There's no question. My job is to still deliver for them as well, and to deliver them the best possible city, because, at the end of the day, they live in the same city."

"I get it," Sorkin interjected. "My question is, would you ever think about sending out one tweet on Oct. 7, saying how terrible what happened on Oct. 7 was, and then waiting till Oct. 8 to say how terrible you think Israel has handled the situation?"

Sorkin's follow-up question sparked applause from the audience.

"When the Israeli government is conducting a genocide of Palestinians, oftentimes the defense that will be given, no matter what day that it's asked about, will be the relationship to Oct. 7," Mamdani responded.

"My point being, this is a day that is not only a horrific war crime. It is also a day that has been used as a justification for all that followed it. And it is in that day that it is important, like in any day, to mourn the totality of its impact."

Mamdani insisted he understands "the other side" of the argument but doubled down on his stance.

"I have a politics of universality. Whenever I'm asked about 'Why do you support Palestinian freedom or Palestinian rights?' I speak about the importance of extending these same values, of empathy. And that is who I am," he added.
