Carney eyes 'other options' for embattled Canadian auto sector on China trip

2026-11-17 15:17

The plight of Canada's car industry has become one of the clearest stress tests of Ottawa's effort to weather US tariffs and chart a path forward.

The past year and a half has been tough on the sector since US President Donald Trump imposed a 25% tariff on non-American-made vehicles, upending decades of free trade and automotive integration with Canada.

Now Prime Minister Mark Carney has signalled that he is looking to China, where he is on a visit for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit (Apec), as part of the solution.

But that avenue is not without controversy, and is once again drawing the ire of those close to Trump.

While in Cambodia earlier this week, Carney told reporters that Canada could turn to European and Asian carmakers to save its auto manufacturing sector if a trade deal with the US could not be reached.

"Look - great auto workers, important market, real assets in Canada," he said. "If they're not fully utilised in a North American context, that provides other opportunities.

"Canada's going to move forward either way."

He went on to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping in China on Tuesday for their second head-to-head in less than a year. The two talked afterwards of the importance of developing their relations, and Carney said they had agreed to further tariff cuts.

Neither side mentioned vehicles specifically, though analysts suggest the issue will have been prominent in their minds.

In January, Carney said that opening the door to China's electric vehicles, external (EV) could result in investment into Canadian-owned companies and the creation of manufacturing jobs in the sector.

American tariffs on imports of Canadian-made vehicles have led to thousands of job losses in Canada and anxiety over the future, as 90% of cars made in Canada are shipped to and sold in the US.

At the same time, China has been eyeing Canada as a potential entry-point to the North American EV market. Earlier this year, Canada announced that it would let in 49,000 EVs from China at a low tariff rate, with plans to raise that quota gradually to 70,000 by 2030.

The move was a significant break from the US, which has tariffed Chinese EVs heavily since the presidency of Joe Biden to protect domestic manufacturing.

Chinese EV manufacturers like BYD and Chery have already been lobbying the Canadian government for access.

Last week, Canada's Minister of International Trade Maninder Sidhu toured a Chery plant in China, external to discuss their potential entry to the Canadian market. He told reporters afterwards that Canadians could expect to see affordable Chinese electric vehicles for sale in Canada by early 2027.

These moves come as the toll of US tariffs on Canada's auto sector has become harder to ignore.

Stellantis, an American car manufacturer that made models like the Dodge Challenger and the Chrysler 300 in Canada, recently announced it would sell its assembly plant in Brampton, Ontario - a move that Carney characterised as a "betrayal" of the plant's 2,200 workers.

The facility had been idle since 2023 for a planned retooling, but what was supposed to be a temporary shutdown became permanent after Trump's tariffs, leading the company to shift some manufacturing to Illinois in the US.

Stellantis said it would sell part of its Brampton plant to Roshel, a Canadian defence contractor and manufacturer of armoured vehicles.

The Canadian auto workers' union has expressed concern about the deal, calling it "underhanded" and warned that Canada would lose thousands of high-paying jobs as a result.

The deal has also led to speculation that a Chinese EV manufacturer could move into the facility, after Canadian media outlet CTV News reported, external, citing anonymous sources, that Stellantis was planning to lease back 25% of its plant to assemble "affordable" EVs.

There are prominent voices who oppose any such move, including within Canadian politics.

In a letter to Carney, Ontario Premier Doug Ford warned against opening the door for Chinese EV kits to be assembled in Canada at the Brampton plant.

China overwhelmingly builds its cars at home and ships them whole - or to be assembled in kits - overseas.

Lynette Ong, director of the China Governance Lab at the University of Toronto, said introducing Chinese auto manufacturing in Canada would be "controversial" due to fears that Canadian jobs could be lost and that the North American auto manufacturing industry could be eroded.

"We want to make sure they're hiring Canadian workers, that they are helping train Canadian workers and that they are transferring the technology over," Ong said.

She argued that Canada should also be wary of making itself vulnerable to potential security breaches and foreign interference from China, as well as risking the ire of the Trump administration.

That already appears to be happening. Peter Navarro, Trump's senior counsel for trade and manufacturing, warned Carney against cosying up to China in a post on X, external.

"Are u really threatening to play China off the U.S.? This is offensive in the extreme," he wrote on Monday. "You should be far more worried China is going to eat Canada alive."

His comments were reposted by Pete Hoekstra, the US ambassador to Canada.

Canadians' views of China have grown more positive since Trump's return to the White House. An Angus Reid poll released on Tuesday, external suggests many are open to Chinese investment in the EV sector, but with additional security or restrictions.

The Carney government has set terms for China's entry to Canada's auto market, including that any venture should be jointly-owned by a Canadian company, that it must follow Canadian labour standards and that the cars should be built using Canadian-made parts.

Ahead of his meeting with Xi on Tuesday, Carney also noted there are "clear guardrails" in how Canada deals with China, including barring work on sensitive matters like artificial intelligence and critical minerals.

Canada has also eyed other partnerships. It is notably pursuing associate member status with the EU, and had signed a free trade deal with the Philippines earlier this week.

Just how much Canada is willing to embrace Chinese manufacturing, however, still depends on the outcome of ongoing US-Canada trade talks.

Progress has been made in negotiations with the US, Carney recently said, but differences remain. It is unclear if Trump and Carney will speak on the sidelines of the Apec summit later this week.
