Hakeem Jeffries on brink of House history as he tells Democrats to ‘fight Maga, not each other’ – US politics live

2026-11-19 19:24

Hakeem Jeffries said it is the job of Democratic lawmakers to “fight Maga extremism” as he stood on the brink of becoming the first ever Black house speaker.

Democrats on Wednesday resoundingly nominated Jeffries to serve as speaker of the House when the new Congress is sworn in next year.

A New York congressman who has served as the Democratic minority leader since 2023, Jeffries is set to become one of Donald Trump’s most prominent opponents for the final two years of his presidency, when Democrats will control both the Senate and House of Representatives and are expected to undertake aggressive efforts to check his powers.

After his nomination was secured during closed-door voting, 251-11, ahead of a full House vote, Jeffries promised to take on the Trump administration’s Maga approach to governing the US. “Our job is to fight Maga extremism, not each other,” he said, “and to get big things done for the American people.”

Democrats nominated Jeffries despite concerns from some progressives that he represents a politically risky status quo for a party that is looking to capitalize on a surprisingly good performance in the midterms, where voters swept them back into power in the House and Senate with significant majorities.

He would become the first Democratic House speaker since Nancy Pelosi, who will retire from Congress at the end of this year.

Meanwhile today, president Trump has left China after hailing his trilateral meeting with Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping, saying the three leaders were “really making history”. Trump said the US, Russia and China, the world’s largest nuclear powers, were “uniquely positioned” to discuss reducing their nuclear stockpiles.

Federal judge says Trump's defamation lawsuit against the New York Times and Penguin Random House can proceed

A federal judge in Florida ruled Thursday that Donald Trump’s defamation lawsuit against The New York Times and Penguin Random House can move forward, handing the president a legal victory in his ongoing fights with major media organizations.

The suit centers on a book by two Times reporters and a series of articles that Trump alleges were “false, malicious, defamatory, and disparaging” and intended to undermine his 2024 presidential campaign, which he ultimately won. The complaint also targets reporting by longtime Times White House correspondent Peter Baker.

The Times and Penguin have denied the allegations, arguing in court filings that the lawsuit is legally deficient and designed to score political points.

While Judge Steven Merryday dismissed two of Trump’s claims, he found that the remainder of the complaint plausibly alleged “actual malice” by the reporters, allowing those claims to proceed.

Democrats are celebrating after, with Mary Peltola’s victory in Alaska, they have officially secured 56 Senate seats after the midterm elections. But Jamie Davis, who lost an incredibly close election for a Senate seat in Louisiana and could have made it 57, told the Guardian it “stings to come close”.

In Louisiana, which has not elected a Democrat to the Senate since 2008, Davis lost to the Republican congresswoman Julia Letlow 49.1% to 50.9%. He was about 25,000 votes short in a state that cast about 1.5m votes.

“Truthfully, I mean, it kind of stings to come close, but what hurts more than anything else is the fact that, still today, people don’t realize and know what we did,” Davis said. “There are still some people out there right now today who do not realize that I only came within a point – I think it’s one point, 1.1 or 1.2 points of winning this election.”

Across the south, Democrats are now wondering what might have been, as other Senate seats were also close. Barry Moore beat Everett Wess in Alabama by nine points, which is a landslide in many states and a catastrophe compared with Donald Trump’s 30-point margin in 2024. Angie Nixon, whose progressive politics and opposition to the Israeli government looked like a mismatch with Florida’s conservative electorate, came within three points of unseating the Republican incumbent, Ashley Moody, in a special election.

In the weeks since, Democrats have questioned which of those states might have been winnable had the national party cast its light in the south. And many have zeroed in on Louisiana.

Unlike Florida, Louisiana is not an expensive media market. And Louisiana does not have the depth of partisan political identification to be overcome in a place like Alabama.

Turnout nationwide ran about three points higher in November than four years ago. In Louisiana, it ran eight points higher, in line with states facing competitive US Senate elections like Michigan and Maine.

Senator Bill Cassidy and Trump have long been at odds. Cassidy voted for his impeachment and asked him not to run in 2024. Trump targeted the Louisiana Republican in the primary, endorsing Letlow.

The Callais decision from the US supreme court originated in Louisiana and prompted the state to postpone primary elections and set off redistricting battles this year. Combined with Cassidy’s third-place finish in the primary, the motivating effect of these events on Black voters should have also set off alarm bells among Democratic fundraisers and operatives, Davis said.

“When it became open, that’s the time when Republicans and Democrats and independents all should have been on notice that there’s an open seat in the Senate,” Davis said. “And not just automatically think that the seat is going to turn to be Republican.”

The Trump administration’s plans to build a “smart border wall” through a 500-mile stretch of Texas that includes the Big Bend national park recently got the green light from the supreme court. But those efforts are likely to slam into a big, blue wall.

Last week’s decision – the result of a continuing challenge to the administration’s decision to waive dozens of federal laws to speed wall construction in the Big Bend region – dealt a major blow to environmentalists and park enthusiasts, as well as locals who value the region’s remoteness, landowners who could see their properties devalued and businesses that rely on outdoor tourism.

But the ruling also came less than a week after Republicans suffered staggering losses in the midterms – including the once-in-a-generation flipping of Texas from red to blue. The state elected a Democratic governor after more than 30 years, and its first Democratic senator since the 1980s. The blue tsunami that handed both chambers of the US Congress to the Democrats with sizable majorities also flipped the Texas state house and weakened Republican control over the state senate.

That could all throw a wrench in the Trump administration’s plans.

The border wall is a federal project, so the White House will still have a great deal of power to push it forward. But for the first time, US Customs and Border Protection (CBP), the agency overseeing construction, will now face open hostility from the Lone Star state’s government as it contemplates resuming building efforts.

“Texas sovereignty and land should be protected,” Gina Hinojosa, the governor-elect, said in response to the ruling. “By letting the federal government bulldoze our land, the supreme court is continuing to put corporate profits before the people of Texas.”
