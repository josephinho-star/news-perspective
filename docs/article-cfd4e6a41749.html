Charlie Kirk supporters vow to ‘continue what he started’ during emotional Utah Valley University ceremony one year after his murder

2026-09-11 01:55

OREM, Utah — Thousands of Charlie Kirk supporters packed an arena at Utah Valley University on Thursday night to honor the conservative activist one year after he was assassinated on the campus.

Students and friends of the Turning Point USA co-founder, who was just 31 years old when he was gunned down during one of his popular open mic events, vowed to “continue what he started” in a touching ceremony.

“Today, one year later, at Utah Valley University. We remember Charlie Kirk. We mourn Charlie Kirk, and we honor Charlie Kirk,” Sage Lloyd, the President of UVU’s TPUSA chapter, told the massive crowd.

“Perhaps the greatest way to honor him is not simply to remember what he did, but continue what he started,” Lloyd said.

“Our chapter has grieved, we have struggled, and we have carried things that few people outside of our own experience will ever completely understand, but through all of the hardship, we are still here.”

“The same students who witnessed a political assassination are willing to stand up, speak out, and keep moving forward all because of the impact one man, Charlie Kirk, had on their lives.”

The massive event, which kicked off at 8:30 EST, also featured speeches by radio host Glenn Beck, Christian author Frank Turek, and Turning Point USA’s Chief Field Officer Andrew Sypher.

Sypher told the crowd he was just 10 feet away from the conservative commentator when he was shot dead.

“I was standing 10 feet to his left when he was assassinated a short walk from this building. Today marks one year,” Sypher said.

“We did not come back to rehearse a crime scene. We came back because Charlie Kirk represented the best of us,” he added.

“He was a husband, a father, a God-fearing man who never stopped leading by example,” Sypher said. “Successful, yes. But that is the least important true thing about him tonight. He loved this country unmistakably.”

Moria Dickman, a 24-year-old Senior at UVU, told The Post she joined TPUSA when she learned that Charlie was visiting the campus and was in line to ask him a question a year ago when shots rang out.

When she escaped campus and learned Kirk was the one who was shot, she said: “I just felt grief.”

“He cared about having conversations. He cared enough to visit our campus. He could have been kinder, but he was really saying what he believed. And that’s something to look up to,” Dickman, a psychology major from Provo who considers herself “nonpartisan,” said.

Jaxson, a 21-year-old liberal studies major at UVU, added that he was disappointed in the “sickening” memes posted online in the wake of Kirk’s death.

“It’s the jokes that are sickening. Both sides making jokes is unacceptable,” Jaxson said.

“Charlie always tried to bring us together.”

Connor Holmstead, 36, only casually watched Kirk before his death, but showed up to his memorial on Thursday in a TPUSA shirt.

“It’s safe to say I’m a fan of his now, for sure,” Holmstead said.

“I don’t think the movement has changed at all. … The whole culture of America was a lot more leaning lefty… but it brought a lot of light to the mission, I think. Of just talking to people. Trying to understand one another,” he said.

The packed service comes as TPUSA membership has surged since Kirk was killed.

Chapters of the conservative “pro-liberty” nonprofit — founded by Kirk in 2012 — have exploded on college campuses by 64% during the 2025-2026 school year. The number of high school TPUSA clubs has also surged by a whopping 180%.

“Charlie Kirk’s murder inspired, and continues to inspire, hundreds of thousands of young people to get involved in TPUSA and to continue his mission,” TPUSA said of its founder in a statement ahead of the anniversary.

Dickman said that she noticed TPUSA membership explode after Kirk’s death, but said it has “petered off” due to “opposing discourse,” such as baseless conspiracy theories spread by Kirk’s former colleague and friend, Candace Owens.

The memorial drew backlash from UVU’s Students for a Democratic Society club, which planned to protest the event — which shut down campus and canceled classes on Thursday, the Salt Lake Tribune reported.

“We, students of UVU, strongly condemn the decision to hand over our campus to TPUSA on September 10. As we approach the anniversary of this tragic event, it should be made clear that many of us will be further negatively affected by allowing TPUSA to occupy our campus on that day,” students said in the open letter addressed to the campus administration.

UVU President Jon Anderson stressed that the event is not sponsored by UVU, though the public school did vet plans for the memorial and is providing security.

“As an institution, it’s important that we stay apolitical, that we don’t jump in the fray,” Anderson said this week, per the outlet. “… Any entity could’ve used that space, and they chose to have that day. And we allowed them to do that.”

Last year’s horrifying shooting sent thousands of spectators at the public Utah university fleeing for their lives and spurred a 33-hour manhunt culminating in the arrest of alleged gunman Tyler Robinson.

Robinson, 23, allegedly used his grandfather’s hunting rifle to gun down the married father of two over his “political expression” against gay marriage, trans rights, and “the LGBTQ+ agenda,” prosecutors have said.

Robinson will stand trial for first-degree murder with political targeting and other aggravating factors for last year’s shooting, a charge that could give Robinson the death penalty if convicted.

The Utah Valley University service was one of several rallies and vigils honoring the conservative icon worldwide — with Thursday’s events stretching from New York City, Georgia, Florida, Nevada, and Sydney, Australia.

National Republicans also planned to honor Kirk with a special tribute during their massive midterm convention in Dallas Thursday night.
