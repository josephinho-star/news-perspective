House lawmakers return to Capitol Hill as Republicans hope to pass stopgap spending bill

The US House is back after a five-week recess. And this week, Republicans are focused a key legislative agenda item: passing a stopgap spending bill to stave off a possible government shutdown.

Currently, funding is guaranteed through 30 September, but after a year marked by record-breaking shutdowns, GOP House speaker Mike Johnson is hoping to advance a bill that would extend funding spending at its current levels until after the November midterms.

Johnson is expected to put a vote on the House floor this week, using a fast-track procedure that requires a two-thirds majority to pass.

Prior to the Senate heading home for their August recess, the upper chamber approved its own continuing resolution to keep the government until 11 December, this also included provisions like preventing political appointees having approvals over federal grants.

The House is set to leave Washington again at the end of this week for Labor Day recess, provided it can get through its to-do list.

Their meeting – of which Bessent revealed few details – came amid speculation that the US would struggle to successfully isolate Iran under its latest sanctions without buy-in from China.

Bessent pushed back on those criticisms during his CNBC interview. He said that the US and China are largely aligned on their goals for Iran, including reopening the strait of Hormuz and keeping nuclear weapons out of the regime’s hands. But he also argued that the US could succeed in crippling Iran’s economy with or without China’s cooperation.

The Treasury Secretary declined to share details of his conversation with Pan Gongsheng, or to speak in any depth about what direction the US and China are trending in – be it toward cooperation or standoff – behind closed doors.

Speaking with CNBC after his opening remarks in Asheville, Bessent said that the goal of the latest round of sanctions on Iranwas to force the Iranian regime to the negotiating table.

Pressed on whether the hardline Iranian leadership could be moved by the economic strangulation of its people, Bessent appeared to suggest that the threat of popular uprising in response to those conditions might motivate the regime to make a deal.

“The main thing they care about is their head staying attached to their neck,” Bessent said.

'We're going to hit them hard': Trump vows retaliation after Tehran targets US bases in Jordan

Donald Trump has told Fox News’s chief foreign correspondent, Trey Yingst, that the US will respond to Iran targeting US military bases in Jordan overnight.

“We’re going to hit them hard,” Trump told Fox News. “There will be a response,” he added, after Jordan’s army confirmed it had intercepted eight missiles early this morning.

In response to the US assault on Larak Island late on Sunday, the IRGC “targeted technical infrastructure, maintenance facilities and enemy fighter jet positions at the US-operated King Hussein and al-Azraq airbases in Jordan” with ballistic missiles, causing “heavy damage”, according to state TV.

In the interview with Fox News on Monday, Trump said all of the missiles were intercepted by American air defence systems, except one which was allowed to pass through the airspace because it was identified as not on course to hit anything “significant”, according to Yingst.

The White House “border czar,” Tom Homan, denied on Sunday that deporting British far-right commentator Milo Yiannopoulos just a day after being arrested by US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) amounted to preferential treatment.

Homan said it was his understanding that Yiannopoulos, 41, had overstayed what he believed to be a visitor visa, had not appeared at an immigration hearing and was issued a final order of removal on 22 July.

“He didn’t show up,” Homan told CNN’s State of the Union. “The judge ordered him deported. So, once ICE gets an order of deportation from a federal judge, their job is to execute that order, and that’s what they did.”

He added: “He had his due process, ordered removed by a federal judge, and ICE removed him. It’s that simple.”

Yiannopoulos’s removal one day after his arrest, when many others who have been deported had first endured lengthy detention center incarcerations, has prompted questions about whether he received preferential treatment in that respect. Homan said the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), which oversees ICE, was trying to speed up the deportation process.

The US treasury department is likely to unveil weekly new secondary sanctions aimed at increasing economic pressure on Iran, with an initial focus on banks, treasury secretary Scott Bessent told Reuters.

After imposing penalties on the United Arab Emirates branches of Egypt’s Banque Misr on Friday over alleged financial links to Iran, Bessent said in an interview that the next step may be cutting off an institution entirely from the dollar-based financial system.

“You’re going to see a lot more of these every week,” Bessent said ahead of a Group of 20 finance leaders meeting. “We’re starting with the banks, and we’re telling the banks it’s not OK to have Iranian money and to aid the regime.“

Bessent said he intends to drive the message home to G20 finance ministers and central bank governors to cut economic ties to Iran, or face secondary sanctions. The Treasury launched the effort, dubbed Operation Economic Outcast, last week.

“There can be no leakage,” Bessent said. “You’re either with us or you’re with the Iranians.“

The Trump administration is shuttering two of the three US Department of Agriculture research projects studying cyclospora after the foodborne parasite has sickened tens of thousands of Americans during the summer, Politico reported on Sunday.

The two projects were defunded by Congress earlier in the year, while the third is due to be moved in coming months from Maryland to Iowa as part of a broader USDA reorganization, according to the report.

Although the third project remains funded, all scientists working on the parasite have declined to relocate to Iowa, Politico reported, citing people familiar with the plans and internal documents.

Reuters could not immediately verify the report.

“None of the research has been disrupted, and research will continue without disruption,” a USDA spokesperson said, while pointing to Congress over the funding cuts.

Congress did not appropriate funding for the two research projects that were being undertaken at the Beltsville Agricultural Research Center outside Washington DC for the 2026 fiscal year in the Agriculture Appropriations Act, the spokesperson said in an emailed statement. The spokesperson added that the Agricultural Research Service is working with research leaders to ensure an orderly transition of the programs.

Having reportedly read Victor Hugo’s Les Misérables, in French, during a military tour of duty in Iraq, Jamieson Greer considered himself an unlikely target for accusations of francophobia.

But Donald Trump’s US trade representative, hitherto one of the president’s less high-profile cabinet members, found himself in that uncomfortable position amid the acrimonious collapse of bilateral trade talks with Canada that now threatens to degenerate into an all-out trade war between the two close trading partners whose economies are tightly intertwined.

An apparent pending deal between Washington and Ottawa aimed at avoiding damaging tit-for-tat tariffs broke down at the last minute, with Canada’s prime minister, Mark Carney, claiming his country had come “under attack” from the US.

Among the multiple and complex points of disagreement was a Canadian accusation that US negotiators had pressed for a weakening of legal protections for the French language, which is most heavily spoken in the province of Quebec.

Canadian provisions include legislation that mandates streaming and online video companies to invest in and promote Canadian content and promote the French language. Carney cast US objections to such regulations as a deal breaker, and said they amounted to “threats to French language and culture”.

For Greer, a fluent French speaker who once served as a Mormon missionary in Belgium, France and Luxembourg, the charge was too much. “I speak French, too,” he told CNBC, dismissing the claims as “a funny, fake story”.

House Democrat plans to introduce bill to nullify Trump's 'Lake America' executive order

Hello and welcome to the US politics live blog.

As House representatives return from the summer recess, one Democrat has already set in motion a plan to nullify Donald Trump’s latest conceited executive order.

“We have a gulf and we have a lake – now all we need is an ocean. Maybe we’ll have to change the name of the Atlantic and/or the Pacific,” he told reporters, sitting at the Resolute Desk in the Oval Office. “Maybe we’ll change them both.”

But House Democrat Debbie Dingell said she plans to introduce a bill later today to nullify the name change, titled the “Hands Off Our Great Lakes Act”.

“People do not understand how intertwined our two countries are,” Dingell told Politico, adding: “And this is insane, this naming Lake Ontario ‘Lake America.’”

Dingell said she had accrued more than 100 signatures from fellow House Democrats in a letter to the president, urging the Trump administration “to change its diplomatic approach with our closest ally, Canada, and recognize the importance of the United States-Canada relationship”.

“I grew up in a small town called St Clair, and when I got up and looked out my bedroom window every day, I didn’t see America. I saw Canada,” Dingell said. “We lived that close. Canada was as close to us as [the Cannon House Office Building] is to the Capitol.”

Trump’s order directed federal agencies to adopt the name-change on US maps, documents and databases. Google Maps has changed Lake Ontario’s name to Lake America for US users to reflect the president’s executive renaming order.
