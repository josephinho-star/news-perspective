'The Traitors' ended with a $246,300 prize—here's who won and what they told us about spending it

2026-11-27 15:49

When Shane Beatty, a contestant on NBC's "The Traitors: New Blood," deceived his fellow finalists, he walked away with the entire $246,300 prize. But the emotional toll of winning caught him by surprise.

"The second I had to reveal that I was a Traitor, everything hit me at once," Beatty, a 32-year-old ironworker from Staten Island, New York, tells CNBC Make It. "I had built genuine relationships with these people, and knowing that some of them were blindsided by my decision was difficult."

Now that he's won, Beatty has a plan for his money, starting with setting aside a sizable share for Uncle Sam. As for the rest, he's more interested in financial security than the trappings of newfound wealth.

After winning, Beatty doesn't expect his day-to-day life to change much.

"I'm still going to get up, go to work, and continue doing what I've been doing as a Local 580 ironworker," he says. "I love my career, and winning this money doesn't change that."

Beatty plans to pay for a wedding he and his fiancée previously struggled to afford, help his mother pay down debt and clear his own debts before saving the rest.

"What it does give me is an opportunity to take some financial pressure off, be a little more comfortable and put myself in a better position for the future," he says.

But before spending his winnings, Beatty is accounting for what could be his single biggest expense — taxes.

"First thing I'm gonna do with the money is put at least $100,000 in a separate bank account just so I can have it ready for Uncle Sam," he told Entertainment Weekly last week.

Setting aside $100,000 is a reasonable move given Beatty's potential tax liability, according to tax experts.

"Taxes are often an afterthought when winning big on a gameshow," says Riley Adams, a certified public accountant and founder of Sequoia Ridge CPAs. "So, it's definitely smart on his part to get ahead with his tax planning."

Game show winnings are generally taxed as ordinary income, meaning the prize money is added to a winner's other taxable income for the year. That can push a winner into higher tax brackets.

In addition to federal income taxes, Beatty, who lives in New Jersey, will have to account for state income taxes.

While Beatty did not disclose his income to CNBC Make It, ironworkers in the New York City metropolitan area typically earn around $100,000 a year, according to 2025 Bureau of Labor Statistics data.

Assuming Beatty lives in New Jersey, earns $100,000 from his job in New York in 2026, receives his winnings this year, files as a single taxpayer with no dependents and takes the standard deduction, here's how much of his prize money he could keep, according to Adams' calculations:

- Total winnings: $246,300

- Estimated taxes on winnings: $86,105

- Estimated take-home winnings: $160,195

Under this scenario, Beatty's winnings would push him from the 22% federal income tax bracket into the 35% bracket. Factoring in state income taxes, an estimated 35% of his prize would go to taxes, according to Adams' calculations. Setting aside $100,000 would give Beatty a comfortable cushion under this scenario. His actual tax bill, however, will depend on his income, deductions and other financial circumstances.

Jim Guarino, a certified public accountant and managing director at Baker Newman Noyes in Woburn, Massachusetts, arrived at a similar estimate.

"Folks tend to get excited by the 'gross amount' of their total winnings," but Beatty is "extremely tax-aware" in recognizing how much less he'll actually get to keep after taxes, he says.

For Beatty, the money won't radically change his life, but it will give him greater financial security.

"Having a savings cushion and knowing I don't have to worry about living paycheck to paycheck or falling behind on bills means so much to me," he says. "I want to be responsible with this opportunity and make sure it gives me greater financial peace of mind."
