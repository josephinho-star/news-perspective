‘Monster: The Lizzie Borden Story’ Episode 8 Recap: 10,000 Maniacs

2026-09-21 21:00

In the final episode of Netflix’s The Lizzie Borden Story, writer-creator Ian Brennan makes the bold choice to begin and end on the Aileen Wuornos story.

This series finale is bookended with scenes from the trial, conviction, and execution of Aileen as a serial killer. An astute use of VHS simulates the look of old news magazine segments and Court TV broadcasts. (Ryan Murphy regular Ari Graynor memorably cameos as a reporter). Aileen’s testimony about being brutally assaulted and raped by a man claiming to be a serial killer himself is shown in unflinching closeup. She doesn’t even finish telling her story before the show cuts abruptly to the judge sentencing her to death. All that pain, all that sorrow, and it means nothing, her life gets tossed away. It’s one of the most brutal edits I can remember in recent TV history.

Aileen is enraged and defiant when she is convicted, wishing the same kind of sexual violence on the jury and prosecutors’ families that was inflicted on her. She learns that evidence regarding the sexually violent history of one of her victims was kept from the trial. She looks forward to asking the Lord what the hell is going on around here, she tells a confidante. She dies from a lethal injection while listening to “Carnival” by Natalie Merchant.

We rejoin Aileen’s story all the way at the end of the episode, as the spirits of Lizzie Borden and Bridget Sullivan greet her and guide her to “the next place…someplace safe. All is forgiven. All is understood at last.” (Yes, this is the second Monster in a row in which characters greet serial killers at the border of the afterlife to anachronistic music. Just go with it.)

The doctor overseeing Aileen’s murder pronounces the time of death. The curtains close. The audience exits, their show over. Granted, these people just sat in the gallery to watch the execution of Aileen Wuornos, but that’s just as much a performance, a production, as anything on stage and screen. This is how the patriarchy and the state it has created for itself put on a show of justice.

Can that patriarchy be defeated? Can the power of the state be commandeered by those it has excluded? Somehow, these questions animate the end of Lizzie Borden’s story. At first she leads a life of luxury, serving as patron for actress Nance O’Neill while also turning her and her sister Emma’s enormous new home and grounds into a sanctuary for all kinds of animals, especially cats.

But Lizzie isn’t particularly happy, and has no close friends except the animals. Emma has become an outright junkie to numb the pain of living with a sister she swore to protect but who she knows murdered their parents. Bridget materializes only for a night or two a year while living an unhappy married frontier life in Montana. (Seriously, it looks like something out of The Revenant.) They finally get to the part of Elizabeth Báthory’s life where she gets walled up alive as punishment for her crimes. (Should have finished the book before murdering your parents, Lizzie!)

The Borden name lives in infamy. Obnoxious children dress as Lizzie for Halloween. Nosy, wealthy, boring guests pepper her with questions about who she thinks the real killer might have been. (Does a Portuguese guy get blamed? You bet.) Lurid stories about her, filled with lies and half-truths, fly up the bestseller lists. (That’s Ian Brennan taking a shot at his own project, just to be clear.)

It’s no life to lead.

Nor is Bridget’s attempt to turn tradwife. She nearly turns her axe on her asshole husband before simply extracting a confession that he, like all men, are afraid of “strong women.” She leaves him with the land and heads right back to Lizzie, moving in, not taking no for an answer. At Bridget’s encouragement, Lizzie prays to both God and her father and stepmother for forgiveness. Out in the woods, in the sunlight, she feels them collectively grant it.

That’s what Lizzie really needed to turn her life around, it seems. She and Bridget turn from animals to humans and fund the women’s suffrage movement, helping to secure half the (white) American population the right to vote. It’s something both Lizzie’s mother and stepmother would support, she tells the press, and for once I think she’s telling them the truth.

The suffrage movement emerges victorious. Women claim their right to vote. The suffrage activists pop champagne. Lizzie and Bridget live as a more or less open lesbian couple.

The music they’re playing mutates into “Carnival,” and we cut to Aileen Wuornos’s death chamber. At the beginning of the series, the curtain opened on Lizzie’s toy piano. Now it closes on a corpse. The show is over.

Monster hangs a lot on The Lizzie Borden Story in the end, from life in the closet to the 19th Amendment. It hangs all this on a murderer who may or may not even have committed the crime for which she remains infamous; alone among the Monster series’ protagonists, Lizzie was, after all, acquitted. Aileen Wuornos was not, and it incenses her to be thought of “like Jeffrey Dahmer,” another Monster protagonist whose story was sensationalized by, among other things, Monster, which Monster is aware of. This meta-game is yet another weight the legend of Lizzie Borden is asked to carry here.

This may be several bridges too far for many viewers. All I can say is that when the closing credits rolled, I cried like a goddamn baby.

Part of it is the use of Fiona Apple’s single saddest song, the masterpiece “I Know,” over the credits. Mostly it’s in knowing that we live somewhere in the gap between the ratification of the 19th Amendment and system that led to Aileen Wuornos being poisoned to death on a table in front of an audience.

Using women like Aileen, and women turned into modern myths like Lizzie, to make this point is a deliberate act of provocation. That’s the Monster project. The idea is that people who have done terrible things are still people, and we can make art that sympathizes with them, that attempts understand what made them what they are, without either artist or audience exploiting or condoning those terrible things. For eight episodes at a time, we can attend their carnival, and take away what lessons we can.
