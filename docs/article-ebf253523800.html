MI5 chief Ken McCallum confirms Russia did interfere in Brexit referendum - but says it had little impact

Dan Sabbagh is the Guardian’s defence and security editor.

MI5 chief Ken McCallum said today that Moscow did attempt to interfere in the 2016 Brexit referendum with anti-EU coverage on RT and Sputnik media and via troll social media accounts. But there was a big difference between “what may have been attempted and what actually resulted”, he added.

The head of the Security Service said that he agreed strongly with what the prime minister had said on Sunday and Monday, when he raised the issue of attempted Russian interference a decade ago – though Burnham then backed off when he was challenged by the Conservatives as to whether this meant he regarded the Brexit vote as illegitimate.

McCallum did not put any new evidence into the public domain but cited already known concerns about Russian broadcasters who “came in pretty strongly around the time of the referendum” in favour of Brexit. He also said that “there was a second line of interference attempted through [internet] trolls”.

However, he argued there was no evidence to suggest the result of the Brexit vote had been swayed as a result of the effort, which is generally believed to have been marginal, and said that it would be to Russia’s benefit if the issue was talked about further.

It is up to us how far we allow our adversaries to divide us.

I’m a little bit worried that if we all stay in this debate for another few days, the Russian trolls who didn’t appear to do all that much at various points over the years, they’ll be delighted they are front page news once again despite the comparative unimpressedness of what they do at all points along the journey.

Reoffending 'off the charts' because of prisoner early release scheme, Tories claim

The Tories have claimed reoffending is “off the charts” due to the prisoner early release scheme.

In an urgent question in the Commons, Nick Timothy, the shadow justice secretary, said the majority of prisoners were not being tagged before release, despite assurances from the government that the tagging system would be the “toughest in history”.

The justice secretary [Alex Norris] told the country that proper safeguards would be put in place with a presumption that all released prisoners would be tagged.

He called it the toughest tagging regime in history, but that was nonsense.

I have some advice for him and his ministers – it is better to work out how to implement your policy before it starts, not after. We know a majority of prisoners are not being tagged before release, and we know reoffending is off the charts already.

Jake Richards, a justice minister, said that Timothy should be showing more contrition because prisoners were having to be released early as a result of the Tories leaving a system where the prisons were full.

Kemi Badenoch has welcomed a poll giving the Tories a three-point bounce since their conference, and putting them just one point behind Labour.

In its write-up of its latest regular, weekly poll, YouGov says:

Our latest figures show Labour on 24% of the vote, down three points on last week’s poll, while the Conservatives are up three points to 23%, their highest level since we restarted our voting intention tracker in January 2025. Reform UK take a 21% share in this week’s poll, their joint-lowest on our tracker this parliament.

Speaking to reporters today, Badenoch suggested the fall in Reform UK’s ratings showed the public had been taking a “long look” at Nigel Farage’s party and “don’t like what they see.”

Asked whether she was confident her party could continue to rise in popularity, or whether it was just a temporary, post-conference blip, Badenoch told the Press Association:

I am confident that the momentum will continue, because people are seeing the Conservative party has changed under my leadership and there’s much more to come, this is just the beginning.

We left office two years ago, it was a historic defeat for the Conservative party. We made a lot of mistakes.

I have been showing the public that I understand what went wrong; apologised and acknowledged those mistakes. But we are the only party with common sense and a plan to unite the country.

Asked what she thought the poll suggested about Reform UK, she said:

I think the public have been taking a long look at Reform and they don’t like what they see. What I need to show them is that if they don’t want a Labour Government there is only one credible alternative.

And this is fromLuke Tryl, the More in Common pollster, on the significance of the YouGov poll figures.

Few interesting things here of which the most important thing I’d say is the level of volatility should be a further warning of risks a snap election. Lab’s conference bounce disappears in a week. Second conferences still matter at least short term. Third battle for the right is now wide open.

Zia Yusuf says he does not agree with Suella Braverman's claim minority ethnic people can't be English

Zia Yusuf, Reform UK’s home affairs spokesperson, has said he does not agree with his colleague Suella Braverman’s assertion that black people cannot be English.

Asked about her views in an interview on Sky News, Yusuf said:

In my view Ashley Cole is the best English fullback in history, and I won’t take any other position. So in my view, Ashley Cole is English. I think the argument that he’s not English doesn’t hold water with me.

Yusuf made the same argument about Britons of Asian heritage.

Nasser Hussain, who I grew up watching when I went to the cricket, the idea that those people aren’t English is an alien concept to me.

I would respectfully disagree with people, who make the opposite argument.

Braverman, Reform UK’s education spokesperson whose parents came to the UK from Mauritius and Kenya respectively, says she is British but not English. She recently explained:

To say I’m English is to take a ‘laissez-faire, anything-goes’ attitude to ethnicity, citizenship and identity. It also denies me my Asian heritage. The liberals (many of them in today’s Conservative party) take this cosmopolitan approach to Englishness. They’re terrified of being called racist so they say that anyone, as long as they’re born here, can be English.

I take the conservative approach. Englishness is an ethnicity. There have been English people for centuries and we need to recognise this more if we are to start taking a more muscular approach to citizenship and nationality. And no, it’s not racist to say this.

The UQs are on the prisoner early release scheme, and on the threats posed by Iran.

And the statements are from Pat McFadden, the work and pensions secretary, on the pensions triple lock, and from Uma Kumaran, a Foreign Office minister, on the Falkland Islands.

Miliband acknowledges ‘difficult’ relations between US and Ukraine’s allies

Ed Miliband has also admitted that Donald Trump’s decision to buy Russian diesel, and his call for Volodymyr Zelenskyy to be replaced as Ukrainian president, has strained diplomatic relations with Washington.

Within the bounds of diplomacy we should acknowledge it’s been a very difficult few days.

I was with him in Ukraine last week as we went to the site where 22 innocent civilians were killed, including five children. He feels every death personally and I think we should praise his leadership today from this forum.

Miliband says Europe needs to revive debate on what it does with frozen Russian assets

Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary, is speaking at the Warsaw Security Forum with his counterparts from Germany, Poland and Norway. Jakub Krupa has been covering it in detail on his Europe live blog.

As Jakub reports, Miliband said the EU should review its policy on dealing with Russian assets frozen under sanctions imposed since the war in Ukraine starts. He said:

Europe has big decisions to make, including on Russian sovereign assets. I think we need to return to this issue.

Britain is willing to do this alongside the European Union, because we know that every wrong message that is sent to President Putin is a message that sets back our security and sets back the Ukrainian cause, and we need to send the right messages.

Most of the frozen Russian assets are held in bank accounts in Belgium. Some European leaders want to see them seized to fund a loan to Ukraine, but the Belgians are resisting. They argue this would be against international law, and they are fearful of Russian retaliation.

Standards watchdog warns Burnham of growing concern that wealthy donors exercising 'undue influence' on politics

Andy Burnham has been warned that progress on beefing up ethical standards in public life is “stalling”.

The comment came in the first annual report to the PM from the Ethics and Integrity Commission, the body set up last year to replace the Committee on Standards in Public Life.

Doug Chalmers, the former deputy chief of defence staff and master of Emmanuel College, Cambridge, has to report every year to the PM on “the health of standards in public life” and he has delivered his first update in the form of an open letter.

A number of positive steps have been taken since the July 2024 general election towards strengthening England’s standards system, but progress is at risk of stalling. Your personal leadership is needed to accelerate standards reform and to ensure that the core values and standards expressed in the principles of public life remain at the heart of government and our public services.

In particular, Chalmers says there is a growing concern that wealthy donors are exercising an “undue influence” on UK politics because parties are ever more reliant on them for money.

The longstanding debate around the merits of a universal annual cap on permissible donations is receiving increased attention from parliamentarians and the media in light of the trend for large donations to originate from a small number of donors.

Political parties play a vital role in our democracy and need significant sums of money to function, yet the reliance on large donations from a limited number of sources creates the risk that donors may exert undue influence, or may be perceived to do so, which is damaging for trust in politics.

Chalmers does not mention Reform UK in his letter, but his comment seems in part influenced by the alarm generated by the news last month that two cryptocurrency billionaires are between them giving £72m to Nigel Farage’s party.

Whether the donations will comply with a new election law going through parliament remains to be determined, but ministers are under pressure to tighten the law further, with some MPs saying the amount an individual can give to a party should be capped.

In his letter, Chalmers says a cap is “likely to affect the major parties in different ways” and he says any reform should not be seen as partisan. “This will also ensure reforms have democratic legitimacy and are more likely to endure,” he says.

The letter also urges Burnham to press on with action to strengthen standards in other areas, including local government and lobbying.

Andy Burnham will take Britain back into European Union, Neil Kinnock claims

Good morning. In his long statement to MPs yesterday, Andy Burnham was evasive when asked whether he wanted his planned renegotiation of Britain’s relationship with the EU to result in a Labour-led government, after the next election, rejoining. In response to one question about this, Burnham told a Tory MP he was “getting way ahead of himself” with the question. But one well-placed observer is not being so coy.

Kinnock said: “Andy makes the declaration of his determination to change the relationship, and it’s obvious that his objective is to join again.”

Asked again if he thought Burnham would be the person to take the UK back into the EU, Kinnock replied:

Yes, sure, sure, sure. His commitment is authentic. He really does understand from the grassroots up why restoring what I think of as normality in our continent is vital for every party economically, politically, culturally, all in security terms, and is able to articulate it in language that people can understand. So yeah, he is definitely.

Asked about Burnham playing down his desire to revisit the referendum result during the Makerfield byelection campaign, Kinnock claimed this was just a matter of deciding when to have this debate.

Andy’s not changed direction. He’s always been a committed pro-European. In the byelection, he rightly didn’t make a noise about it. That’s tomorrow’s debate. Simply because the byelection wasn’t about Europe. It was about winning Makerfield in order to enable him to become leader of the party. That’s all it was about.

Kinnock was interviewed by Antonello Guerrera who asked how, as someone who inherited a leftwing party and dragged it to the right, Kinnock felt about a sucessor taking Labour to the left. Kinnock said he thought Burnham’s approach was right.

It’s common sense. It’s not left and right. Andy’s view of the engagement of the state is about what Winston Churchill did in the 1950s, and I don’t remember Churchill being thought of as on the left. Andy’s a socialist, but he’s a very common-sense socialist. He’s very rational. He’s cool, not just with his dark blue T-shirt, but in his thinking, and he’s advanced a hell of a lot in the last 10–12 years in terms of his understanding of the absolute importance of utility, of usefulness in politics.

That’s what puts us, basically, he and I, on the same spot. Of course he’s on the left. I’m on the left. We’re socialists, but we are prompted by the usefulness of the application of ideas.

We have got a lobby briefing later and no doubt No 10 will clarify that its understanding of Burnham’s position on rejoining the EU is not quite the same as Kinnock’s. Kinnock is a former vice president of the European Commission, and an ardent pro-European, and perhaps there is some wishful thinking in what he says. But he is also a good friend of Burnham, and an astute observer of Labour politics. You will have to decide for yourself if he is right.
