The lesson of the bizarre, tragic death of Fiona the hippo

2026-11-21 00:24

A child had a Mary Tyler Moore moment in Cincinnati this month — and on Thursday, a beloved hippo named Fiona died as a result.

In a bizarre series of events at the Cincinnati Zoo, a child reportedly threw a knit cap into the air, and in a freak accident, it was swept up by an overhead ceiling fan and propelled through a small gap in the glass separating the enclosure from the viewing public.

It was a sick Rube Goldberg event, the likes of which even Kevin McCallister from “Home Alone” could only dream.

Fiona swallowed the hat, and despite extraordinary efforts to help her pass it, the cap ultimately led to the animal’s demise.

Immediately, the internet erupted in sorrow — and fury.

Commenter after commenter on social media called for the arrest and punishment of the family of the child.

It wasn’t a fringe sentiment, if likes and views are any indication.

“Y’all gonna go after the owners of the hat right?” Eliza Delossantos Kelly demanded on the zoo’s official Facebook page.

“The family of the child who killed Fiona the hippo should AT THE VERY LEAST be held legally responsible for the veterinary expenses,” read one much-praised X comment.

Internet rumors abounded about the incident: how many times the hat was thrown, the age of the child and whether the family alerted zoo staff to the hat.

The zoo itself has offered little comment beyond stating, “We probably couldn’t make that happen again if we tried. It was clearly an accident.”

Never mind that, wrote X user David Frozen Arnold: “The Cincinnati Zoo, and really any zoo, should institute a mandatory 1:1 adult to child ratio for any visitors with children under 10. Parents are just not sufficiently looking after their children.”

A mandatory one-to-one adult-to-child ratio. At a zoo. Should families with four children have to recruit two additional adults just to visit the giraffes?

Perhaps the zoo should simply ban families altogether and save everyone the trouble.

As a mother of six, I have no patience for the gentle-parenting nonsense that has convinced a generation of parents that discipline is emotional abuse.

But here’s the thing about children: They’re not robots. They do stupid, silly things. They’re unpredictable in the best and worst ways possible.

But in the Fiona case, could this even be classified as a stupid mistake? Who could have imagined a thrown hat could possibly lead to this?

The notion that every instance of childhood misbehavior or rambunctiousness is evidence of parental negligence could only come from people who have never spent much time around actual children.

And that’s exactly who we’re dealing with.

With Americans having fewer children, more adults are living lives in which children are a foreign species.

They don’t have children of their own, their friends don’t have children, and they rarely encounter them outside of the occasional airplane, restaurant or zoo visit.

When they do, they’re appalled to discover that children sometimes behave like . . . children.

A child cries on an airplane, and the internet debates whether airlines should offer child-free flights.

A toddler has a meltdown in a store, and strangers demand that parents keep their offspring at home.

A child throws a hat at a zoo, and thousands of people decide the family should be arrested.

The message is clear: Children are welcome in public only if they can be guaranteed not to behave like children.

Which means a world in which children aren’t welcome in public.

And this is how America’s fertility crisis became a vicious cycle.

The fewer children people encounter, the less tolerance they have for them.

And the less tolerance society has for children, the less appealing it becomes to raise them.

We are now at a place where the mere presence of children in public is treated as an imposition on everyone else.

Parents are expected to guarantee perfect behavior, and when they inevitably fail, the internet is ready with its pitchforks.

Fiona’s death was a tragedy. But almost as tragic, it has made clear that the only thing some Americans hate more than a dead hippo is a living child.

Bethany Mandel writes and podcasts at The Mom Wars.
