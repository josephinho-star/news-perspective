Alcohol taxes and marketing need review, inquiry finds

A parliamentary inquiry into the health impacts of alcohol and drugs has tabled its final report, recommending new mandatory alcohol advertising and marketing regulations be developed along with national standards for the online sale and delivery of alcohol.

The way alcohol is taxed should also be reviewed, the inquiry recommended, with many of the health groups giving evidence to the inquiry that a uniform volumetric tax across all alcohol products is needed.

Spirits, pre-mixed drinks and beer are currently taxed volumetrically with the amount of tax paid determined by the volume of alcohol within the product. Wine and cider are taxed based on their wholesale price, rather than alcohol content, leading to cheap wine with high alcohol content.

Many previous government reviews have recommended a volumetric tax for wine.

Ayla Chorley, the CEO of the Foundation for Alcohol Research and Education, said the recommendations “provide a clear pathway to reduce alcohol-related harm”. She said:

This harm is not inevitable.

The way alcohol is marketed and sold should be determined by governments, not the industry that profits from a harmful and addictive product.

DrErin Lalor, the CEO of the Alcohol and Drug Foundation, said the report was a milestone. She said:

This report highlights the significant harms associated with alcohol and other drug use, including long term chronic health conditions, relationship breakdowns and violence against women and children.

One Nation migration plan would badly damage international education, universities warn

Universities Australia has accused One Nation of threatening to destroy “one of Australia’s biggest exports”, warning that “slashing international student numbers would do lasting damage”.

The comments follow Pauline Hanson’s announcement of the party’s immigration policy, which would cut the number of temporary migrants in Australia by more than 750,000 over three years. The plan targets international students and family members of skilled migrants in a “net-negative” migration policy.

Universities Australia’s chief executive, Luke Sheehy, said the major political parties were competing in “a race to the bottom on international students”. In a statement, he said:

A vote for dramatically fewer international students is a vote for a poorer, less skilled Australia. You can’t take skills, jobs and billions of dollars of economic activity out of the country and pretend there will be no consequences.

International education is one of Australia’s biggest exports. It supports jobs across the economy, helps build the skilled workforce we desperately need and strengthens our relationships around the world.

It also helps keep our universities going. For years, governments have asked universities to do more while public funding has failed to keep pace with the cost of teaching, research and infrastructure. International education has helped fill that gap.

If politicians want to rip that revenue out of the system, they need to tell Australians what replaces it.

The Nationals leader. Matt Canavan. has questioned the detail and viability of One Nation’s latest immigration policy, saying the party has failed to provide clear costings or explain how its proposed migration cuts would affect key industries.

Speaking on ABC’s Afternoon Briefing, Canavan said:

It’s a bit hard to judge this policy. It’s the fourth migration policy One Nation has had in four weeks, and still we don’t really see proper costings. There’s no indication of exactly what the numbers would be, that 750,000 figure is over three years.

That’s really important. Those details are really important to industries like farming. There is no doubt we need to get migration numbers down, a lot lower than what the government has them at the moment or even what they want to get them to.

Federal government announces $10m dollar support package for Whyalla workers

The federal government has announced a $10m joint support package for the 500 Whyalla workers who face losing their jobs, their families and the broader community affected by the decision to abandon efforts to restart the plant’s troubled blast furnace.

The package will provide career support, transition assistance, skills recognition and funding for workers who want to upgrade or expand their skills. Wellbeing services and financial counselling will also be available.

Speaking in Canberra, employment and workplace relations minister Amanda Rishworth said that while it was “a very difficult day”, the decision was not unexpected and support measures had been planned.

The Peter Malinauskas and the Albanese Labor government have been working to plan for the event if this was to occur, so we’ve been able to immediately stand up worker support.

This package has been thought through and will provide support to those workers and, importantly, their families, because we know that these workers are not the only ones affected.

Importantly, we will be working with the South Australian government to ensure that workers who are affected and will no longer have work as a result of this closure get other job opportunities.

About 500 Whyalla steelworks employees will lose their jobs after officials abandoned efforts to restart the plant’s troubled blast furnace.

The decision is expected to put about 500 out of work, according to the plant’s administrators KordaMentha, taking the workforce from the steelworks, the port and the associated mine down from about 1,700 to about 1,200.

The decision to abandon further revival efforts shuts down Australia’s sole local manufacturer of rail steel and idles one of only two integrated steel facilities in the country.

The Whyalla steelworks were part of British industrialist Sanjeev Gupta’s metals empire until early last year, when the South Australian government tipped the operations into administration after years of underinvestment.

The South Australian premier, Peter Malinauskas, said today that the blast furnace’s “days were numbered”.

There was always the intention that it would close. We would have liked it to last a little bit longer, but, alas, it hasn’t been able to.

Last year, the federal and state governments unveiled a $2.4bn support package to keep the plant running, pay employees and creditors, upgrade infrastructure, and ultimately entice a new buyer to reinvigorate the plant.

The coal-fired blast furnace has been offline since April. Administrators are seeking a buyer to restore operations.

Australia’s old tech is vulnerable to AI hacking, top intelligence chief says

One of Australia’s top intelligence agencies has warned that old technology is leaving Australian businesses and governments vulnerable to AI attacks.

Abigail Bradshaw, director general of the Australian Signals Directorate, has told the Sydney Dialogue to take note of Anthropic’s recent warning of AI agents and hacking risks. She said the ASD did not know how many agents were on the internet:

I think that prospect of getting some sort of definition or calculation of how these agents are out there is really remote, and I would just say a lot.

She said Australian systems faced “enormous” hidden costs of maintaining outdated technology but said they needed to be addressed:

If we don’t move fast enough, almost certainly it would be legacy technology which is first compromised in a major AI-enabled attack.

Government and business had the same issue and neither had the funding to replace all of its old systems, she said. But she said partial replacement had to start immediately and that would mean taking down systems that Australians expect to be available all the time.

What AI calls for, because of the ease and speed in which vulnerabilities are detected, and the need now to patch not every Tuesday, not every two weeks, but potentially within 48 hours, means that we are asking people to make a different decision. We are asking private owners of infrastructure to actually optimise security over system availability. And that means we’re all going to have to be a little bit patient and do it.
