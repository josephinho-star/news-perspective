Fugitive developer Jean Nassif details feud with David Elliott, telling Icac that Liberals were ‘rejecting all my properties

Returning to Jean Nassif‘s appearance at the NSW Icac, questioning has focused on the property developer’s feud with centre-right Liberal figures, including David Elliott, who he says were “rejecting all my properties across the suburbs and putting blocking on it”.

Asked about comments by Elliott in 2019 about constituents complaining of defects in apartments built by his company Toplace, Nassif describes Elliott as “a racist person and ... corrupt politician”.

Nassif says: “I want to stop at that for now, because I’ve got plenty to say”.

Elliott, who has told the commission that Nassif began to target him after he opposed his developments, is not accused of any wrongdoing.

Counsel assisting Peggy Dwyer SC asks how Nassif came to know with Jeremy Greenwood and Christian Ellis, who, alongside Charles Perrottet, are alleged to have taken $2m in prohibited donations from Nassif. Greenwood and Charles Perrottet have rejected that payments were for political outcomes, while Ellis, who has moved to the UK, is not expected to give evidence.

Nassif says he spoke at a function to the Liberal figure JP Abood and the former Liberal prime minister Tony Abbott, during which he says he complained to Abbott about “my problems with the centre-right”, including federal and state Liberal MPs.

Nassaf says Abood suggested he speak to Ellis and Greenwood, claiming Abood said: “They might take over the Hills soon, so talk to them and see if they can help you.” Abbott is not named as a person of interest in Icac’s inquiry and is not accused of any wrongdoing.

On Sunday, the state’s environment minister, Emily Bourke, said the sea lion was found dead at Seal Bay on Kangaroo Island, Australia’s third-largest colony of the animals.

As of the latest federal figures, Australia has recorded more than 500 confirmed events of H5 bird flu in more than 30 species of wildlife, the vast majority of cases in greater crested terns, a common native seabird. South Australia accounts for nearly 300 cases.

Dfat providing assistance to family of three Australians found dead in Bali

The Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (Dfat) is assisting the family of three Australians who died in Bali.

The ABC reports Balinese police are investigating the deaths as a suspected murder-suicide after a man and his two children were found dead inside a home in Kuta on Sunday morning. Dfat said in a statement:

We send our deepest condolences to the family at this difficult time.

Owing to our privacy obligations we are unable to provide further comment.

The ABC says a welfare check was conducted at a bungalow rented by the family after the man’s wife had left for Jakarta. On arrival, authorities discovered the bodies. A city police official told the broadcaster:

Investigators are carrying out enquiries, including crime scene analysis, reviewing CCTV footage and interviewing witnesses, including the victim’s wife to establish the exact cause of death.

The Guardian has reached out to Balinese police for comment on the matter.

About 500 Whyalla steelworks employees will lose their jobs after officials abandoned efforts to restart the plant’s troubled blast furnace.

The decision is expected to put about 500 out of work, according to the plant’s administrators KordaMentha, taking the workforce from the steelworks, the port and the associated mine down from about 1,700 to about 1,200.

The decision to abandon further revival efforts shuts down Australia’s sole local manufacturer of rail steel and idles one of only two integrated steel facilities in the country.

The Whyalla steelworks were part of British industrialist Sanjeev Gupta’s metals empire until early last year, when the South Australian government tipped the operations into administration after years of underinvestment.

The South Australian premier, Peter Malinauskas, said today that the blast furnace’s “days were numbered”.

There was always the intention that it would close. We would have liked it to last a little bit longer, but, alas, it hasn’t been able to.

Last year, the federal and state governments unveiled a $2.4bn support package to keep the plant running, pay employees and creditors, upgrade infrastructure, and ultimately entice a new buyer to reinvigorate the plant.

The coal-fired blast furnace has been offline since April. Administrators are seeking a buyer to restore operations.

Australia’s old tech is vulnerable to AI hacking, top intelligence chief says

One of Australia’s top intelligence agencies has warned that old technology is leaving Australian businesses and governments vulnerable to AI attacks.

Abigail Bradshaw, director general of the Australian Signals Directorate, has told the Sydney Dialogue to take note of Anthropic’s recent warning of AI agents and hacking risks. She said the ASD did not know how many agents were on the internet:

I think that prospect of getting some sort of definition or calculation of how these agents are out there is really remote, and I would just say a lot.

She said Australian systems faced “enormous” hidden costs of maintaining outdated technology but said they needed to be addressed:

If we don’t move fast enough, almost certainly it would be legacy technology which is first compromised in a major AI-enabled attack.

Government and business had the same issue and neither had the funding to replace all of its old systems, she said. But she said partial replacement had to start immediately and that would mean taking down systems that Australians expect to be available all the time.

What AI calls for, because of the ease and speed in which vulnerabilities are detected, and the need now to patch not every Tuesday, not every two weeks, but potentially within 48 hours, means that we are asking people to make a different decision. We are asking private owners of infrastructure to actually optimise security over system availability. And that means we’re all going to have to be a little bit patient and do it.

The Albanese government has named the former Liberal leader Sussan Ley to the board of Snowy Hydro Limited.

Ley, who quit politics after being rolled as opposition leader by Angus Taylor in February, was appointed by the energy minister, Chris Bowen, and the finance minister, Katy Gallagher.

Snowy Hydro, the massive hydroelectricity project in regional New South Wales, is a government owned corporation.

The government also appointed Merryn York, the former chief executive of Queensland’s Powerlink and a former executive with the Australian Energy Market Operator.
