3 weight loss mistakes GLP-1 users make — that can ruin their progress

2026-11-25 16:11

Getting slimmer on a GLP-1? Don’t let these slip-ups weigh you down.

Millions of Americans have tried the next-generation diabetes and obesity drugs, with 11% of US adults currently taking one to drop extra pounds, according to a July Gallup poll.

But don’t get too comfortable watching the numbers on the scale fall. Doctors warn that a few common missteps can quickly sabotage users’ hard-earned success and put them at risk of regaining what they’ve lost.

“What most often derails progress of weight loss are three preventable mistakes,” Dr. Maram Khalifa, an endocrinologist and assistant professor of medicine at UConn Health, told The Post.

The first is not eating adequate protein or doing enough resistance training to preserve muscle.

GLP-1s work by mimicking hormones produced in the gut after eating. They suppress appetite, help regulate blood sugar and slow how quickly food leaves the stomach, making people feel full longer.

While that can help users drop significant amounts of weight, it also means they’re eating less — and potentially missing out on important nutrients, including protein. That can cause the body to burn through muscle along with fat.

“In people who are not getting enough calories or protein, we see poor nutritional status and loss of muscle mass, which can lead to less fat loss and less overall weight loss,” said Dr. Jacinta Anyaoku, a family and obesity medicine physician and the founder of Hibiscus Aesthetics and Wellness in Katy, Texas.

To help preserve muscle, Khalifa recommends getting more than 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily and incorporating resistance training, such as lifting weights, into your routine.

The second common mistake GLP-1 users make is stopping the medication without a plan to maintain their results.

“Many people start GLP-1s thinking that once they hit their goal, they can just stop the medication. They don’t necessarily think about a long-term weight-maintenance plan,” Anyaoku said. “The reality is, obesity is a chronic condition that requires long-term treatment. Once the medication is stopped, the weight can easily return.”

Studies consistently show that many people regain a substantial portion of the weight they lost after stopping GLP-1s, sometimes within weeks or months of their last dose.

One analysis found that people regained an average of 60% of their lost weight within a year of stopping the medication. Another projected that patients would return to their starting weight in roughly 1.7 years.

The rebound can also come with worsening cardiometabolic risk factors, including higher blood sugar and cholesterol levels.

Rather than stopping cold turkey, Khalifa said patients should work with their doctors on a long-term strategy.

“The American Diabetes Association’s 2026 guidelines support continuing the lowest effective dose, intermittent rescue therapy or structured lifestyle support rather than abrupt cessation,” she said.

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and constipation are among the most common complaints among GLP-1 users. And when these symptoms become too difficult to tolerate, patients may throw in the towel and stop taking the medication altogether.

“Poor GI side-effect management drives non-adherence,” Khalifa said. “When patients stop due to intolerance, they lose both ongoing benefits and the opportunity to taper or transition to alternative maintenance strategies.”

The good news is that patients don’t have to just grin and bear it.

Khalifa said there are several ways to make GLP-1s easier on the stomach, including gradually increasing the dose, drinking plenty of water, eating slowly and opting for smaller, lower-fat meals. Cutting back on alcohol and fizzy drinks and keeping a food diary to figure out which foods trigger symptoms can also help.

If that doesn’t do the trick, doctors may prescribe anti-nausea medication, hold off on increasing the dose or lower it before considering switching to another drug.

Often, Khalifa said, these three mistakes go hand in hand.

“Inadequate protein intake may be driven by poorly managed GI adverse effects limiting food tolerance, while abrupt discontinuation may follow frustration with side effects or weight plateau,” she explained.

To avoid falling into these traps, Anyaoku recommends thinking about the long game from the very beginning, rather than focusing solely on reaching a goal.

“Remember that obesity is a chronic condition and may require long-term treatment in order to maintain the weight loss,” she said. “Work on establishing healthy lifestyle habits from the start.”
