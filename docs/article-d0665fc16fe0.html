Trump on war in Iran, Houthi advance in Yemen: 'It’ll all work out'

In a new interview with NewsNation on the war in Iran – where the president’s hopes for a quick toppling of the Iranian regime have dissolved into an ever-escalating regional conflict – President Trump said he was still waiting to determine how he would move forward.

“Well, we’re gonna see what happens,” Trump told NewsNation. “Right now, they want to make a deal. And if the deal’s not a correct deal, then I wouldn’t even think about it. But right now, they want to make a deal because they’re losing in every way.”

Since the initial strikes on Iran failed to force the Iranian leadership to capitulate, Trump has increasingly turned to economic measures. After Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, strangling global energy supplies, the Trump administration responded with a counter-blockade. And the administration has installed a new round of secondary sanctions aimed at cutting Iran off from the countries still doing business with it.

Trump also addressed his conversations with the Houthis – the Yemeni faction whose rapid advance on the Red Sea coast threatens shipping through another vital artery of global trade.

“Well, we’re talking to the Houthis also, and they would like to see if we can make a deal,” Trump said.

Majority of Michigan voters dislike Trump's Canada tariffs, new poll finds

Ove 60 percent of prospective voters in Michigan, a border state both heavily dependent on trade with Canada and key to control of Congress in 2026, dislike the Trump administration’s tariffs on the US’s northern neighbor, according to a new poll.

The poll by the The Washington Post and Schar School found that most likely voters it surveyed in Michigan, including a majority of independents, weren’t a fan of the tariffs on Canada. Support largely broke down along party lines, with 89 percent of Democrats against them, but only a slim majority – 56 percent – of Republicans in favor.

Michigan hosts one of this year’s most closely-watched Senates races, between Republican congressman Mike Rogers, a Republican, and his progressive Democrat challenger Abdul El-Sayed. Unlike other Republicans in northern border states, who have spoken out against the escalating US/Canada trade war, Rogers has largely voiced support for Trump’s tariffs.

The latest round of US tariffs against Canada snapped into effect in late August, after negotiations between the two countries broke down in the eleventh hour. Canada retaliated with new tariffs on US goods, disrupting a flow of trade vital to the economies of border states like Michigan.

Josh Shapiro, Gavin Newsom stake positions on AI regulation

As the threat of an AI apocalypse becomes a growing issue ahead of the midterm elections – and as both parties are struggling to form an identity around the subject – two prospective 2028 presidential candidates are going public with their positions: Josh Shapiro and Gavin Newsom.

First at an event on Thursday in Pittsburgh, and later in an interview with NBC News, Pennsylvania governor Josh Shapiro made an appeal for guardrails on AI technology.

Shapiro’s take landed somewhere between President Trump’s all-out support of AI development and the doomsaying of high-profile opponents like Bernie Sanders. Steering a middle course, Shapiro called for comprehensive regulations on AI development without putting a halt to it altogether.

“We’ve got to regulate this, we have to have real guardrails, we have to understand this technology better,” Shapiro said in an interview with NBC News.

“You have to have third parties in the room with these companies, understanding what they’re doing,” Shapiro continued. “When the CEO says ‘I can’t control what I’ve built, and what I’m building could destroy humanity,’ that’s not a time to kick the can down the road.”

Once a cheerleader of data center development in his state, Shapiro began to take a more skeptical position toward AI as pushback began to grow.

He isn’t the only 2028 hopeful seriously mulling AI policy; Gavin Newsom, governor of California (home of the AI industry’s biggest players), also told POLITICO that he’s considering taking further action on the tech before his term expires.

Head of Postal Service tells AP work has stopped on Trump-backed mail voting system

Earlier this week, the Supreme Court struck a final blow at President Trump’s audacious plan to centralize authority over mail-in voting under the federal government, which would’ve given his administration a say in who does and doesn’t get to vote by mail.

By Thursday, the behind-the-scenes efforts in the Postal Service to bring that system to life had ground to a halt, David Steiner, Trump’s postmaster general, told the Associated Press in a new interview.

Steiner and his agency had been caught in the middle of a legal and political whirlwind since March, when Trump signed an executive order seeking to position the USPS as an arbiter of mail-in voting. Under the terms of the order, states – which typically oversee their own elections – would’ve been required to turn over lists of eligible voters to the federal government for approval, and adopt a uniform design for mail-in ballot envelopes. The USPS could’ve refused to deliver ballots for states that declined to go along.

The whole project was the latest manifestation of Trump’s fixation with mail-in ballots and illegal voting by noncitizens, a largely debunked issue which has nonetheless formed a cornerstone of Trump’s domestic policy agenda in his second term.

In the same interview, Steiner dismissed rumors that the strain of being torn between Trump and the courts had prompted him to consider resigning.

Analysis: What Trump’s attacks on mail-in voting mean for the midterms

For the millions of Americans who vote by mail – almost a third of the country – the week began with significant uncertainty that they would be able to easily submit their ballot this fall, thanks to a March executive order from Donald Trump that aimed to restrict mail-in ballots. By Monday night, the supreme court restored a measure of order by rebuking the president and rejecting his plan – but Trump’s frothing response portends more danger for the rapidly approaching midterms.

Trump’s March order forbade the US Postal Service from delivering mailed ballots to anyone the administration deemed ineligible. The Department of Homeland Security, working with the Social Security Administration, would have devised that list of supposedly ineligible, noncitizen voters. On its face, the move appeared plainly illegal. One expert told the Guardian’s Sam Levine, “This is a wholly unconstitutional EO,” because ultimately the federal government cannot set election rules; only states can.

The plan was full of logistical problems, too. A whistleblower told Congress earlier this month that the Postal Service simply didn’t have the ability to implement the system safely, and that the whole plan was “perilously rushed and potentially unlawful”, according to Richard Blumenthal, the senator who handled the whistleblower complaint. State election officials also complained they would be unable to implement Trump’s restrictions this close to an election, even if they were deemed lawful. Alabama, North Carolina and Wisconsin had already begun mailing out ballots before the supreme court ruled, and chaos would have reigned there and across the country had the court blessed Trump’s plan.

Trump officials sending undocumented children to Texas sparks fears of mass deportation

The Trump administration has started sending a majority of undocumented children who came to the US alone and have new immigration court cases to Texas, where government facilities for them are unlicensed, their chances of getting a lawyer are low and advocates fear that “a deportation churn-out factory” awaits them, the Guardian has learned.

Almost 80% of children in federal custody who arrived unaccompanied by a parent or legal guardian and who were placed in immigration court proceedings in July – and over 90% in August – have been funneled into shelter networks somewhere in Texas, a dramatic upswing from the months prior, according to analysis shared exclusively with the Guardian from researchers at the immigration data insights platform bklg.org.

This is understood to amount to a total of 925 children in the care of the Health and Human Services (HHS) department’s office of refugee resettlement (ORR) and in open immigration court proceedings based in Texas, with 651 sent there in July and August alone.
