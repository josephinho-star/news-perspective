Taking Ozempic or Wegovy could lead to extra cancer screenings, new study suggests

2026-11-26 00:02

Women taking popular weight loss medications may have higher breast density on mammograms and require more follow-up imaging, even when none of those abnormalities turn out to be cancer.

That's according to a new study scheduled for presentation at the annual meeting of the Radiological Society of North America, Nov. 29-Dec. 3, 2026, in Chicago.

The study examined 107 women, ages 34 to 79, who took GLP-1 receptor agonists, a class of drugs that includes Ozempic and Wegovy, for weight loss for at least one year.

Participants lost between six and 107 pounds, with an average loss of 40 pounds. Researchers reviewed 214 screening mammograms taken before and after at least a year of treatment, according to the study.

Specialized software showed that breast density rose by at least one category in 62.5% of participants who took GLP-1s. Eight women moved up two density categories.

Radiologists also saw smaller breasts in roughly 58% to 79% of patients, depending on the reviewer, and increased skin thickening in about 9% to 18%.

Higher breast density is generally tied to a greater risk of breast cancer and can make tumors harder to spot on X-rays.

However, the pattern seen after weight loss likely reflects a shift in proportions rather than more dense tissue growing, according to lead author Dr. Eva C. Gombos, a diagnostic radiologist at Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston.

"It was surprising that 62.5% of women had increased BI-RADS breast density despite substantial weight loss, with no decreases observed," Gombos said in a release.

"This may reflect disproportionate loss of breast fat, causing a relative increase in mammographic density even as absolute fibroglandular volume decreases."

When a person loses significant fat from the breast, the remaining milk ducts and supportive tissue — known as fibroglandular tissue — can make up a larger share of what shows up on the image, even if the total amount of that tissue did not grow.

Gombos also noted that losing breast fat and volume can leave extra skin or skin folds, which may help explain why skin and connective tissue strands looked more prominent on some scans.

Nearly 22.4% of the women were called back for additional imaging after screening, compared with an average U.S. recall rate of about 10%.

Two women had technical recalls to repeat scans due to positioning or imaging issues. Four women were referred for biopsies, and all four came back benign. All other callbacks also turned out to be benign or negative, including three simple cysts.

Gombos emphasized that the findings should not pose a concern for women taking GLP-1 drugs or cause them to skip routine screenings.

Whether these density shifts carry any long-term change in breast cancer risk remains unclear and needs more research.

The study was retrospective and looked back at medical records rather than following patients forward in a controlled trial, the researchers noted.

It did not establish whether the imaging changes affect long-term breast cancer risk.

Gombos recommends that women tell their breast imaging team if they use GLP-1 medications or have recently lost a significant amount of weight because it helps technologists and radiologists more accurately compare new scans to older ones.

Novo Nordisk, maker of Ozempic and Wegovy, emphasized that it continuously monitors the safety profile of its medicines, reviewing emerging data as it becomes available.

"Individuals respond to treatment uniquely based on multiple biological and lifestyle factors, which means outcomes can vary from person to person," a spokesperson told Fox News Digital.

"We recommend that all patients maintain close contact with their healthcare professional to discuss any questions or concerns about their treatment, and for ongoing guidance and careful monitoring."
