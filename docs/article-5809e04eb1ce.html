Home Office orders review after two small boats reach Kent without being intercepted by Border Force

Not every migrant boat can be intercepted, a cabinet minister has suggested, after two uncontrolled landings from the Channel in two days. The Press Association says Lucy Powell, the education secretary,said “(you) can’t stop everything all of the time”, the day after 21 people were detained when a boat evaded Border Force to reach the beach of Samphire Hoe in Dover. PA reports:

It was the second undetected landing in two days, following a dinghy making it to shore at Folkestone on Monday morning.

Shabana Mahmood, the home secretary, who has ordered an urgent review by the Border Security Command, said the failure to intercept the two boats was “not acceptable”.

Powell echoed that language in interviews this morning, while suggesting not all crossings could be prevented.

Asked on BBC Radio 4’s Today programme if the situation was embarrassing for the government, she responded: “[You] can’t stop everything all of the time. Of course, it’s a concern. That’s why the home secretary has demanded and ordered this urgent review, because this is not what we want to see. This is not acceptable.

“But the overall picture is an improving picture. It is hard, difficult work that requires constant, constant effort by our border forces, our security services, police, our partnerships with France and other countries.

“This is a mammoth and huge task, which is now starting to reap some dividends. But of course, you know where there are still issues arising, we must absolutely tackle them.”

MoJ figures show crown and magistrates' court backlogs at record high, with 24,000 serious cases held up more than year

Backlogs in magistrates’ courts and crown courts have reached a record high, according to Ministry of Justicefigures released today.

At crown courts in England and Wales, where the most serious crimes are prosecuted, there were 80,829 cases waiting to be heard at the end of June. That is a record since the MoJ started compiling these figures, and represents a 3% increase on the previous year.

These cases are also taking longer to process. Average waiting times, from the case being referred to crown court to it being finished, are also at a record high, with the median at 203 days, a 21% increase on the previous year. (The mean is much higher because the relatively few very, very long waits distort the average when it is calculated as a mean, not a median.)

And at magistrates’ courts, there were 380,230 cases waiting to be heard at the end of June, up 9% on the previous year.

Sarah Sackman, the courts minister, said the figures showed “the scale of the challenge facing our courts, with victims waiting far too long for justice – that’s why it’s our priority to reduce the backlog so that cases are heard sooner”.

But Nick Timothy, the shadow justice secretary, claimed Labour policies were making the problem worse.

Labour have spent their time in office attacking our ancient right to a jury trial, rather than taking effective action to tackle the courts crisis.

With both the crown court and magistrates’ court now facing record backlogs, Labour’s proposals to simply shift the problem from one part of the court system to another are dead in the water.

Mark Evans, president of the Law Society of England and Wales, said the figures also showed that 23,706 crown court cases have been in the backlog more than a year. This is 22% more than a year ago.

The government must seize the chance to deliver a long-term, whole-system plan for criminal justice reform. This should include usable courts, enough judges and staff and the maximum number of sitting days to hear cases promptly.

Labour special advisers summoned last week to the pillared state room on the first floor of No. 10 to hear the plan for conference from Burnham’s political secretary, Hayden Munro, left in no doubt that the PM wants to be a transformational figure. The conference ‘narrative’ these advisers were urged to push includes the telling line: ‘This Labour government will bring forward the biggest change to Britain in 40 years, with a new political and economic model.’ On the specifics, Burnham will pledge: ‘We will make the choices that are needed to end rough sleeping, solve the social care crisis and get more young people into work.’

Burnham will pledge to run a ‘cost-of-living government’ with further ‘immediate action’ to ‘provide the breathing space people need’, as well as outline plans to devolve power ‘out of Whitehall’, ‘reindustrialise Britain’ and take back public control of ‘the essentials in life – such as energy, water and housing’ to ‘make them affordable again’.

The article is headlined “Is the prime minister planning to call an election?” Like everyone else, Shipman does not know for sure, but he leans towards probably not. This is his conclusion.

Ultimately, the election choice is Burnham’s. ‘If you knew what Keir’s aides thought, you knew what he would do,’ says a ministerial aide. ‘But Andy has political opinions of his own.’ Burnham may go for it. But those closest to him doubt it. ‘He likes risk, but he has stuff he wants to get done,’ says one close confidant. New vocational GCSEs will be just as rigorous as existing ones, Powell says

Richard Adams is the Guardian’s education editor.

Lucy Powell, the education secretary for England, has defended her plans to add vocational courses to the subjects available for pupils taking GCSEs, saying that schools could choose to offer the new courses based on local interest.

The government has announced that the range of GCSEs in England will be expanded to include more vocational subjects such as business management and building and construction, starting from September 2029.

Powell told BBC Breakfast:

What I imagine is that some schools will offer some of these subjects where their local labour market demands, and this is where our mayors and our local leaders are really helping to shape that in their area.

So I wouldn’t expect a school to offer all of these, as they don’t offer all GCSEs at the moment. They will specialise in particular areas.

The Conservatives claimed that adding vocational subjects would dilute GCSEs, with shadow education secretary Laura Trott arguing it was “sabotaging young people’s futures” by creating “easier routes” for some pupils.

But Powell told Times Radio that the vocational courses would be just as rigorous as existing GCSEs. She said:

If you’re trying to do engineering or construction, for example, you know that involves a lot of problem solving, a lot of maths, a lot of thinking, as well as the hands-on skills that’s required.

So it’s not dumbing down, it’s the opposite of that, actually, because this is about raising the standards and the rigour and regulating these qualifications.

Adverts will appear on national television, as well as on social media channels.

The Ministry of Justice (MoJ) is aiming to have a 21,000-strong magistracy by 2029, with the number having fallen to 12,500 in 2022.

Magistrates, made up of unpaid members of the public, hear around 95% of all criminal cases.

Under Labour reforms, their powers will be increased so they can hand down sentences of up to 18 months’ imprisonment, up from 12 months currently to deal with more cases.

Some 57% of magistrates are women, and 14% are from ethnic minority backgrounds.

The role requires volunteers to sit for a minimum of 13 days a year, which the MoJ says makes it compatible with work and family commitments.

Announcing the campaign, Alex Norris, the justice secretary, said:

Magistrates embody the very best of public service. They give their time, experience and judgment to make vital decisions that help ensure justice is delivered fairly in communities across the country.

I’m calling on the public to play their part – we need more people from all walks of life to step forward and become their local magistrate.

Lucy Powell cautious about Burnham's call to lift football alcohol ban, saying good behaviour could be 'at risk'

Andy Burnham has suggested he would like to lift the ban on drinking alcohol in the stands at football matches. He did not say the government would do this; he was stating what has been his personal view for a long time. In the past, prime ministers have worked on the basis that they can’t have a personal view on anything, because the moment they articulate it, it is taken as government policy. But Burnham has said that he wants MPs to have a bit more freedom to speak out without having to worry about the party line.

This means that cabinet colleagues don’t necessarily have to agree – and in an interview on BBC Radio 5 Live this morning Lucy Powell, the education secretary, suggested she had concerns about allowing drinking at football matches.

Asked about Burnham’s idea, she said:

I go to the football most weekends with my young son and 30 years ago that probably wouldn’t have been all that comfortable an environment for a mum and their child to go to, but things have changed significantly. And that’s why we’ve been able to reintroduce safe standing, for example, which is something that fans want.

So I think it’s a conversation we need to have to make sure that we keep the great atmosphere.

Powell said other sports, like cricket and rugby, allowed drinking without “bad behaviour” being a problem.

I think we need to do this in consort with fans and with football, so that we make sure we keep the really good atmosphere, the good behaviour, and we don’t put that at risk.

Tactical voting at next election could push Reform into fourth place, poll finds

There are two MRP polls out today. In addition to the YouGov one, commissioned by Sky News and the Times (see 8.59am), the Guardian is today publishing details of a Survation one. It says progressive voters could push Reform UK into fourth place and put Labour within touching distance of a majority if tactical voting is widespread at the next election.

In their report, Jessica Elgot and Ashley Kirk say:

Andy Burnham is beginning to attract a significant number of left-leaning voters back to Labour, motivated by a desire to stop Nigel Farage, the results suggest.

The poll of more than 8,000 voters, shared with the Guardian, suggests a widespread openness to voting tactically. It also shows the prime minister’s most successful electoral strategy would be to unite the centre and left bloc of voters, while the right remains split.

It would be a marked contrast to the tactics of Keir Starmer’s Labour before the 2024 general election, when the party tried to attract more conservative older voters. Such a tactic would be likely to unsettle some in Labour, given the same strategy was deployed for Ed Miliband’s failed bid in 2015, albeit before Reform’s arrival as a major electoral force.

The poll on the eve of Labour’s annual conference in Liverpool shows a significant shift of left-leaning voters towards Burnham’s Labour and will encourage those in the party who are thinking of gambling on an early election while Reform’s ratings are ebbing and while leftwing parties such as the Greens appear to be losing momentum.

Twice as many voters have returned to Labour from the left as from the right since April.

The report illustrates the potential impact of tactical voting and it contains this quote from Damian Lyons Lowe, Survation’s chief executive.

Estimating tactical voting is a complex consideration, but the mechanics here are simple. Four in 10 voters are currently backing a party that can’t finish in the top two where they live and are willing to vote tactically.

But when we ask whether they would lend their vote to the stronger local contender in a two-horse race, around half said yes. That gap – worth some 18 points of the national electorate – is an enormous untapped reservoir for parties looking to squeeze voters.

Should these voters act as they say, our model shows Reform falling from second place to fourth, losing around 90 seats, despite its own vote share actually going up. Reform-leaning Conservatives will consolidate too. Reform doesn’t collapse; it risks being outnumbered, seat by seat, by voters coordinating around whoever is best placed locally on the left and right.

Yesterday YouGov published separate polling showing that the number of people minded to vote tactically against Reform UK has risen significantly over the past year.

Labour likely to be biggest party in hung parliament, mega poll suggests

Good morning. Andy Burnham and Kemi Badenoch are both preparing for their party conferences (next week, and the following week, respectively), and this morning YouGov has released a new MRP poll with encouraging news for both of them. Polling is very good at revealing electoral trends, but a lot less good at predicting electoral outcomes, and wise politicians don’t treat them as gospel. But they also find polling impossible to ignore, not least because it shapes the landscape within which parties operate. A leader expecting to win the next election acts differently from one expecting to lose it.

And would Burnham win an election held now? Not exactly, according to the YouGov figures, but Labour would be the largest party. Here is YouGov’s summary of its results.

-Labour would be the largest party in a hung parliament, were an election held tomorrow, winning 241 seats in our model’s central estimates

-Such a result would be a net loss of 170 seats for Labour on the 2024 election, but is up from our MRP this time last year, which projected the party would be reduced to just 144 seats and showed Reform UK within touching distance of a majority

-Reform UK are now neck-and-neck with the Conservatives, with the parties respectively on 123 and 130 seats in our median projection

-Our central projection shows the Lib Dems on 71 seats, the SNP on 40, Plaid Cymru on 15, and the Greens on nine

-37% of constituencies are decided by margins of five points or less in our central estimate, with this rising to 67% of seats projected to be won by Reform UK

This is not a terrific result for Labour, but the party is in a much better position under Burnham than it was under Keir Starmer.

The YouGov figures suggest Badenoch has no chance of forming a government. But she may be even happier with these numbers than Burnham. A year ago polling suggested Nigel Farage was on course to be the next prime minister, and there was serious talk about the Conservative party being totally eclipsed by Reform UK. Not anymore. Today’s figures have the Tories narrowly ahead of Reform on the central estimate (the best guess – YouGov also has highest and lowest estimates for seat numbers). More importantly, the poll confirms that Farage’s hopes of forming a government are – for now at least – fading.

YouGov surveyed almost 17,000 people for the poll, between 23 August to 18 September.
