1,800-year-old Roman toddler’s grave found frozen in time — and it still had toys inside

2026-11-21 08:15

Andrea Margolis, Fox News

Published Nov. 21, 2026, 3:15 a.m. ET

See more of our coverage in your search results.

Archaeologists in Romania have unsealed an intact 1,800-year-old Roman child’s sarcophagus — finding clay toys, protective amulets and preserved flower garlands buried alongside the toddler’s remains.

The discovery was announced by the Museum of National History and Archaeology Constanța (MINAC) in late October, with additional details released in early November by Romania’s Ministry of Culture.

The find was made during archaeological excavations in the city’s Flămânda area, ahead of the construction of a private residential building.

On-site archaeologists Constantin Băjenaru and Cătălin Nopcea first spotted signs of an intact sarcophagus on Oct. 22, before unearthing it and moving it to a museum facility, according to a translated statement from the museum.

It wasn’t until a few days later, on Oct. 27, that conservators, archaeologists and restoration specialists at MINAC opened the stone sarcophagus — revealing a nail-free wooden coffin inside.

Officials say the grave dates to between the second and third centuries A.D., and was located near an important ancient road on the northern edge of the ancient Tomis necropolis.

In its statement, Romania’s Ministry of Culture said the child was estimated to be between 1 and 3 years old at the time of death.

“The skeleton, measuring approximately 63 centimeters in length, appears to have been preserved in good condition, and numerous locks of hair were also discovered in the cranial area,” ministry officials said in a translated release.

“The way the tomb was sealed contributed to the preservation of materials that, under ordinary conditions, degrade over time,” ministry officials said, noting the limestone lid was secured with lead clamps and waterproof mortar.

Inside the burial, archaeologists found clay toys, flower garlands, and bronze jewelry and amulets.

Photos from the excavation show the remains of the toys: a clay rooster and a small horse on wheels, both bearing traces of paint.

Other items inside the burial included a bone button, two bronze bracelets, a piece of gold foil engraved with magical symbols and a pair of bronze bullae — protective amulets Roman children wore to ward off evil spirits.

Archaeologists also recovered fragile organic remains from the tomb, including plant stems, folded bundles of plants and fabric strung onto a wooden support and a piece of cord that may have been used to pull the wheeled toy horse.

“These are details that transform an archaeological discovery into an encounter with the everyday life of people in antiquity,” ministry officials said.

“Behind the funerary objects emerges the image of a childhood cut short too soon and of those who wished to preserve a part of it.”

While further testing could shed light on the burial rituals and materials used, officials said the child’s identity — and the story behind their early death — remain lost to history.

“We do not know who the child’s parents were, what the child’s name was or what the cause of death was,” the ministry said.

“However, we know — thanks to the objects discovered — that the burial was accompanied by gestures of love and a very carefully prepared ritual.”

Looking ahead, MINAC officials are partnering with specialized restoration labs in Bucovina and Iași to preserve the fragile textiles and wooden coffin.

“Although we are still at the beginning of the documentation process, we consider this discovery to be an important one, first and foremost due to the intact preservation of the sarcophagus,” museum officials said.

“The untouched preservation of the funerary inventory … offers valuable information regarding Roman-era funerary practices in Tomis.”
