Stream It Or Skip It: ‘Primetime’ Streaming on Prime Video, a Provocative Dramatization of ‘To Catch a Predator’, Featuring Another Weird Robert Pattinson Performance

2026-11-03 20:30

Primetime (now on VOD platforms like Prime Video) is prime viewing for fans of Weird Robert Pattinson. You know, the skewed RPattz similar to what we saw in The Lighthouse and Mickey 17. He plays TV journalist/personality Chris Hansen, who hosted mid-2000s reality series To Catch a Predator. The series stirred controversy with its ethically questionable method of luring pedophiles in front of cameras so they could be confronted by Hansen and subsequently arrested; the film is similarly controversial in its satirical fictionalization of Hansen, and the tragedy that resulted in To Catch a Predator’s ugly demise.

PRIMETIME: STREAM IT OR SKIP IT?

The Gist: About 15 minutes into the movie, we get what I call a Contextual Expositional Reminder. In this case, a speech by a TV exec asserting that Friends and Seinfeld are kaput, being replaced by reality shows. Sure, canned cream corn like The Bachelorette and sleazy slop like Temptation Island were rock-solid hits, but To Catch a Predator? Righteous, live-wire stuff. Host Chris Hansen (Pattinson) and producer Andie Bender (Merritt Wever) work in conjunction with cops and activist org Perverted Justice — led by Xavier Von Erck (Matthew Maher), who exposes pedophiles by posing as minors online — to bust creeps before they harm children. Predator is big, and NBC wants it to be so big, they’re slotting it in direct competition with Lost.

And Chris brims with wide-eyed vigor: He’s gonna be in [INSERT MOVIE TITLE HERE]! Success finds him leaning into his larger-than-life, faux-authoritative celebrity persona these days, and the film depicts him as a guy with a sweaty fetish for busting people with far ickier fetishes. He also teeters on the edge of mighty egotism and debilitating insecurity — when a vending machine fails to deliver his snack, he calls the company to complain and introduces himself as “Chris Hansen from Dateline NBC,” as if that’ll get him his 65 cents back quicker. Add a layer of quasi-hypocrisy to his story as we get POV-stalker-cam footage as he cheats on his wife, then fields calls from The National Enquirer asking him to comment before they publicize his philandering.

Such is the tension quietly brewing beneath the broader drama occurring as the To Catch a Predator crew treks to Texas, lured by the hope of landing “a whale,” a big-time public figure Xavier might’ve hooked. Andie and Chris recently hired baby-faced 23-year-old aspiring actor Dan (Skyler Gisondo) to pose as a 13-year-old and improvise through interactions with pedos. Dan isn’t so sure about this until Chris gives him a pep talk: He’s soon to be famous as Decoy Dan, a “superhero” who takes down bad guys. Be grateful Dan is in this story, because everyone else in this movie is about as lovable as a rotting fish head in your bowl of Froot Loops.

What Movies Will It Remind You Of? Primetime doesn’t need to hew too closely to true events when 2025 documentary Predators already did that.

Performance Worth Watching: Wever is extraordinary as the TV producer who’s willing to pitch her integrity into a black hole for ratings. But this is Pattinson’s movie, and his admirably bizarre take on Hansen’s persona makes the film wildly entertaining.

Sex And Skin: Full frontal during a couple of those gross pedo-busting scenes.

Our Take: Funny and engaging as it can be, Primetime doesn’t go any deeper than the usual gray-area ethical and legal arguments that accompany To Catch a Predator, which raised questions about entrapment methods and the muddying of due process when law enforcement works in conjunction with ratings-driven media. Remember: no bust, no show. And the film layers in familiar representations of hypocrisy and the corruptive power of fame, whether you have it and want more like Chris, or desire it like Decoy Dan.

However, talented director Lance Oppenheim — who made extraordinary documentaries Some Kind of Heaven and Spermworld before co-helming You Can See Everything with Nathan Fielder — compensates for the screenplay’s warmed-over thematics by marrying grimy visual panache (period-specific digital graininess, a tube-TV-tight aspect ratio) with Pattinson’s over-the-top, he-made-some-choices performance. They find the sweet spot between tragedy and satire, striking a distinctively provocative, nervy, unnerving tone for a story that’s ultimately about desperation, about people trapped in the no-man’s-land between need and desire. The result is as discomfiting as it is funny.

Our Call: On top of that, Oppenheim lights Pattinson in a scene that makes him resemble one of the They Live aliens. I’m going to insist that was intentional, and you therefore should STREAM IT.

John Serba is a freelance film critic from Grand Rapids, Michigan. Werner Herzog hugged him once.
