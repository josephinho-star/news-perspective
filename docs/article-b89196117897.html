Ecuadorian fishermen sue Trump administration after surviving boat strike

2026-11-17 23:30

More than a dozen Ecuadorian fishermen have filed a lawsuit against the U.S. government after surviving a military drone attack against their boat in the Pacific Ocean earlier this year, claiming they were wrongfully targeted for drug trafficking.

The lawsuit, filed Monday by sixteen fishermen, alleges that two explosive-laden drones struck their fishing vessel on March 17 as the crew returned from a two-week fishing trip near the Galapagos Islands.

The explosions set the boat ablaze and caused it to sink, forcing the fishermen to jump into the ocean. Two crew members suffered severe burns, including one whose leg was burned to the bone, according to the 17-page complaint.

The Trump administration’s strikes on drug-trafficking vessels in the Pacific were part of operation Southern Spear, which Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said was created to quash “narco-terrorists” in the Western Hemisphere.

The U.S. forces have carried out 72 strikes against alleged drug-trafficking vessels that have killed at least 244 people since September 2025, according to a tally by The New York Times.

The fishermen say they had no ties to drug trafficking and that Ecuadorian naval authorities had boarded and inspected their vessel just hours before the attack, finding no drugs or evidence of criminal activity.

After escaping in emergency boats, the men approached a nearby vessel, where they say armed U.S. personnel restrained them with zip ties and placed plastic hoods over their heads. They allege that they were forced to stand barefoot on a hot deck for 24 hours without food and with little water.

They were subsequently transferred to El Salvador in what they described as “deplorable conditions” before returning to Ecuador later that month.

The lawsuit is challenging the legality of the Trump administration’s broader maritime campaign, arguing that Congress never authorized the strikes and that no armed conflict exists to justify the use of lethal force against suspected drug traffickers, particularly on March 17.

The U.S. military has not acknowledged carrying out the March attack. The Pentagon and Coast Guard previously denied involvement in the reported attacks.

“We have no knowledge of, nor were Department of War forces involved in, the incidents described in those reports,” a U.S. Southern Command spokesperson said in June.
