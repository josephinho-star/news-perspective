Robert Pattinson is the new James Bond. Can he become the hero the West needs?

2026-11-18 10:00

So, Amazon MGM Studios announced that it chose the British actor Robert Pattinson to be the new James Bond. This could be bigger than mere new films in the franchise, as Ian Fleming’s creation was never more important for the Western psyche than now — in this time of political division in America and a weak and troubled Europe, the West needs its myths and heroes.

But can Pattinson really be Bond?

Bond is an old-school ultimate man’s man. He dresses above occasions, is cool under pressure, is a lady’s man, a secret agent and spoiler of the plots of terrorist masterminds. At 40, Pattinson is the right age to play the long-running and, arguably, the most iconic man’s man in film history. Sean Connery was just 32 years old when his first Bond film, "Dr. No," came out. But Pattinson is no Connery.

Pattinson has the looks, almost, but he is more emblematic of a weak Timothy Dalton than the unforgettable Sean Connery or even of Roger Moore. Still, he is more of a Bond than Daniel Craig, an actor never stately or commanding enough to carry the role.

Disappointingly, like Craig, Pattinson has expressed anti-gun sentiments. In interviews promoting the 2014 film, "The Rover," (in which his character uses a gun), Pattinson said he was "quite anti-gun." But that was a long time ago and Pattinson has since been careful not to express political positions. This is good, as we don’t need more divisive political rhetoric from actors who are seemingly embarrassed by their roles.

Pattinson’s casting followed screen tests and competition from actors reportedly including Callum Turner, Josh O’Connor and Jack Lowden. Given those choices, it is not hard to see why Pattinson stood out, even though his roles in "Harry Potter" and the "Twilight" films were boyish, not Bond-like.

Sean Connery’s Bond set the standard of the tall, dark-haired, impeccably tailored agent who could effortlessly go from a tuxedo to a wetsuit. This character is supposed to be both suave and understated, and very few can carry both off at once. Bond’s lines are supposed to be dry and straightforward. He is never weak, though he can be captured and beaten.

The official teaser for the next 007 film to come does not include a single word, just Pattinson, gun in hand, emerging from blackness with the iconic Bond theme strumming. This tells us nothing, but it is on theme.

Pattinson shares key physical attributes with Connery — he is tall, has dark hair and an ability to look sharp in a tux. But can Pattinson look cool under pressure while emanating the danger of a lion in the tall grass?

I am hopeful, but previous roles never showed that Pattinson has the acting range or the manly bearing needed to pull this off.

In "The Batman" (2022), Pattinson showed he can carry himself in a larger-than-life role, and he had the poise to make the part believable, but again the strength was not there. He is too weak-chested and never quite gives the impression he is as sure of himself as Bond must be — Frank Sinatra, for example, had this quality.

So, OK, there can never be another Connery; therefore the comparison is not quite fair. Then, can Pattinson carry the part forward into something this generation needs? So many today prefer to apologize for or ignore the Western canon that produced a fictional hero like Bond — a man willing and able to risk his life to save us.

The official teaser for the next 007 film to come does not include a single word, just Pattinson, gun in hand, emerging from blackness with the iconic Bond theme strumming. This tells us nothing, but it is on theme.

James Bond, at his best, is sophisticated, but hardly a snob. He has what the Spanish call duende, a characteristic author James Michener said is almost indefinable, as it means something with taste, refinement, beauty, perfection and elegance all in just the right proportion and with no showiness at all. He is what the Japanese mean when they use the word shibui, which is something a samurai tried to embody, but only could manage in fleeting moments when life and art meet before again separating with a bad gesture or misstep.

In other words, James Bond is an archetype of what men wish they could be in the moments they are needed. Cool under pressure, not afraid to act or to fail and confident they are fighting for something good. This, again, is not something Hollywood has given us enough of today.

Maybe, just maybe, if Pattinson is given the right script and if Hollywood does not try to apologize for his part, 007 could again be what Odysseus was once to the Greeks. He could be a hero representing an ideal showing the way again to the well-rounded man of action.

Perhaps he can do it. The English, in particular, need Bond to be a clean hero again standing strong against those who would imperil their civilization.
