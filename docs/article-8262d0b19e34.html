Texas attorney general-elect refuses to defend Ten Commandments classroom law

2026-11-28 10:32

Texas Attorney General-elect Nathan Johnson said this week that he won't defend a law requiring public schools across the state to display the Ten Commandments as the U.S. Supreme Court weighs whether to review the case.

In an interview with The Dallas Morning News, Johnson, the first Democrat to be elected Texas attorney general in nearly three decades, called the law "unconstitutional," marking a distinct split with outgoing Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton, who championed the bill.

"My jurisprudential opinion is that the law is unconstitutional," Johnson said.

"The court will have the ultimate say as to what's constitutional and what is not, but I'm the one who says what the state's position is, and it will not be the position the state has previously taken," he continued.

Under state law, Senate Bill 10 requires that a "conspicuous" copy of the Ten Commandments be displayed in every classroom in every public school and open-enrollment charter school across Texas.

In April, the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that the law did not violate the Constitution, a major victory for proponents of the legislation, including religious freedom advocates, who have long argued the biblical text is a crucial component of America's legal, moral and historical heritage.

However, the law may still face legal scrutiny if the Supreme Court opts to review the case.

Johnson's opposition to the law has already become part of Paxton's legal strategy, according to The Dallas Morning News. In a filing with the high court earlier this month, attorneys from Paxton's office cited Johnson's reluctance to back the law as a reason the justices should decline to hear a challenge to it.

In the filing, Paxton argued that Johnson's office might not adequately defend the law, depriving the justices of "an adversary presentation" if they agree to hear the case, the newspaper reported.

The federal appeals court ruling upholding the Ten Commandments law would remain in place if the Supreme Court declines to hear the case, preserving a legal victory for Paxton before he leaves office in January.

Johnson's victory in Texas was part of a series of Democratic electoral victories across the Lone Star State, including wins in the U.S. Senate and gubernatorial races.

His refusal to defend the Ten Commandments law, which was passed by Texas' Republican-led Legislature, sets up a potential political clash early in his tenure.

Fox News Digital's Louis Casiano contributed to this report.
