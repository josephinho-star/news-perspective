US supreme court blocks Trump administration from changing mail ballot rules for midterm elections – live

2026-09-15 00:04

Decision allows states to continue sending out mail ballots without meeting new requirements the USPS tried to impose through executive order from Donald Trump

US supreme court blocks Trump administration from changing mail ballot rules for midterm elections

The US supreme court on Monday denied a request from the Trump administration to implement new US Postal Service restrictions on mail voting in the 2026 midterm elections, which has already started.

The decision allows states to continue sending out mail ballots without meeting new requirements the USPS tried to impose to comply with an executive order from Donald Trump who insists without evidence that vote-by-mail is riddled with fraud.

The application for the high court to lift a lower court order blocking the implementation of the new postal service rule was denied by the court with just two noted dissents, from the conservative justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas.

“The Government is unlikely to succeed on the merits of its challenge to the District Court’s preliminary injunction,” the brief, unsigned ruling stated. “And the equitable factors applicable for obtaining emergency relief from this Court do not favor a stay.”

Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote in a concurrence that he agreed that the restrictions should not go into effect for the midterms but indicated he might rule in favor of the Trump administration later.

California congressman Robert Garcia, the senior Democrat on the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, welcomed the US supreme court’s decision to block the US Postal Service from enforcing a rule to restrict the delivery of mail ballots in the midterm elections, in line with Donald Trump’s wishes.

“The Supreme Court has done the right thing by protecting the American people’s right to vote,” Garcia said in a statement. “Donald Trump tried to illegally interfere with our elections and undermine vote-by-mail for millions of Americans and failed. We will keep fighting to protect the right to vote of every single citizen in this country”.

“This decision is a big victory for voters this midterm election season,” Kristen Clarke, the NAACP general counsel said in a statement on the US supreme court ruling that blocks the US Postal Service from enforcing a rule to restrict the delivery of mail ballots in the midterm elections.

“The Supreme Court rightly rejected an effort that would have made it harder for millions of Americans to participate in our elections,” Clarke added. “Mail voting is safe, secure, and deeply rooted in our nation’s history. The Court’s decision preserves a critical pathway to the ballot box and reaffirms that our democracy works best when every eligible voter can participate. We will continue to use every tool available to support Americans in registering their voices and their ballots this midterm election cycle.”

Gavin Newsom tells CNN he won't run in 2028 if Kamala Harris does because 'the zen diagram on that is just pure crossover'

With a slip of the tongue reminiscent of Joe Biden, Gavin Newsom, California’s Democratic governor, told CNN that he would not run for president in 2028 if his fellow Californian, former vice-president Kamala Harris, decides to run again, since “I know what that means: I know her base of supporters, I know her friends, the zen diagram on that is just pure crossover”.

Newsom made the remark in part of an interview with Jake Tapper, the CNN host, conducted while the two men were fly-fishing at the governor’s in-laws’ ranch in Montana.

When Tapper pointed out that he could face two fellow Californians in the 2028 Democratic primary – Ro Khanna, the progressive congressman and Harris - the former vice-president, Newsom replied: “I don’t know if she runs, but we’ll see.”

After Tapper said, “I know she’s called around”, Newsom added: “But I wouldn’t run if she ran.”

Pressed to explain why, the governor suggested that the two would likely compete for the same supporters, and donors, and open the door for another candidate to win.

“First of all, electorally it’s a gift from God for everybody else. They’d enjoy the hell out of it. Mutual assured destruction. It services no greater good,” Newsom said.

The fact that Newsom clearly said “zen” and not “Venn” in video of the exchange posted online was hinted at in the CNN transcript of the conversation, which read: “I know what that means. I know her base of supporters, I know her friends, the (Venn) diagram on that is just pure crossover”.

While the Associated Press incorrectly reported that Newsom said “Venn diagram”, rightwing outlets highlighted and mocked the governor, who has been open about his dyslexia, for his slip. The Blaze, founded by the former Fox News host Glenn Beck, shared the video with the caption: “Gavin Newsom apparently doesn’t know what a Venn Diagram is”.

Newsom’s botched reference to a Venn diagram in relation to his own supporters and those of Harris might have been prompted by the former vice-president’s repeated references to her love of Venn diagrams in recent years, which was frequently mocked online by the Republican National Committee.

Mitch McConnell returns to Senate after long absence

Mitch McConnell, the 84-year-old former Senate majority leader from Kentucky, was seen in Washington for the first time in months on Monday, minutes before his office released a statement saying that he was back at work.

McConnell said in a statement that his months‑long absence for health reasons, which fueled rampant online speculation that he might be incapacitated, or worse, was over.

“Today, I’ll cast my first Senate vote since I took a bad fall back in June. I’m really looking forward to being back on the Senate floor and seeing my colleagues,” the statement began.

“My recovery has been a long and often frustrating process, and the lingering effects of childhood polio haven’t made it any easier. I’m still not quite back to 100%, but I’ve assured Leader Thune that, as I continue with physical therapy on the advice of my doctors, I will do my best to be present for tough votes when our Conference needs me,” he continued.

“Elaine and I are very grateful for all the continued well wishes from our fellow Kentuckians, and I’m glad to get back to more of business as usual on their behalf this month,” the statement added, with a reference to his wife, Elaine Chao, a former cabinet secretary in the first Trump administration whose visit to her native China during her husband’s illness had also been the focus of speculation online from McConnell’s critics on the left and the far-right.

Video of McConnell being moved from his Washington DC home in a wheelchair was posted on social media by one of his critics on the Maga right, the Kentucky influencer Nick Sortor, with the caustic comment: “This dude is in NO shape to be a US Senator.”

After Trump says he likes Flock surveillance cameras, Montana Senate candidate responds: 'Flock off, man'

One day after Donald Trump expressed support for AI-powered Flock surveillance cameras, “because of law enforcement”, Seth Bodnar, a former president of the University of Montana who is mounting a strong campaign for the US Senate as an independent candidate, responded to the president on social media with a video of himself saying, “Flock off, man,” before shooting one of the cameras.

“Are you tired of security cameras watching your every move?” Bodnar asks at the start of the campaign video that shows him loading a rifle and destroying two cameras. “Yeah, me too.”

“Now the whole thing isn’t just creepy as hell, it’s also part of a massive economy designed to buy and sell our personal data. Now, we’ve known for years that Big Tech follows us online, but now these Flock cameras are literally tracking our every move,” Bodnar continues. “And all this data needs a place to live, so that’s why they want to build data centers right in our backyards.”

"They are all getting rich, and we’re getting screwed, and nobody is holding them accountable,” the candidate, who is second to his Republican rival in a recent poll, says before taking aim at one of the cameras and blasting it.

Trump, who dodged a question about Flock cameras last month amid a bipartisan backlash, was asked on Sunday by Beni Harmony, a correspondent for the far-right network Real America’s Voice, if he agreed with law enforcement officers who support the network of over 100,000 cameras.

“I sort of like them because of that, because of law enforcement, but some people don’t, they think it’s an infringement,” Trump told Harmony, a former spokesperson for two far-right Republican congressmen, Paul Gosar and Matt Gaetz.

“They help catch a lot of criminals,” Harmony interjected.

“But I like them,” Trump concluded.

Harmony, who began her media career as a producer for Turning Point USA, achieved viral fame in right-wing media last year after she claimed that she had been suspended by a local news station in 2025 for an on-air tribute to Charlie Kirk after his death.

Johnson says he will meet with AI execs 'soon' alongside Trump

House speaker Mike Johnson told reporters on Capitol Hill that the White House will schedule a meeting with artificial intelligence executives and company CEOs “soon”.

“I think either end of this week or early next week. But wait and see,” he told reporters.

The speaker didn’t say whether Congress would try and rush through any legislation to curb AI development this week, before House lawmakers head out of Washington to continue campaigning ahead of the November midterms. However, Johnson confirmed that the Ratepayer Protection Act – a piece of legislation that would require tech companies to cover the costs associated with datacenters – will head to the House floor this week. The speaker said that he supported the bill.

On Sunday, Johnson said it was up to tech companies, not Congress, to guarantee the safety of their artificial intelligence products. However, on the Hill today, he seemed to say that the legislative branch could have “a role” moving forward, but did not want to act “irrationally or haphazardly”.

“The reflex of legislative bodies is to cover things up with red tape and hyper-regulation. If you do that, then you lose your edge, and that is a national security concern for the American people,” he said.
