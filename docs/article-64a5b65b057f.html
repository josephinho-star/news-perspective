'Russia wants more than Ukraine,' Germany's Merz warns, as he says Moscow is 'escalating on a massive scale'

In his wide-ranging budget speech, Merz talks about Germany “neglecting fundamental issues for far too long” on defence, infrastructure, demographic change, and the economy – and the need to address these issues urgently.

But the starkest part of his speech comes as he talks about the external situation – and particularly the threat from Russia - to explain why some decisions, particularly on defence, are needed.

“Russia knows exactly what it wants and Russia wants more than Ukraine,” he warns.

“Anyone who fails to grasp this either does not understand the world as it is, or is pursuing an entirely different agenda – one that stands in opposition to the freedom, openness, and liberal values of our own country.”

Merz warns that “Russia is escalating tensions in the Baltic region, in Poland, in the Black Sea, and even in the outer space.”

“It is escalating on a massive scale – including here within our own borders, even at the cost of human lives. That is the situation, ladies and gentlemen.

And that is why we, as Europeans, must—and will – set clear boundaries for Russia.”

And, on that note, we will be closing the blog for today.

Why are Spanish politicians saying so little about climate crisis before the election?

Beneath the blackened branches of a pine tree that rises high above a landscape of scorched hillsides and the odd charred and ruined house, two older men address the obvious. Four months after one of the summer’s many wildfires threatened to devour the hamlet of El Morro, an hour west of Madrid, local people are still counting the cost and awaiting compensation.

“Look at that house,” said Esteban Pérez, pointing to one of the properties that did not make it through July’s flames. “Everything inside is scorched, completely destroyed. You can still see the windows, but they’re gutted. The entire structure of the house next to mine had to be torn down, too. And they’ve given the owner nothing so far.”

His friend nods and offers a more laconic observation: “We’re still screwed here.”

The ruling socialist party – which describes the climate emergency as “one of the greatest challenges of our time” – has promised to “speed up a just ecological transition, boost renewable energy, move towards greater energy autonomy, and turn sustainability into a source of employment, innovation and prosperity”.

The conservative People’s party (PP) has vowed to “act from science and without dogmatism” when it comes to fighting climate change and pursuing decarbonisation.

Meanwhile the far-right Vox party, which could well form a coalition with the PP, has pledged to tackle “climate fanaticism” and to put an end to international agreements that “hinder our productive development and the prosperity of Spanish workers, families, businesses and industries”.

Even though Spain’s three biggest parties enthusiastically defend their very different climate policies on paper, some observers are unsurprised at their reluctance to place them centre-stage at rallies and in interviews.

EU 'strongly rejects' premise underpinning new US tariffs

The EU has hit back at Donald Trump’s plans for more tariffs which come almost a year after his previous “liberation day” levies were struck down by the US supreme court.

Washington’s proposed new tariffs on 14 major trading partners including China, Japan, South Korea and the EU to combat what it calls structural excess industrial capacity, reviving global trade tensions.

According to a statement by the US trade representative, “the EU’s unreasonable cars, policies and practices” include “industrial plans to expand manufacturing”; policies to “promote production an exports”, “sustained market access barriers” and “preferential lending” to target domestic sectors.

These increase the US economic security risks especially in cars, chemicals and pharmaceuticals, it says.

The current tariff plan is to impose a 15% levy on EU imports, pegging it at the same level agreed with the EU and Trump’s course last July.

The EU said it would study the findings, but added:

“At the same time we strongly reject the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity. To the contrary, Europe is among those most exposed to overcapacity,” it said a possible reference to imports flooding in from China.

“The EU and the US share a real concern about global overcapacity, much of it state-driven and concentrated in third countries. We regret the lack of joint action.”

US plans new tariffs against China, Japan, EU and other partners

Meanwhile, the Trump administration has proposed new tariffs on 14 major trading partners, including China, Japan and the European Union, according to reports.

The US Trade Representative’s office announced the duties after completing an eight-month Section 301 unfair trade practices probe into 16 trading partners, including close allies Japan, the EuropeanUnion and SouthKorea, Reuters reports.

The move is designed to clamp down on “structural excess industrial capacity”.

The new proposals largely stick to rates set with trade deals, US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told CNBC in an interview, adding that some may be “topped up” a few percentage points.

Poland wants Nato to shoot down Russian missiles flying towards its borders

Poland wants Nato allies to consider shooting down Russian drones and missiles on the Ukrainian side even before they cross the alliance’s borders, after three cruise missiles crashed in Poland last week amid a broader attack on Ukraine.

The discussion has resurfaced just days after three Russian cruise missiles breached Poland’s airspace and fell deep inside its territory last Friday.

The foreign minister, Radosław Sikorski, said Nato should “anticipate threats, and not only respond when someone gets hurt.”

“Ukraine keeps asking us to shoot down threatening objects over its territory. Shooting [missiles] down does not mean they simply vanish; the shot-down piece of junk still falls on the ground and can hurt someone or cause damage … So a missile or drone approaching our airspace already poses a direct threat to us,” he told Poland’s public broadcaster Polskie Radio this morning.

“I believe that shooting them down over Ukraine, at Ukraine’s request, would fall within the definition of self-defence,” he said.

Sikorski will represent Poland at next week’s meeting of Nato foreign ministers in Brussels. He declined to comment if the proposal came up during a meeting of Nato’s North Atlantic Council on Monday.

The defence minister, Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz,told RMF FM that “this discussion did not start now, it has been ongoing for a while,” but allied countries showed little “determination” to follow through on the proposals so far.

“I have not yet seen any readiness on the part of our allies – because this must be an allied response – to take such a decision. … I do see, perhaps, a greater willingness to discuss the matter, but I do not see any determination in this regard,” he said.

Kosiniak-Kamysz suggested on Wednesday that he was unhappy with the level of support given to Poland by Nato allies, particularly the US, after the incident, with no public condemnation of the incident by Donald Trump or his senior officials.

“It’s not the first time,” he said.

“All information is being passed on to the Pentagon on an ongoing basis, and a meeting was held at Nato headquarters with the ambassadors of all member states, including the United States… but, once again, as was also the case in July, there has not been the kind of reaction we would have expected.”

The minister urged Nato’s Mark Rutte to raise the security of the alliance’s eastern flank with the US president, Donald Trump, during their meeting at Mar-a-Lago later today.

“I would like Mark Rutte to speak on behalf of the countries on Nato’s eastern flank and explain the consequences we are facing as a result of Russia’s aggression against Ukraine. … I think it is necessary to reiterate this every time [he meets Trump],” he said.

Poland is hoping that, as part of a broader review of the US military presence in Europe, Washington will soon confirm plans to deploy up to 5,000 soldiers to a new permanent base in western Poland.

Three sites in western Poland are being actively considered for the base, following a series of meetings with senior US officials in recent months. Polish officials suggest a final decision should be made around 5 December.

The base, which would cost Poland around £3bn to build, would strengthen Poland’s defence amid growing tensions with Russia.

“Until it’s signed [by Trump], we can’t say for certain, but … as I’d put it today, it’s highly likely,” Kosiniak-Kamysz said.

There are currently about 6,000 US troops stationed in Poland, mostly on rotation and down from 10,000 last year, with about 200 permanently deployed at the missile defence base in Redzikowo, northern Poland.
