Syrian Kurdish leader reveals high-level contacts with Turkey

2026-09-10 14:43

Syrian Kurdish political leader Ilham Ahmed has revealed that Syrian Kurdish officials have kept high-level channels open with Turkey’s security establishment, despite Ankara’s public portrayal of Kurdish-led forces in Syria as terrorists.

Ahmed is a senior figure in the Syrian Democratic Council and has overseen foreign relations for the Kurdish-run administration in northeastern Syria.

Representatives of that administration and Turkish security officials have exchanged messages about developments in Syria for years, Ahmed said.

Syrian Kurdish officials have no channel with the Turkish Foreign Ministry and want direct talks instead of public exchanges, she added.

Ahmed described the level of the existing contacts as “fairly high.”

She made the disclosure in interviews with Banu Güven of İlke TV, Nezahat Doğan of the Yeni Yaşam news website and İrfan Aktan of the Spot Press Cooperative, published by Serbestiyet on Monday.

Ahmed called for direct political talks with Ankara and said she hoped to visit Turkey with former Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF) commander Mazloum Abdi.

She said they also wanted to meet with Abdullah Öcalan, the jailed leader of the Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK), on İmralı prison island.

She urged Turkey to remove her and other Syrian Kurdish figures from its terrorism lists, arguing that she had never taken up arms against the country.

Turkey is a direct party to the Kurdish struggle for recognition on both sides of its border with Syria, Ahmed said.

She also credited Ankara with helping start talks between the SDF and Damascus, secure a ceasefire and steer their disputes away from fighting.

Turkey will have a “determining influence” on Syria’s future, she added.

Öcalan helped stop an attack on Kurdish forces around the northern Syrian city of Manbij before the SDF signed a March 10 agreement with Damascus, Ahmed said.

Messages that reached Syrian Kurdish officials showed that Öcalan supported talks and the integration of Kurdish forces into Syrian state bodies, she added.

Her account suggests that Öcalan’s role extended beyond Turkey’s domestic peace process into talks over northern Syria.

The PKK, which launched an insurgency against Turkey in 1984, is designated as a terrorist organization by Turkey and its Western allies.

Ankara regards the SDF’s main Kurdish component, the People’s Protection Units (YPG), as an extension of the PKK.

The United States backed the SDF as its main local partner against the Islamic State in Iraq and the Levant (ISIL), putting the force at the center of a dispute with NATO ally Turkey.

Turkey launched several cross-border operations against the YPG and demanded that the group disarm and disband.

The SDF has now dissolved and its units have joined Syria’s army under agreements with Damascus, Ahmed said.

The move shifted the Kurdish movement from armed conflict to politics, with constitutional recognition and language rights now its main goals, she added.

Kurdish-language education will continue under the new system but will fall short of the official status sought by Kurdish representatives, Ahmed said.

Turkish citizens who fought ISIL with Kurdish forces will be covered by Turkey’s PKK process and may return once arrangements are made, Ahmed said.

That process began in 2024 and resulted in Öcalan’s call on the PKK in February 2025 to lay down its arms and disband, in an effort to end a conflict that has killed more than 40,000 people.

The PKK announced its decision to disband in May 2025, and the Turkish Parliament adopted a law to regulate PKK’s giving up of arms in August.
