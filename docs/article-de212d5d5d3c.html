Florida approves sweeping property-tax breaks — but New Yorkers planning a move face a catch

2026-11-04 18:36

Florida voters have approved sweeping property-tax breaks that could save homeowners thousands of dollars a year — but out-of-staters looking to cash in on the Sunshine State’s latest perk face a five-year catch.

Amendment 3 passed during the Tuesday midterms with roughly 62% of the vote, clearing the 60% threshold required to change Florida’s Constitution and paving the way for a major expansion of the state’s homestead exemption.

Beginning in 2027, Florida homeowners who qualify for the state’s homestead exemption will be able to shield the first $150,000 of their home’s assessed value from most local property taxes, up from $50,000 today. That tax-free portion will rise to $250,000 in 2028 and increase with inflation beginning in 2029.

Foe example, a homeowner whose property is assessed at $400,000 would generally pay applicable non-school property taxes on just $150,000 of that value in 2028, rather than $350,000 under the current exemption.

The savings could be substantial.

A previous Realtor.com analysis estimated that the median Florida homeowner could save roughly $1,800 annually by 2028, although the actual amount will depend on local tax rates and the home’s assessed value.

But there’s a catch for prospective transplants.

Anyone who is not a Florida resident by Dec. 31, 2026, will initially receive only the existing homestead exemption upon qualifying. The expanded benefit won’t kick in until their fifth year of homestead eligibility.

Florida has long attracted affluent Northeasterners, especially New Yorkers, with its lack of a state income tax and warm weather.

As The Post previously reported, wealthy New Yorkers helped fuel a dramatic surge in Palm Beach property prices, with the median listing price nearly doubling between September 2019 and January 2025.

In Florida’s luxury market, the amendment is already generating enthusiasm.

Miltiadis Kastanis, executive director of sales at Compass, told The Post that wealthy clients have been discussing the proposed tax changes for months, with some greeting Tuesday’s passage as another reason to consider moving south.

“My clients are texting it around the good things, saying, ‘Did you see this? Look at this, it’s great,'” Kastanis said.

Rather than discouraging wealthy buyers, the five-year waiting period could actually accelerate some relocation plans, Kastanis suggested.

“I think what they would do is just start the move sooner,” he said.

Many families must coordinate moves around businesses, school schedules and other commitments, meaning their relocation decisions are often made years in advance.

Kastanis said New York and California remain major sources of wealthy buyers, alongside Illinois and Washington, DC.

“In the high-end marketplace, buyers follow the tax trend,” he said.

Inquiries have recently picked up, Kastanis added, although that interest has not necessarily translated into completed transactions.

The latest changes come as South Florida continues to attract major financial institutions and their employees.

In January, The Post reported that Wells Fargo was moving its wealth-management headquarters to West Palm Beach, joining a growing roster of financial firms establishing a presence in the region.

Amendment 3 could also benefit owners of second homes and investment properties.

The measure cuts in half the amount by which local governments can increase a property’s assessed value each year, from 10% to 5%. That means if a vacation home or rental property is assessed at $500,000, its taxable assessment generally could rise by no more than $25,000 the following year, rather than $50,000 under the old rules.

The change covers vacation homes, rental properties, investment properties and commercial buildings, potentially slowing the growth of their property-tax bills even as real estate values climb.

The cap applies to assessed values, however, not tax rates, and assessments can reset after a property changes hands.
