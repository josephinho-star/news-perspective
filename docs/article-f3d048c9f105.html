Michael Marra and Ken Skates tell conference how they think Labour can recover in Scotland and Wales

Scotland and Wales both have recently acquired new Labour leaders. The Scottish Labour leader, Michael Marra, and the Welsh Labour leader, Ken Skates, are both taking over parties demoralised and diminished by the May elections results.

This is what Marra said in his speech about the lessons for Scottish Labour.

In Scotland, we have some difficult truths to face up to. May was the sixth Holyrood election in a row where we have lost votes and seats.

Scotland knows what we are against. We’re against an SNP government that has been in power too long. We’re against a Tory party that should never be in power again. And a Reform party that would take us and this country to a dark and dangerous place.

But for all Scotland knew what we are against, people didn’t know enough of what we were for. And it’s my job to change that.

Social justice. Good work, good housing, good public services. That’s what Labour is for.

The good life for working people, not struggling for a holiday once a year or a pint once a week. That’s what Labour is for.

In Scotland, Labour has been out of power at Holyrood since 2007. But until May Labour had always led the devolved government in Cardiff, and it had been the most powerful party in the country for 100 years. Skates told the conference Welsh Labour had to, in effect, start again from scratch. He said:

I want to speak honestly today about what happened to us in May. The result was shattering. There is no point dressing it up. After more than two decades in government, the people of Wales delivered a profound judgment on us. We heard it. I heard it.

There are some things which, when they break, you can pick up the pieces, glue them carefully back together, and put them on a shelf. May was not like that. The judgment was too fundamental.

Our task is not to reconstruct a Welsh Labour party that looks like the past. It is to build something new. Something confident. Something exciting.

Something for the future.

No more Ming-vase politics for Wales. There should be no steady as she goes for Labour. That is not what we were built to do. It is time to stop protecting what was, and start creating what could be.

Emily Dickinson wrote that hope is: “the thing with feathers— That perches in the soul— And sings the tune without the words— And never stops—at all—”. I like that. But hope isn’t about pretending the storm never happened. Hope is what survives it, and politics is what rebuilds after it.

Politics must go further than poetry. Hope needs substance. Hope needs purpose. And hope needs results. People need to feel progress in their daily lives, because once they stop feeling it, soundbites mean nothing. And this is the lesson we have heard and learned. Not grudgingly. Not partially. Completely.

Douglas Alexander accuses SNP government of failing to support Scottish defence industry

Douglas Alexander, the Scottish secretary, criticised the SNP government in Edinburgh for failing to support the defence industry in his speech to the conference. He said:

In March, this Labour government committed to invest £10m to support the Defence Technical College programme in Scotland. We asked and urged the Scottish government to match that funding so that Scottish workers would have the skills to benefit from the investments this government is making in defence.

Match funding from the Scottish government would mean that Scotland could have, not one, but two DTECs that would benefit young people in both the east and the west of our nation. And six months on? No mention of these colleges in their newly published Programme for Government and then late in the day - not a commitment but simply an offer to talk.

Talking is of course welcome, but the rest of the world isn’t waiting. Indeed, we’re at real risk of Scotland falling behind as DTECs move forward elsewhere across the UK. And this isn’t the first time that our government has had to urge action - and provide the leadership - our defence industries need.

Just last year, on this very stage, John Healey had to step in and provide the funding needed to save a specialist welding centre on the Clyde. All because the Scottish National party in its wisdom, decided that apparently there is something about welding metal to keep our country safe that is morally wrong.

Alexander was referring to what was then an SNP policy banning investment in the production of munitions. John Swinney, the first minister, subsequently reversed that policy.

Louise Haigh says utility companies will be told to use procurement to support British jobs

As first secretary of state, Louise Haigh is following in the footsteps of Barbara Castle, who was one of the first ministers to be given this title. Haigh started her speech to the conference this afternoon by praising her predecessor.

The last time I was here with you as your transport secretary, I spoke about my political hero - Barbara Castle.

A diminutive Yorkshire red head with a grit and determination that occasionally found herself in trouble.

Barbara once said “in politics, guts is all”.

I often think about that line.

What Barbara meant was that there’s no point being in politics if you’re not prepared to be brave.

I believe she was right then. I believe she is right today.

Haigh is in charge of the Cabinet Office and she used much of her speech to talk about procurement rules. She said changes introduced last month would ensure government spending protects British jobs. She said:

The chancellor John Healey and I have been very clear, if your business is bidding for government contracts to defend our country, then you will be judged on your commitment to backing high quality, British jobs.

And just in case Kemi Badenoch is watching - that’s what we mean when we say social security and national security go hand in hand. That’s the Labour way.

Haigh said the same approach would be applied to utility companies.

Because the big water companies, the energy giants - these are not free marketeers.

These are not Adam Smith’s merchants, in fierce competition to outdo one another on price or on quality.

These are monopolies with a vice-like grip on British people and British businesses.

So today I want to be clear that we expect private utility companies to use their own procurement to back British jobs and skills in manufacturing.

Haigh said she wanted more insourcing.

When existing contracts end in 18 months’ time we will begin bringing more than a thousand outsourced cleaning, catering and security jobs across Whitehall back in-house.

And that is just the start.

We will establish a dedicated Insourcing Unit in the Office of the prime minister and cabinet to work across departments, share best practice and accelerate the insourcing of thousands more jobs across Whitehall.

And she announced that the government is commissioning a new state-of-the-art marine research vessel.

ITV’s Robert Peston has an intriguing theory as to how Andy Burnham will fund his plans for an NHS-style social care service. He has posted about it on social media. Here is an extract.

I came away from Andy Burnham’s pre-conference interview thinking he may well promise to fund his new free system of care for the elderly (at least in part) by reforming the expensive triple lock guarantee for above-inflation increases in the state pension.

Here were the clues in his chat with the bbc.

First he said that the plan would not be implemented in this parliament. It would be in Labour’s next general-election manifesto. And his reason was that he would not break Starmer’s manifesto, which promised to keep the triple lock.

Second, he said everyone would contribute to the funding of social care, but that the funding mechanism would be different from that of the NHS. In other words he would not use general taxation to pay for his new national care service. And to state the obvious, if the rate of increase in the state pension becomes less generous - up-rating by inflation perhaps rather than the higher of inflation or earnings, with a floor of 2.5% - this would have an impact both on current pensioners and future ones. Third, he has been listening to what I and others have been querying, which is whether he is prepared to take tough decisions that upset and enrage important sections of the community, when he believes doing so is in the national interest. He made a point, in the context of how he would fund social care, in saying he would show his mettle, his backbone. Lucy Powell dismisses Nigel Farage as like 'modern-day love child of Enoch Powell and Margaret Thatcher'

At the Labour party conference last year Bridget Phillipson and Lucy Powell went head to head in a hustings for the Labour deputy leadership. Powell, who had been sacked from cabinet, was seen as the pro-Burnham candidate and her victory (by a margin of 54% to 46%) was seen as evidence that members were not happy with Keir Starmer’s leadership.

Today they were in effect head to head again, speaking on after the other on the main conference platform.

Phillipson went first, speaking in her capacity as Labour’s chair. (She is also minister for women and equalities.) She spoke about Labour’s achievements in office, such as the Employment Rights Act, the Renters’ Rights Act, and getting rid of the two-child benefit cap, and she attacked the Tories and Reform UK.

The next election won’t just be a choice between Labour and the right. It is bigger than that. It is a choice between hope and fear.

We’ve seen what Reform believe. We’ve seen how they want to rip up the laws that protect our rights, our lives, our families, our chances.

And this month, we’ve seen how they make policy. When the crypto billionaires pay up, they don’t hang about. They change the line …

As for the Tories, well I hardly know where to start. Racing through the gutter with Reform, from maternity pay to Muslims at prayer. But they still love their greatest hits, they really, really do.

Kemi Badenoch sacked her shadow chancellor [Mel Stride] for suggesting Liz Truss might, just might, have got things wrong. Replaced him with a man who defended Liz Truss’s budget as “the right plans” which “make our economy competitive”.

(Phillipson was wrong about the reason for Stride being sacked. Stride did give a speech disowning Liz Truss, but that was more than a year ago, and was not the reason for Stride being replaced.)

Lucy Powell was speaking in her capacity as deputy leader and as education secretary. As deputy leader, she also attacked the Tories and Reform UK. She said:

Nigel Farage wants us to all blame immigrants for our country’s ills. He wants a race to the bottom on workers’ rights and deregulation. Especially for his Billionaire crypto mates. He believes in trickle-down economics, that never actually trickles down.

Conference, he’s just a modern-day love child of Enoch Powell and Margaret Thatcher. And quite bizarrely, for someone who seems to hate foreigners so much, he really does love their money, doesn’t he?

And as for the Tories … well, they seem somewhere between an identity crisis and collective amnesia.

But we won’t forget - the British people won’t forget - the damage they did in 14 years of government. They left this country worse off, our public services hollowed out, and now they’ve got the nerve to lecture us on the crises they created.

Anti-Reform UK strategy 'simple' - just 'knocking on doors' and listening, says Labour's outgoing general secretary

Hollie Ridley gave a speech to the Labour conference in her capacity as the outgoing general secretary. She is being replaced by Joe Fortune, the former Co-operative party general secretary, who was backed for the job by Andy Burnham.

Ridley told the conference that there was a simple way to Reform UK. She said:

[At the 2024 election] we ripped up the rulebook and delivered the most efficient ground operation in the history of British politics. 411 seats. 33.7% of the vote. If that doesn’t demonstrate the power of organising, the power of knocking on doors, the power of getting into our communities and mobilising people, then I don’t know what does …

I’m often asked what our Reform strategy is. And the answer is simple, knocking on doors. That’s our Reform strategy. Getting into our communities, listening to people. Working together to improve things, day by day. That’s our Reform strategy. Showing people that we understand their concerns. That we share them.

And, most importantly, proving that politics can be a force for good in Britain once again. That is the strategy and it is the only one we need. Because winning isn’t rocket science, but it does take hard work. It takes discipline. It takes organisation. And it takes a ruthless focus on the goal of a second term. The same focus that allowed us to win in 2024 will be needed to win again.

Ridley also said it was her belief that “when the trade union movement and the Labour party work together in the interests of the country, there is nothing we cannot achieve.” Labour should never forget that, she said.

TUC leader Paul Nowak predicts government will 'change course' over proposed tougher residency rules for foreign care workers

Ben Quinn is a Guardian political correspondent.

Andy Burnham’s government “will change course” on Shabana Mahmood’s controversial migration reforms, the TUC general secretary has predicted as the plans came under attack at Labour’s annual conference.

Paul Nowak said politician of all types had a responsibility to be careful with their language as he appeared at an event which heard that people from BAME backgrounds sometimes felt sentiment seized on by the far right was being “legitimised” by some government policies

Proposals that would force more than 300,000 low-paid workers to wait 15 years for the legal right to remain in the UK have emerged as an ongoing point of friction in Labour at at time when the party has largely been unifying around a new leader. (See 11.35am.)

The plans were criticised by some attendees at a fringe conference event organised by the anti-fascist group, Hope not Hate, to discuss the role of communities in resisting racism and xenophobia.

Michaela Chen, a human rights lawyer at the Work Rights Centre - a charity with helps migrant workers - told the event some government policies appeared to be “legitimising” sentiment which a resurgent far right was seeking to build on as it targeted some communities.

“It’s wonderful that there is a sense of hope now, but also it’s hard to continue to be hopeful when there is also a betrayal,” she told the Guardian afterwards.

Nowak, who was one of the speakers, said “an atmosphere of fear” created by the far right was having an impact on migrant as well as black and ethnic minority workers across the UK. He said:

I think the language of politicians across the spectrum matters. On the government’s settlement reforms I hope the government change course. I think it will change course, I think it has to change course. Changing the rules for people who have come to live and work in this country is untenable.

Reform UK revives 'death tax' slogan to attack Burnham's plan for new levy to fund NHS-style free social care

When Labour, with Andy Burnham as health secretary, proposed social care reform in 2009 funded by a tax on estates, the Tories swiftly described the proposal as “a death tax” and the idea was probably doomed even before the 2010 election campaign began. Labour deployed similar tactics in 2017, when Theresa May published plans for social care reforms that would have raised costs for wealthier pensioners. Labour said it was a “dementia tax”, and May lost her majority.

Burnham said this morning that he hoped this time there would be a more responsible debate. (See 9.35am and 9.47am.)

Three hours after his interview, there does not seem to be much evidence that anything has changed. Reform UK has already accused him of proposing a “universal death tax”.

Robert Jenrick, the Reform UK Treasury spokesperson, said:

Andy Burnham has yet again refused to rule out his universal death tax. He’s too weak to cut the sky-high benefits bill, so he’s coming for your home instead.

It is an unfair, cruel levy on the people of this country, and it would make Britain the single-worst place in the Western world to hand down money to your kids.

Reform UK will fight Burnham’s universal death tax. Money that is taxed when it is earned, saved and spent should not be taxed a fourth time as you are laid to rest.

Jenrick is assuming that Burnham will propose an extra tax on inheritance. Burnham did make it clear this morning that there would have be some form of tax rise to fund his plan, but he would not give details, and he played down the idea it would just be a re-run of his 2009 plan. (See 9.47am.)

Chris Philp says Tories would oppose any new tax to fund better social care

Chris Philp, the shadow home secretary, was also on the BBC’s Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg. Responding to what Burnham said about social care, Philp said the Conservative party would not support having a new tax to fund better social care. He said:

This country is already taxed too highly. It’s already destroying jobs. It’s already making businesses go bankrupt. High taxes are already driving businesses and successful people overseas.

What I inferred from Andy Burnham’s interview just then is they do plan more tax rises.

And let me be categorical; the Conservative party will not support more tax rises if more money is needed, for example, for social care or indeed for defence. We would find that money through savings elsewhere, particularly in the welfare budget, which is far too high.
