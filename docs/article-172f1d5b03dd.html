Blood sugar spikes may be tied to everyday habits you can control, expert says

2026-10-27 11:00

Everyday habits — from what you eat for breakfast to how much you sleep — can have a significant impact on blood sugar levels, according to a metabolic health expert.

Maintaining healthy blood sugar levels is essential for energy, organ function and overall metabolic health, while frequent or prolonged spikes and drops can contribute to health complications.

Jim LaValle, a Texas-based clinical pharmacist and metabolic medicine expert, told Fox News Digital that understanding how food, exercise, sleep and stress affect blood sugar is key to keeping levels in check.

LaValle recommends monitoring blood sugar after meals, known as postprandial glucose, to better understand how different foods, beverages and eating habits affect blood sugar levels.

"Do I spike more from eating rice, or do I spike from eating quinoa? How much can I eat?" he said. "I think that's a foundational issue."

The metabolic health expert noted that insulin resistance occurs when cells become less responsive to insulin, a process that can be influenced by inflammation in the body.

Normally, insulin helps move glucose from the bloodstream into muscle cells, where it can be stored as glycogen for later use.

LaValle said he’s a "big believer" in eating a higher-protein breakfast rather than one that’s heavy in carbs, pointing to naturally elevated cortisol levels in the morning and their potential impact on blood sugar.

"You've got to watch how many sugars are in food, how many refined carbohydrates you're [eating]," he added.

One of the simplest lifestyle habits to help lower blood sugar, according to LaValle, is taking a 10-minute walk after eating. Regular exercise is an "obvious" healthy practice, he added, although he acknowledged that it's not easy for everyone.

"It’s easy to say, ‘eat better, exercise, sleep better,’ but then people are working 16-hour days or are single parents, they get home, they open up their pantry and destroy a bag of cookies because that's the first thing they can eat and their blood sugar’s plummeted," he said.

Even a little movement after eating can be beneficial, LaValle said, particularly in a modern world that’s "eliminating the need to move."

LaValle also emphasized the importance of managing stress, which can contribute to insulin resistance.

Along with healthy eating, exercise and stress management, getting adequate sleep is "huge for glucose regulation," he added.

"When you don't sleep and you don’t get enough rest to reset your body for the next day ... all those hormones that are supposed to be getting released get skewed," LaValle said, noting that insulin regulation is particularly affected by inadequate sleep.

LaValle noted that sleep apnea is often associated with conditions such as diabetes, prediabetes, high blood pressure and obesity, emphasizing the importance of healthy sleep habits.

Hyperarousal of the brain can contribute to insomnia, LaValle added.

For someone who’s "jacked up all day long," feeling overcommitted and anxious, that heightened state of stress can disrupt sleep and, in turn, interfere with glucose regulation.

Inadequate sleep can also affect appetite and eating behaviors, potentially making blood sugar management more difficult.

"Then you're going to start to put weight on and then, when you don't get enough sleep, you’ve got to eat more to fuel your brain," LaValle cautioned.

"So you eat more, you're not efficient at packaging that fuel up, you're moving less ... and those are the things that you have control over."
