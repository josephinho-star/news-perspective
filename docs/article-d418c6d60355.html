Mamdani’s rent control fairy tale is bad news for New York — but he keeps telling it

2026-11-23 22:35

Mayor Zohran Mamdani says critics of rent stabilization have it all wrong — and that the policy isn’t to blame for a large number of housing units languishing off the market.

But economics 101 has a clear message: tinkering with the free market always has unforeseen consequences. History teaches us, in the case of rent stabilization, that means landlords investing less and even pulling stabilized units off the market altogether.

Still, according to Mamdani, his critics’ concerns are simply “rumors,” and anyone who dares to ask questions about his housing policies is clearly in the pocket of big, bad landlords.

That’s the accusation the mayor levied at New York Times correspondent Andrew Ross Sorkin at his DealBook Summit on Wednesday, where he dared to ask Mamdani about the estimated 26,000 rent-stabilized units that are off the market — ostensibly because landlords have decided that it costs less to keep them empty than to invest in a property that can’t provide a return.

“This is housing stock that could be put back on the market very quickly, potentially, if the landlords were allowed to effectively pass on the costs of upgrading any work that needs to be done to get those apartments back on the market,” Sorkin said, to which Mamdani snapped, “Andrew, did a landlord write that question?”

The mayor then refuted the so-called “rumor” that rent stabilization policies can make units go off the market and remain vacant. According to his own calculations, 6,000 of those 26,000 apartments had been vacant for five years — which is hardly the win he thinks it is — and “a lot of the units that are being described [are] newly constructed… housing that’s actually just being brought online.”

He then took to X, where he said Sorkin’s claims were “not true” and “dangerous.” But what’s more dangerous is a mayor doubling down and threatening to expand a failing policy.

“The mayor’s comments are incomplete and not really looking at the full economic history,” Jeff Miron, an economist at Harvard and the Cato Institute, told The Post. “Just looking at the number of vacancies is incredibly incomplete. There are many ways the market will respond to rent control. The absence of vacancy doesn’t mean rent control is a good idea, or that it didn’t have any distorting effect.”

Everywhere America has tried rent control policies, it’s backfired. One landmark study out of San Francisco found that, when they expanded a rent control policy, affected landlords reduced the available housing supply by 15%, sometimes by converting rental units into condos or by redeveloping.

According to Miron, converted units like those wouldn’t be counted in vacancy numbers, but he warns there are all manner of other possible side effects of the government getting involved in the housing market.

“One possibility is that [landlords] do less maintenance,” he said. “Another is that they hold their units off the market until they think the rent-control regime might change to allow them to raise the rent.”

The math is pretty simple. Say you’re a landlord with a unit in need of repair or renovation. You’re willing to make the investment, but the city caps the rent you can charge in the unit to such a severe degree that they guarantee you’ll rent the place at a loss — or break even by the time the place needs to be renovated again. Why even bother? Better to use the space for something else, or leave it empty as is.

These sorts of policies have all sorts of ripple effects, too. For instance, some would-be landlords and developers might simply steer clear of a city whose policies antagonize them and take their investments elsewhere.

And yet, in spite of the evidence, Mamdani rolled into office promising to freeze the rent for all 1 million rent-stabilized apartments and to add 200,000 new rent-stabilized homes to the city over the coming decade.

If Mamdani were truly concerned with low-income New Yorkers’ ability to afford housing, he wouldn’t be punishing landlords; he’d be handing more housing vouchers directly to residents in need to use as they see fit.

“If your goal is to help low-income people, housing vouchers are relatively straightforward,” Miron said. “They’re explicitly targeted to the people you’re trying to help, and they avoid all the messy complications of rent control.”

Instead, Mamdani is continuing to defend the rent-stabilization regime that allows anyone, regardless of their income, to shortchange their landlords. It’s hard to come up with a system that would more randomly make winners and losers out of New Yorkers.

It’s hardly “man of the people” politics, but it’s just what you should expect from a privileged guy who only gave up his own rent-stabilized pad for Gracie Mansion. He’s more concerned with the interests of resentful and disgruntled elites like himself than normal New Yorkers just trying to get by.

As Miron put it: “A lot of voters are not low-income. Most voters are in the middle. If you focus only on means-tested stuff, most people say, ‘Well, that’s not going to help me.’”
