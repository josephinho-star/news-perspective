Why Mamdani’s move to close infamous NYC jail could be 'massive hit' that reverberates for years: expert

2026-11-28 10:00

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani (D) announced that Rikers Island would be closed "once and for all" by 2032, a promise one crime expert said doesn't add up and could release violent criminals back onto the streets.

Mamdani pledged to replace the city's largest jail with four smaller ones across different boroughs, while corrections officers and criminal justice scholars have raised concerns that shrinking the population of more than 6,000 inmates could carry lasting consequences.

Rafael Mangual, head of research for policing and public safety at the Manhattan Institute, told Fox News Digital there won't be enough beds to accommodate a city of nearly 9 million people.

"If New York City gets this wrong, make no mistake, there will be a massive hit to public safety," Mangual said.

The replacement jails in Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens and the Bronx will have a capacity of 4,160 beds, with an additional 340 therapeutic beds for inmates with mental illnesses and other medical needs. They will be placed closer to public transit and courthouses.

Mamdani said the decision to push the closure of Rikers back by five years was because of "prior administration delays." The city will also invest $146 million in support services.

The mayor’s office directed Fox News Digital to its Rikers Island closure plan and Mamdani’s remarks at a press conference last week, where he defended his position by saying, "We are making a conscious decision to advance a new model of a criminal legal system in our city, one that takes on that problem head-on."

Mangual maintained the plan could lead to significant overcrowding and trigger more "violence and other kinds of contraband problems."

"You can expect that the jails will continue to operate at 100% of capacity for as long as they exist if that project actually comes to fruition," he said.

He added that the city's jail population dropped below 4,000 in April 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, shootings and homicides spiked.

That year, there were 1,531 shootings, up 97% from 2019, and 462 murders, up from 319 in 2019, according to the New York City Police Department (NYPD).

"The experiment is not going to be a pretty one and that's the thing that I think people need to be most concerned with," he said, saying that Rikers had between 9,000 and 10,000 inmates during 2017 and 2018, which were some of the city's safest years.

Despite the city seeing record-low murders and shootings so far this year, according to NYPD data, Mangual said other major crimes, like felony assaults, remain high.

"That's a major problem that is only going to get worse," he said.

He said previous efforts to bring down the jail population have posed additional risks to public safety, pointing to the city's bail reform law and Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg's limits on low-level prosecutions that have made it easier to avoid jail.

"We certainly haven't been able to get it down to where it is now without compromising public safety," he said.

Mangual said repeat offenders like Alexander Florentino could now be put back on the streets. Florentino, a homeless illegal immigrant, was arrested this month on a murder charge in the death of a 62-year-old woman inside her Chinatown apartment one day after a judge released him without bail, according to the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) and court records.

Warning that reducing the jail population will only "erode the incapacitation benefits that incarceration provides," Mangual said the city will be left with no other solutions.

"The idea that there's an alternative to incarceration is one of the most misguided notions in criminal justice policy debates," he said.

Rikers has faced years of controversy, with many inmates and activists criticizing harsh conditions, trial delays and limited access to family members and support services. The city’s goal is "to build a safer and more humane jail system," according to a Nov. 20 press release.

But Mangual believes city officials still aren't looking at the full picture, arguing that building multiple jails with the same systems, inmates and guards will not solve the problem and that some criminals are "simply too dangerous to coexist with the rest of society."

"What they ought to have done was invest in refurbishing the jails that already existed, building new ones with adequate space, and then moving the inmate population around as required," he said.

If the plan moves forward, Mangual said the city could see crime surge on a "much larger scale."

"We're already seeing what that could possibly look like when you have individuals who are out walking the streets with 30, 40, 50, 100 prior arrests and they eventually kill somebody or shove somebody in front of a subway train," he said.

Fox News Digital reached out to Bragg's office for comment but did not receive a response before publication.
