Trump downplays outages and Nvidia security concerns in remarks before China summit

Speaking with Fox news, Donald Trump said he wasn’t worried about multiple outages this week affecting Microsoft, US banks, United Airlines computers, the National Security Agency’s internal comms network, Apple services and the national air traffic control system.

“They’re all individual events. These are big companies. They’re putting in new equipment and things happen when you put in new equipment. And these are all individual things,” he said.

When asked whether he raised the issue of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s affair with a woman suspected of being a spy for China with Xi, Trump said he “might’ve referred to it just briefly”

“Because it’s such a tragedy. It’s a tragedy. He’s such a brilliant man ... we have to take care of our geniuses,” Trump said of Huang.

Of Alice Huang, Jensen Huang’s affair partner, the president mused: “I haven’t heard that she was a spy. She was a business woman who bought chips. Who knows if they were bought right or wrong. But I can tell you we allow them to buy. We want to make money.”

However, according to a report by the New York Times, Trump was briefed aboard Air Force One in May en route to China that the US intelligence showed that that Nvidia’s chief executive was in a relationship with a woman thought to be a Chinese spy.

Trump also shrugged off the Fox reporter’s question about whether he would confront China over spying allegations.

“We spy on them too”, he said. “I think we spy better than they do.”

Donald Trump says he's open to deal making with Democrats

Donald Trump, who repeatedly prophesied an apocalyptic future if Democrats regained control of Congress, has changed his tune, telling Fox News the next two years will be “unbelievably productive.”

He suggested he would be liberal with his veto power, but also open to cutting deals across the party line.

“They’ll send me a bill on health care and I’ll say no way and I’ll veto it. And then, they’ll call me and we’ll sit down. That’s what I do,” Trump said on Wednesday. “Politics is a nasty game. They won.”

Donald Trump asked about one-on-one with Vladimir Putin amid the Russian-Ukraine war

Donald Trump will reportedly meet one-on-one with Vladimir Putin, the prime minister of Russia, during his Asia summit.

When asked by Fox News if he mentioned the bilateral meeting to Ukrainian president Volodymyr Zelenskyy, Trump appeared to sidestep the question.

“They got to get along,” Trump replied, referring to Zelenskyy and Putin, whose countries remain at war. “If I were president, if the election weren’t rigged, that war would’ve never happened,” he added, repeating a baseless election fraud theory.

Since February 2022, when Putin ordered the full-scale invasion of Ukraine by Russian forces, nearly 18,000 citizens have been killed and 58,000 injured, according to estimates from the United Nations.

Trump then offered a wildly inflated death toll for the conflict, claiming that “probably two million people, mostly soldiers, are dead.” In fact, more than 400,000 Russians have been killed fighting in Ukraine since Russia’s full-scale invasion, according to leaked data from the state statistics agency, Rosstat. In July, the Center for Strategic and International Studies estimated that between 125,000 and 150,000 Ukrainian soldiers have been killed.

“It’s a shame. It’s so sad. It doesn’t affect us,” Trump told Fox News. He noted that the US has sold military equipment to the European Union, which provides it to Ukrainian forces, in contrast to what he framed as handouts from his predecessor Joe Biden’s administration. Trump repeated his false claim that “Biden gave $350m in cash away” to Ukraine. In fact, the US Congress provided about half that sum, $174bn, in arms to Ukraine, and the Biden administration gave Ukraine a $20 billion loan to be repaid with frozen Russian assets.

“I guess you could say, we make a lot of money with that. I don’t care. I don’t want that,” Trump said.

US admits ‘substantial’ change to extradition request translation for leftist activist

US authorities have admitted altering the translation of an extradition request for James “Fergie” Chambers from English into Spanish, attributing violent and illegal actions to the wealthy activist and philanthropist that were not in the original.

Chambers’s case is the first known instance of the US seeking to extradite a citizen over alleged support for Hamas, an accusation apparently tied to an international money-laundering charge in the criminal complaint underpinning the extradition request and donations to organizations helping Palestinians in Gaza.

In a notification sent to Spain’s ministry of justice, the US Department of Justice (DoJ) cites three of the examples of legally significant alterations revealed in the Guardian’s reporting on the issue, calling them “substantial [and] inadvertent”.

The notification arrived to defense attorneys, the prosecutor and a three-judge panel on Tuesday as the judges were deliberating whether to grant the US extradition request, leading the defense team to file a motion calling for a halt in the proceedings and a new translation.

The US has now admitted to three of the alterations detailed in the Guardian’s reporting, and calls the fourth “an acceptable translation”. One of them involves introducing the word “fire” in describing late 2023 protests against Elbit, an Israeli weapons manufacturer. Two of the three charges in the criminal complaint against Chambers are related to the protests – where nothing was set on fire.

Construction on Trump's triumphal arch continues to be barred

A federal judge has extended the construction ban on Trump’s triumphal arch, in the latest setback for the president’s capital makeover agenda.

The Memorial Circle-slated arch was conceived as a visual ode to the nation’s 250th year of independence, among several other beautification projects, but its construction has been halted amid litigation.

In February, a trio of veterans and an architectural historian filed a legal challenge against the Trump administration, saying the arch would obstruct the symbolic view between the Arlington national cemetery and the Lincoln memorial, a line of sight meant to represent a unified nation post civil war, according to the complaint.

Members of the Kennedy family have since joined the legal action, arguing the arch would cast a shadow on former president John F. Kennedy and other relatives’s graves, which US district judge Tanya Chutkan cited in her extension.

The order is set to expire on 3 December, or before if the court rules on pending motions for a preliminary injunction.

Trump's transportation secretary blames contractor for nationwide ground stop

As legislators press for more answers on last week’s outage that caused thousands of flights to be halted, snarling travel plans nationwide and globally, US transportation secretary Sean Duffy has pointed a finger at contractor L3Harris.

Transportation officials have said the aerospace and defense company was updating copper wire lines to fiber in Arkansas, when a newly installed router flooded the network.

“This was not identified quick enough. It was not isolated quick enough,” Duffy said Wednesday on digital media outlet 2WAY’s The Morning Meeting. “We’re working on how to remedy that right now.”

A spokesperson for the government contractor could not be immediately reached for a response.

The official cause of the router malfunction remains unknown.

Duffy’s agency has been modernizing an outdated air traffic control system, switching copper lines to fiber and wireless, and analog systems to digital. Last Friday’s ground stop order was the second advisory of its type, since the 9/11 attacks.

In its wake, Duffy has also tore into Democratic lawmakers and previous administrations over funding to the aging system.

Jim O’Neill will not undergo a confirmation hearing on Wednesday to head the National Science Foundation (NSF) because he “did not do enough to stand up for science” in his previous position, said Bill Cassidy, a Republican from Louisiana who is chair of the Senate committee on health, education, labor and pensions.

This ends O’Neill’s run for the top US scientific organization, an embattled independent federal agency that has seen major budget cuts and grant freezes under the Trump administration. The next NSF nominee will face a Democratic-led Senate.

O’Neill served as acting director of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) from August 2025 to February 2026. In that role, he failed to push back against “irresponsible decisions and statements from Secretary Kennedy”, Cassidy said in opening remarks before the committee on Wednesday, hours before O’Neill was slated to appear.

“His role in the firing of Susan Monarez raised further concerns about the independent judgment he would bring to NSF,” Cassidy added, saying that several members of the committee had concerns about those lapses. Cassidy said that he likes O’Neill personally, “but this time requires leadership and fortitude in standing up for what is right”.

O’Neill also served as deputy secretary of the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) from June 2025 to February 2026, a time during which HHS slashed recommendations for routine childhood immunizations.

The White House did not pull O’Neill’s nomination. But Cassidy noted that the White House did yank the nomination of Heidi Overton for commissioner of the US Food and Drug Administration, saying she lacked the votes to pass confirmation – which Cassidy said was “pretty odd”.

“I think she would’ve had the votes,” Cassidy said. He noted that anti-abortion senators “sought reassurances from the administration” about Overton’s stances against abortion.

“They chose not to make such commitments and ultimately withdrew Dr Overton,” Cassidy said.

White House hits back at requests for information of officials and aides

Will Scharf, the White House general counsel, has hit back at Democratic lawmakers who have requested information and documents from administration officials, aides and allies in recent weeks, as the party prepares to take the majority of both chambers in the new year.

This comes after Robert Garcia, the incoming chair of the House oversight committee, sent 12 letters to members of Donald Trump’s family, current and former officials, and allies ordering them to preserve documents ahead of the sweeping inquiries that Democrats are set to launch in the new Congress.

Scharf addressed a letter, first obtained by Politico, to all ranking committee members in the House and Senate, claiming that several of their requests “drip with an animus or pettiness revealing that the purpose of these letters is not legitimate oversight but to harass and aggrandize”.

Your demands do not reflect a request of the House, Senate, or a committee to which that body’s oversight authority has been delegated. As a result, Ranking Member requests in the 119th Congress do not trigger any obligation to “accommodate[e]... the needs of the conflicting branches.” Although some of you may (or may not) become a Chair in the 120th Congress, that does not mean that requests from the 119th Congress carry over.

This, Scharf highlighted, “should not be surprising” to Democrats, noting the party took the “same position” in 2022, when they were relegated to the minority in the House.

In the majority, Democrats have vowed to launch sweeping investigations into a variety of issues and officials when they have subpoena power.

“The American people deserve to know: did the secretary of commerce seriously and perhaps irreversibly compromise US national security interests to make money for his adult children and Cantor Fitzgerald?” said Jamie Raskin of Maryland, Gregory Meeks of New York and Sydney Kamlager-Dove of California.

Cantor Fitzgerald insists it had “no role” in the Trump administration’s decisions about Nvidia.
