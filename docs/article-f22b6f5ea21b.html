Ankara’s COP31 message: electrify, recycle, don’t mention coal

2026-11-09 09:51

As the COP31 climate summit opened Monday in Antalya, the Turkish government put electrification at the center of its agenda, promoting renewable energy expansion and its Zero Waste initiative as ways to curb emissions.

But local environmental campaigners say Ankara is sidestepping the “elephant in the room” of the country’s continued reliance on coal while overlooking its role as a major destination for Europe’s plastic waste.

COP31 President Murat Kurum, who is also Turkey’s environment minister, is promoting a flagship initiative to make electricity account for 35 percent of global final energy demand by 2035.

The nonbinding “35 by 35” target is central to a 10-point action agenda that Ankara says will accelerate the shift to renewables and strengthen energy security.

“This year’s energy crisis has shocked us … everyone now understands that energy that you import is energy that you do not control. Electrification is the answer. It protects communities from volatile global markets and cuts emissions,” Kurum said Thursday ahead of the summit.

COP31’s aim is “to usher in the age of electricity,” he said.

Turkey has expanded its renewable energy infrastructure in recent years. Ahead of the summit, Energy Minister Alparslan Bayraktar outlined a plan requiring $500 billion in investment to electrify the economy and nearly quadruple installed wind and solar capacity to 175 gigawatts by 2040.

Yet about a third of the country’s electricity comes from coal, and Ankara has set no deadline for phasing it out. Plans to expand two existing coal-fired power plants alongside the electrification drive will make COP31 a test of the government’s climate leadership, campaigners say.

“What electrification should do is end the dependency on fossil fuels. That should be the ultimate goal,” said Emel Türker Alpay, an energy campaigner with Greenpeace Turkey.

She warned that the “35 by 35” target did not specify that electricity should come from clean sources.

“The most important thing would be a commitment to no new coal in Turkey and giving a date for starting a just transition away from fossil fuels,” she told Agence France-Presse.

Ankara has offered no timetable for an exit from coal, with Bayraktar linking the transition to an expansion of nuclear power.

The government is targeting the initial startup of the first reactor at Akkuyu, Turkey’s first nuclear power plant, by the end of 2026. Talks are continuing on two further nuclear plants.

The Turkish government holds the COP31 presidency, while Australia is leading negotiations. Both countries have emissions targets, but campaigners want clearer commitments to moving away from fossil fuels.

Although Ankara has set a net-zero goal of 2053, the independent Climate Action Tracker rates its climate targets and policies as “critically insufficient.”

The research group says the country’s pledges lack the ambition needed to drive emissions reductions and has urged Ankara to strengthen its targets for 2030 and 2035.

Yağız Eren Abanuş of the Center for Spatial Justice, one of 17 environmental organizations in the Turkish Climate Network, said the country’s targets needed a radical rethink.

“Turkey is not decreasing greenhouse gases but increasing them,” he told AFP.

Ankara’s 2030 pledge calls for a 41 percent reduction from projected emissions under a business-as-usual scenario, rather than from a historical emissions level. Its separate 2035 target also allows emissions to rise, according to Climate Action Tracker.

Ankara is also championing its Zero Waste initiative, a high-profile environmental diplomacy campaign led by Emine Erdoğan, wife of President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, as part of efforts to strengthen international cooperation on waste and methane emissions.

“When you look at the Action Agenda, they want to reduce projected global waste growth by 50 percent by 2035, which is a good thing,” Alpay said.

“But these policies are only focusing on managing waste after it is produced, and not preventing waste at its source. Without stronger commitments to waste prevention and methane reduction, it won’t deliver meaningful emission cuts,” she told AFP.

Turkey has not endorsed the Global Methane Pledge. It also continues to import plastic waste, notably from Europe, drawing criticism from environmental campaigners over pollution and the plastics industry’s dependence on fossil fuels.

“The plastics industry is the fossil fuel industry’s Trojan horse because when they cannot use the fuels they extracted for energy production, they redirect it into the petrochemical industry,” Alpay said.

“When we limit waste management to recycling, it doesn’t mean much for climate change or emissions.”
