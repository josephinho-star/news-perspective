Merz says ‘shock’ AfD victory in key German state ‘is an election result we cannot simply move past’ – Europe live

2026-09-07 14:51

Merz concedes 'most severe election defeat' in 'years, or decades'

Merz begins by saying “we are all deeply shocked” by the result in Saxony-Anchalt.

“We did not expect it to turn out this way,” he says.

He says the vote “will have repercussions” and says even a glance at who congratulated the AfD shows “the level of international attention this result has garnered.”

He says that “when nearly 60% of voters … cast their ballots for political parties that fundamentally call into question our country’s democratic institutions … it is an election result we cannot simply move past as if nothing happened.”

He concedes this is “the most severe election defeat the CDU has suffered in years, indeed in decades.”

EU's report to challenge conventional thinking on defence scrapped amid opposition from big member states

Meanwhile, an initiative from the EU’s top diplomat “to challenge the conventional thinking” on Europe’s defence has been scrapped, after opposition from big EU member states.

Kaja Kallas, the EU high representative for foreign affairs and security policy, had hoped to announce on Monday a high-level group of grandees - retired ministers and defence experts – to draft a report to bolster Europe’s defence policymaking.

But the plan has been definitively abandoned, following opposition led by Italy, France and Germany, who claimed to have not been consulted, EU sources told the Guardian.

Kallas announced the group ahead of a meeting of EU defence ministers last Tuesday where she said she wanted the group “to challenge the conventional thinking and look beyond doing more of the same”.

The group was meant to produce a ‘Draghi report for defence’, a reference to the widely lauded set of recommendations from the former Italian prime minister Mario Draghi, which is the unofficial bible of EU economic policy. Representatives from the UK and Ukraine were to be included.

But less than 24 hours before a big launch event in Brussels on Monday morning, the EU’s diplomatic service announced its cancellation.

“Some member states felt this was not the right path to pursue at this moment. After some discussion with ministers, we have decided not to proceed with the initiative.”

“The diagnosis has not changed: Europe needs to deliver faster on defence. We don’t want to find ourselves in a situation in a few years’ time, where we look back and find that Europe was so focused on where it needed to be in 2035 that it failed to do what was necessary in 2027.”

The last-minute cancellation caps a difficult period for Kallas, who has also faced a Franco-German proposal to dismantle the diplomatic service she leads and sweep some of its functions into the European Commission.

But the former Estonian prime minister is not giving up. Kallas is expected to raise the issue of Europe’s defence preparedness when EU ministers meet at the end of the month (28 September).

Why is far-right AfD’s state victory significant and what might happen next in German politics?

Back to Germany,the country is reeling from the biggest far-right victory in a state election since the Nazi period after voters in Saxony-Anhalt in the former communist east gave the anti-immigration, pro-Kremlin Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) party a thumping first-place finish of 44%, just short of an absolute majority.

Our Berlin correspondent Deborah Cole looks at some of the key questions in the aftermath of the vote.

“Coming in nearly 27 points ahead of the co-ruling Christian Democrats (CDU), the conservative party of the chancellor, Friedrich Merz, the AfD pulled off a coup whose aftershocks will ripple across Germany and beyond its borders.

The AfD will have broad powers over education, culture and domestic security if it manages to take power in the state. Among other policies, it has promised to start a remigration and deportation offensive, ban rainbow flags in schools, and halt the building of new windfarms. It now has a strong wind at its back for the two state elections yet to come this month. …

Its triumph also raised doubts whether the unpopular Merz could hold on in the face of such a clear rejection of his policies and leadership after just 16 months in power.”

Serbia's government asks president to dissolve parliament, call early elections

Meanwhile, we are getting more news from Serbia, where – just hours after Ratko Mladić’s controversial funeral – the country’s government asked president Aleksandar Vucić to dissolve the parliament and call a snap election.

The next parliamentary elections in Serbia are not due until December 2027, but there had been growing speculation in recent weeks that the government could push for an earlier vote in mid- to late-October.

Reuters noted that the move could allow Vucić, whose Serbian Progressive Party nominated him on Saturday to run for prime minister, to try to tighten his grip on Serbian politics after nearly two years of student-led protests that have presented the biggest challenge to his rule.

Vucić, who has run the country as president or prime minister for 12 years, has faced anti-corruption demonstrations triggered by the collapse of a concrete canopy at a railway station in the northern city of Novi Sad in November 2024 that killed 16 people, Reuters said.

The opposition and demonstrators accuse Vucić and his allies of violence against political opponents, rampant corruption, ties with organised crime and stifling media freedoms, all of which Vucić and his allies deny.

A recent poll by Faktor Plus for Blic has Vucić’s SNS on 47.2% of the vote, with the student list at 31.5% in the second place, and the Socialist Party of Serbia in third with 4.9%.

The vote is expected to be held on 25 October, local media reported.

Russia orders Germany to close St Petersburg consulate, Goethe Institutes in retaliation for Leipzig drone attack attribution

Meanwhile, as sort of expected (Europe Live, 1 September), Russia has just ordered the German consulate in St Petersburg to close down as part of its retaliation against Germany’s decision to formally blame Russia for the recent attempted drone attack at Leipzig airport.

In line with the Russian foreign ministry’s decision, the consulate needs to close down by 18 September – the same deadline that the German authorities have given to the Russian consulate in Bonn.

Germany’s cultural Goethe Institutes in Russia must close down and their staff leave by 13 September, it added.

“It was stated that it was the German authorities who once again provoked a new round of escalation in bilateral relations. All responsibility for the consequences lies entirely with the German government,” the Russian ministry said.

Back to German politics and the Saxony-Anhalt result, barring any defections (14:09), the only German party that has signalled readiness to work with the AfD is the populist left-wing BSW, Reuters noted.

The BSW, which narrowly cleared Germany’s 5% electoral threshold, shares the AfD’s hostility towards mass immigration, which it says undermines local workers’ rights, and also advocates for ending Germany’s support for Ukraine and restoring ties with Moscow.

Both parties characterise sanctions on Russia as needlessly self-sabotaging, denying German businesses access to Russia’s vast cheap energy resources on which Germany’s export-driven economy was able to thrive for decades.

German auto firm Volkswagen has said that one of its plants would be sold off and converted to make defence equipment after car production ends there, AFP reports.

VW’s Osnabrück plant, in northwest Germany, is to be sold to Tel Aviv-based investors Aurelius Capital and the German state of Lower Saxony, a major VW shareholder, with an initial air defence project planned for the Israeli firm Rafael Advanced Defence Systems.

EU's von der Leyen unveils €200m 'partnership package' for Greenland amid US interest

Meanwhile, EU’s Ursula von der Leyen, Greenland’s PM Jens-Frederik Nielsen and Denmark’s PM Mette Frederiksen are speaking in Nuuk, Greenland.

Their press conference is part of von der Leyen’s landmark visit to Greenland, during which she pledged €200m to invest in projects there focusing on energy, raw materials and connectivity there, AFP reported.

The new EU-Greenland Partnership Package is meant to strengthen the territory’s ties with the EU amid continuing interest from the US president, Donald Trump.

In a pointed remark, von der Leyen said the bloc “stands in full solidarity with Denmark and Greenland,” and Greenland’s future is only for the people of Greenland to decide.

Merz ends the presser with another defence of his time in the government, and saying he needs to get better at explaining his rationale and reforms to the public.

He concedes there is a problem, and he needs to address it.

Merz says he will not deviate 'even a millimeter' from pro-Ukraine course despite support for pro-Russian parties

Merz also gets asked about Germany’s position on Russia and Ukraine given the high support for pro-Russian parties in Saxony-Anhalt.

He says it was 57.7% of the vote last night.

He says resolutely that he will “not deviate by even a millimeter from my fundamental conviction that we must defend our freedom, especially now, from Russia.”

“I will likely have to explain this much more frequently and much more intensively,” he concedes.

But he says the threat to Germany’s internal security “is not abstract; it is very concrete” and that “if we stop supporting Ukraine, things will not get better, but worse.”

“The goal of the Russian leadership is to destabilise the value system of what we used to call the West. And that value system is not up for negotiation.”

In a more direct question, Merz gets asked at what point he would be prepared to accept his approach is failing and conclude that he can no longer serve as the chancellor.

Could the two other upcoming state elections be his breaking point?

He says he is “thinking hard how we can find our way back to shared political success.”

He says he is “not the sort of person that wears their emotions on their sleeve every day,” but “giving up is not an option.”

CDU's Saxony-Anhalt leader 'cannot see' defections to help AfD get majority

Merz also gets asked about apparent differences of opinion within the coalition government with the SPD.

He says “we need to talk” between partners, but lists what he focuses on what he says are some of this government’s success stories.

He says more needs to be done to remove obstacles to competitiveness and reduce bureaucracy.

Saxony-Anhalt’s Schulze gets asked if he can see some CDU politicians defecting to AfD to give the party a majority in the state parliament.

He says the AfD previously said it would not want to talk to other parties. But he says he cannot see any members of the CDU faction leaving to join AfD.
