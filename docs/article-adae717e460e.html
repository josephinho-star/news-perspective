Burnham makes Milburn head of unit to implement new jobs and welfare approach for young people – live

2026-11-17 15:38

Burnham gives Milburn job chairing Youth Opportunities Unit to implement new jobs/welfare settlement for young people

The government has announced that Alan Milburn has been appointed as chair of the new Youth Opportunities Unit mentioned by Pat McFadden earlier. (See 1.17pm.)

Alan Milburn has been appointed as the chair of the new Youth Opportunities Unit.

The new unit will sit at the centre of government, reporting directly to the prime minister, driving the cross-system changes needed to deliver greater opportunities and support for young people …

The responsibilities of the new unit are to:

-Shaping and delivering the government response: Shaping and delivering the cross-government response to the Milburn review, maintaining an ambitious cross-system approach to reform and linking closely to the government’s wider agenda on growth, re-industrialisation and devolution.

-Unblocking delivery and driving progress: Unblocking delivery obstacles and supporting secretaries of state to drive progress towards reducing Neet rates.

-Guarantee, service design and devolution: Driving delivery of the Youth Opportunity and Support Guarantee and the Youth Opportunities Service, partnering with Number 10 North, departments, mayors, strategic and local authorities.

-Mobilising partners: Galvanising action beyond central government, working with mayors, securing commitments from employers and voluntary and community organisations, and demonstrating progress.

-Data, metrics and insight: Drawing on data and insight to understand what is and is not working, and addressing gaps in, or barriers to, data sharing to aid delivery.

Political figures who are given non-ministerial government roles often find that, in practice, they have a lot less power than they would like. Government is organised around departments, and within departments the secretary of state normally has the final say.

But the wording of this press release implies that Milburn could end up with considerable authority. If he is in charge of “unblocking delivery obstacles”, that will only happen if he is allowed to apply a robust approach towards “supporting” secretaries of state. This appointment will be seen as confirmation that Andy Burnham is fully committed to implementing this agenda.

Funding tax cuts 'difficult', says McFadden, when asked about Milburn's national insurance proposals

In the Commons, Pat McFadden sidestepped a question from Oliver Dowden about whether the government would implement Alan Milburn’s recommendation for a cut in employer national insurance for young people. (See 2.06pm.) But, when the Labour MP Jonathan Brash asked if ministers would accept this proposals, McFadden gave a more direct reply. Arguing that tax was a matter for the chancellor, he said:

There is already a break in the system for employers with regard to employees under the age of 21.

I’m sure the chancellor listens to all representations about tax but he has the difficult job of netting off all these representations whenever there is a fiscal event.

This is what the Milburn report says about cutting national insurance.

While structural reductions in employment taxation cannot be delivered immediately, that should not prevent government from setting a clear direction towards lower payroll taxes. Rather than across the board, poorly targeted and unaffordable cuts in employer national insurance, I propose a different course: targeted support now, followed by a 5-year glide path to lower national insurance costs for workers aged 21 to 24. Businesses need confidence that government’s ambition is to reduce, not continually increase, the cost of employing a young person.

A start should be made with the 2 sectors which provide the most entry level jobs for young people: retail and hospitality. When fiscal conditions allow, the point at which employers in those sectors pay employer national insurance for 21 to 24 year-olds should double and rise progressively from the current £5,000 threshold to a new one of £10,000. Before the 2025 changes that threshold was £9,100. Unlike a full or partial employer national insurance exemption for 21 to 24 year-olds, increasing the threshold would have the biggest impact on the costs of recruiting low paid young workers and those who work fewer hours. It would especially help employers recruit young people doing ‘Saturday jobs’ as once the threshold reaches £10,000, a 21 year-old could work 15 hours a week at current minimum wage rates without the employer paying any employer national insurance. Over time the government should consider how to extend this new employer national insurance relief to other sectors in the economy, focusing on those with the biggest potential to create more entry level jobs for the young.

Corbyn says young people should not 'pay price for endless war', while Polanski says Milburn plans are 'flimsy sticking plaster'

In the Commons there was very little outright criticism of the Alan Milburn proposals from Labour MPs, even from the Socialist Campaign Group left. That does not mean there won’t be any rebellions when MPs have to vote on legislation next year. But, for the moment, the reception in the party is about as warm as Milburn and Andy Burnham could have hoped for.

Outside Labour, other leftwingers are criticising the plans strongly.

This is from Jeremy Corbyn, the former Labour leader who now leads Your Party in parliament.

I am very concerned by proposals to remove health-related and disability-related welfare payments from under-25s. Why are we forcing young people to choose between support and employment? They need - and deserve - both. These proposals amount to a sanctions regime, which removes the very support that young people need to get into work, and live secure, happy and healthy lives.

If the government wanted to help young people, it would recognise that the system is stacked against them, and tackle the crises holding back a generation: the housing emergency, the broken system of student debt, and the climate crisis jeopardising their futures. And it would create unionised, well-paid jobs in the sectors that benefit us all: green energy, social care and public education.

Milburn says that the proposed cuts will not be “pain-free”. This begs the question: painful for who? Our society is defined by grotesque inequality and corporate greed. Why are we targeting young sick and disabled people, and making them pay the price for a crisis they did not create?

We know why these cuts are being made: to fund yet more increases in military spending. Young people should not pay the price for endless war. Instead, we should be rebuilding public services, investing in social housing and protecting our environment. That is what real security means - both for young people, and for us all.

And this is from Zack Polanski, the Green party leader.

These proposals are a flimsy sticking plaster that will do precious little to heal the damage inflicted on young people by successive governments over their lifetimes.

Today’s young people are suffering some of the worst consequences of over a decade of economic rot, with a lack of meaningful job opportunities compounded by the sky-high cost of housing and essentials that puts dreams of living independently out of reach for millions.

This awful doom-loop will not be solved with handouts to big business to give low-wage jobs to young people in the absence of more meaningful career opportunities, with no plans to actually create new jobs.

Marie Tidball (Lab) said she particularly welcomed Milburn’s call for a target to close the employment gap for young disabled people. She asked how that would happen.

McFadden said he had already lifted travel restrictions on disability bus passes, with the transport secretary. And he said the DWP has set up an innovation fund to look at ideas that could close the employment gap for disabled people.

Richard Burgon, another Labour MP who was prominent in the backbench revolt over welfare cuts last year, said there was “some really welcome stuff” in the Milburn report. But he asked for an assurance that disabled and sick people would not be forced into poverty by the cuts to Pip and the health element of universal credit.

McFadden said the report was clear that “help will be there for those with both severe, long-lasting disabilities which make work not a realistic possibility, and also [for] those that will have extra costs”.

McFadden rejects Tory call to repeal Employment Rights Act measures relating to young people

Desmond Swayne (Con) asked if there were any measures in the Employment Rights Act affecting young people that the government would repeal to help young people find work.

We believe that young people should have rights. We believe they should have pay, and we believe that they should have opportunities. If the, idea of the party opposite is to take away those things and think that is the answer to the problem, I’m afraid I disagree.

The Conservative party is committed to repealing all aspects of the Employment Rights Act that it regards as “job-destroying”.

Lara Bird (SNP) said the Milburn report showed that young people had been “failed by a Labour government that has increased the job taxes and has overseen a catastrophic decline in vacancies”.

McFadden said he was not surprised “to hear the SNP reflecting Tory propaganda”.

McFadden ducks question about whether government backs plan to cut national insurance for younger workers

Oliver Dowden, the Tory former deputy PM, said the rise in employer national insurance and the Employment Rights Act made firms more reluctant to take on young people. He asked McFadden specifically about three recommendations in the Milburn report.

He quoted paragraph 267 in the report, which says the national insurance threshold changes have had “a profoundly negative impact on business sentiment”. Milburn proposed cutting employer national insurance for workers aged 21 to 24. (It does not apply to workers under the age of 21.)

Dowden also asked about paragraph 284 in the report suggesting raising the minimum wage rate for younger people to the full adult rate (a Labour commitment) should be slowed down.

And he also asked about paragraph 304, where Milburn quotes evidence saying “too much regulation [relating to employing young people] will deter employers from employing more young people by limiting employers’ ability to flex hours and conditions”.

On regulation, the Milburn report says:

I would propose that when considering the responses to the recent consultation that the goal should be to ensure that the potential impact on youth employment should be a primary consideration, and a cautionary principle should be adopted. I believe this would lead to an approach that aims to limit the potential negative impact on seasonal and temporary work and clearly balances the goal of reducing one-sided flexibility with retaining a dynamic and flexible labour market.

In response, McFadden did not comment on the national insurance questions. And he said the Low Pay Commission already takes into account the impact on employment when setting minimum wage rates.

Labour’s Rachael Maskell asked what the government would do to ensure employers did not discriminate against young people with severe anxiety or mental health problems.

McFadden said the government wanted to work with employers to ensure they could offer opportunities to young people.

(This was significant because of what wasn’t said. Maskell was one of the lead rebels when Labour MPs rejected welfare cuts last year. She did not fully welcome the Milburn plans, but her question was neutral, not critical.)

Esther McVey, a Conservative former work and pensions secretary, asked McFadden if he realised youth unemployment would rise when the government raised employer national insurance.

McFadden said McVey ignored the fact that Neet numbers rose by 250,000 when the Tories were in power between 2021 and 2024. And he said McVey had not mentioned today’s figures showing unemployment falling. (See 9.25am.)
