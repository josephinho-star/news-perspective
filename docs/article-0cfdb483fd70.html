Republicans must embrace early voting to win again

2026-11-15 17:00

Democrats sailed to a crushing victory in the midterm elections, partly on the strength of their under-discussed decisive advantage in early voting.

Democrats so far flipped eight Republican Senate seats in states including Texas, Kansas, South Carolina, Iowa, Ohio and Mississippi, and could potentially flip Alaska. In the House, Democrats flipped over 40 seats with a 13 point generic ballot advantage. In contrast, Republicans flipped one congressional seat in Maine. Governors’ results were similarly devastating to the Republicans.

An underdiscussed factor in the Republican’s recent electoral underperformance was conservatives’ refusal to vote early at the same rate as liberals. Registered Democrats accounted for roughly 45 percent of early ballots versus 30 percent for Republicans, almost identical to their margin of victory.

A good example of this outcome comes from Texas’ governor race. Third term Republican Gov. Greg Abbot received roughly the same number of votes in 2026 than in 2022. He was defeated by Democrat Gina Hinojosa, who received almost 1.7 million more votes in 2026 than 2022 Democratic nominee Beto O’Rourke.

A 2026 Abbot would have easily beaten much hyped 2022 O’Rourke, but Democrats benefited from surging turnout relative to the previous midterm. Hinojosa actually got more Texas votes during a midterm than Kamala Harris did in a presidential election. This turnout surge contributed more to the Democrats’ victories than swing votes.

If Republicans want to remain a viable political party, they simply must embrace early voting as a core turnout tool. It is no longer optional to prevent the Democrats’ next perfect storm.

The first rule of voter mobilization is to make it as easy as possible for your people to vote while spending as little as possible. Democrats play to win, while Republicans aren’t even really on the field. Democrats operate whole enterprises driving their voters to the polls during early voting, while most Republican turnout operations are effectively outsourced.

Early voting has a serious economy of scale advantage, allowing a political party’s turnout operations to get more bang for their buck.

It is simply more expensive per voter to hire 10 vans for a single day for 10 trips moving 100 people to the polls, rather than hiring just one van for the entire early voting period and using it 100 times over the course of that much greater length of time. This is vastly more efficient per dollar than relying on a single day surge.

If fully embraced, early voting could actually be more of an advantage for Republicans, because Republican-led states have higher rates of employment and Republicans are more likely to be active in a religious community, be married and have children. Liberals are more likely to be single, childless and states led by liberals have more people living in their parents’ basements and posting on social media as a kind of substitute.

Living a busy, productive life is a good thing and related to conservatives’ being happier, more satisfied, and having better mental health than liberals. But it also means that conservatives have less time to obsess over politics, and less time to vote out of competing life priorities.

On a personal note, I would have missed Virginia’s 2025 election due to a family flu if I hadn’t banked my vote early. The flexibility of early voting makes casting a ballot easier for people living full lives with changing family schedules, the kind of people who mostly vote Republican. I have also been personally told by many voters that they’ll vote on election day “as God intended,” so I’m well aware of the difficulty in altering this preference.

This isn’t the first time Republicans lost a winnable race due to failures adopting early voting. Nevada Democratic Sen. Catherine Cortez Masto narrowly won reelection in 2022 over Republican nominee Adam Laxalt likely due to a serious snowstorm. Laxalt even tweeted a “weather alert.”

This matters. One study found that for every inch of rainfall, turnout decreases by nearly 1 percent, and for every inch of snowfall it decreases by half a percent. Given how Republicans currently win a much larger percentage of the Election Day vote, it isn’t an exaggeration to say that storm cost Republicans a Senate seat. As long as Republicans rely heavily on Election Day turnout, their fate is literally at the mercy of the weather.

In 2024, Trump and the Republican National Committee more actively encouraged early voting, so our early vote numbers rose and narrowed Democrat’s early edge, resulting in electoral victory. Nothing on a similar scale was attempted in 2026.

When confronted with these facts, Republicans often express a desire to simply end early voting and return to Election Day instead of election month. If Republicans want to change that, they first need to win elections. Doing that without early voting is like bringing a knife to a gunfight.

Republican leaders need to adjust to that reality and signal that it’s wise for Republican voters to vote early. It will take many years and electoral cycles to dig out of the hole Republicans are now in. Best to get started today.

Andrew Follett conducts research analysis for a Washington nonprofit. area.
