In-N-Out CEO reveals simple secret behind burger chain’s cult following

2026-11-21 18:29

In-N-Out fans may be clamoring for a Double-Double in their hometown — but the burger chain’s CEO is perfectly fine making them wait.

In-N-Out chief Lynsi Snyder says the secret to the wildly popular California eatery’s cult following is keeping the restaurant from opening in every city that wants one.

“There’s that saying, ‘Keep it simple, stupid.’ I don’t want to veer from what my family started,” Snyder told David Novak on his “How Leaders Lead” podcast.

Snyder, 44, was just 27 when she took the reins at In-N-Out after the deaths of her father, Guy, and uncle, Rich, according to KTLA. She inherited a family business with two competing visions for its future: Her grandfather Harry and her father favored a cautious approach to expansion, while uncle wanted to move faster.

Snyder sided with her father and grandfather, a choice she says still guides the company even as its red-and-white signs pop up in states that once seemed out of reach.

“It’s funny because I think sometimes people’s perceptions — because we might be in a new market or a new state — they go, ‘Oh, they’re opening all over,’ and it’s like, ‘No, we’re really not,’” she said.

“It’s a pretty calculated growth rate, and there are times when we’re not hitting the amount of stores we planned on opening, and we’re okay with it.”

That patience goes back to a tiny burger stand in Baldwin Park, where Harry and Esther Snyder opened the first In-N-Out in 1948. When Harry died in 1976, the chain had just 18 restaurants, all in the Los Angeles area. It took until 1992 for In-N-Out to cross the state line and open in Las Vegas.

The family-owned company now has 445 restaurants across 10 states, including 289 in California, according to Inc. Tennessee is already serving up Double-Doubles, and the first three New Mexico locations are expected to open in Albuquerque in 2027.

Exactly where those burgers can go comes down to a surprisingly practical question. How far can a truck drive in a day?

In-N-Out uses fresh, never-frozen beef, so its restaurants need to be within a day’s drive of a distribution facility. That puts a limit on how quickly the company can fill in the map, even in places where customers are eager to line up.

That rule could leave East Coast fans waiting indefinitely.

“I don’t see us being on the East Coast in my lifetime,” Snyder said earlier this year, according to Inc. “We won’t compromise on quality just to expand.”

Snyder says that same thinking applies when ingredient prices climb. The company absorbs higher food costs, she said, and pays workers more because it expects them to deliver the kind of service that keeps customers coming back.

“We’re loyal to the customer,” Snyder told Novak. “We are not cutting corners. We’re not compromising.”

When the Tennessee expansion stirred fears that In-N-Out might be leaving California altogether, Snyder took to Instagram in March to reassure customers they weren’t moving the corporate headquarters from Baldwin Park.

She also credited fans who made the chain a success and pointed out that the company had been opening in other states since 1992 under the same standards.
