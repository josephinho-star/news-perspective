President of the European Central Bank Christine Lagarde to step down. Here's who is tipped to replace her

2026-10-29 11:59

European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde will step down on Feb. 7 next year, she announced Thursday, formally kicking off a succession race that will be shaped by political bargaining and France's looming fiscal crisis.

Lagarde has led the central bank since July 2019, steering its responses to the Covid-19 pandemic, the Russia-Ukraine war and a series of energy price shocks that sent inflation in the European Union above 11% in 2022.

She will depart nearly nine months before her term was due to expire in October 2027, while the process to appoint her successor will begin immediately.

Senior ECB figures are already in talks to fill other senior roles, with chief economist Philip Lane and executive board member Isabel Schnabel also due to step down next year.

The ECB is institutionally independent, and its Governing Council comprises the six-member Executive Board and the governors of the 21 euro-area national central banks, whose voting rights rotate.

But appointments to its top jobs are made by euro area governments, with negotiations generally reflecting political bargaining over national representation and influence.

Former head of the Netherlands central bank, Klaas Knot, and former head of Spain's central bank, Pablo Hernández de Cos, are widely regarded as top contenders to replace Lagarde.

"Both are excellent candidates," ING's global head of macro, Carsten Brzeski told CNBC by email on Thursday.

"Nationality will also matter. While Knot might find more support among Northern European governments, the question is whether De Cos will be supported by the current Spanish government."

"In any case, both would bring a high level of monetary policy expertise to the table. Something that Christine Lagarde had to pick up on when already in office."

Berenberg Chief Economist Holger Schmieding said that as well as the usual issues of monetary policy competence and regional balance, the ECB would need to consider who could deal with potential financial turbulence amid widening spreads in bond yields between member states.

Market participants are debating whether the ECB could be forced to use one of its intervention mechanisms as France faces a mounting sovereign debt crisis, with further uncertainty ahead due to next year's election.

"As a former chair of the G20's Financial Stability Board (FSB), Knot may have an edge in this respect," Schmieding said in a note.

"The European Union is trying to guard itself at least somewhat against the risk that right-wing Marine Le Pen may win the French presidential election in April/May 2027."

"However, the ECB has no mandate and no appetite to fund French fiscal follies. In our view, the ECB would only support France if Paris returns to more sustainable policies," he said.

The announcement of Lagarde's departure came ahead of the ECB's latest monetary policy decision and her usual press conference, due later on Thursday. The central bank is expected to hold interest rates despite inflation in the 21-nation euro zone hitting a three-year high in September.

Lagarde was given a standing ovation by the Governing Council of the central bank on Thursday.

"It has been a source of great pride to serve this institution. I thank all of those who have supported me on this journey over the past seven years," Lagarde said.

European Council President Antonio Costa said she had provided a "steady and determined stewardship, helping to safeguard stability and the resilience of the euro area.

Lagarde, born in Paris in 1956, first worked as a high-ranking lawyer before joining the French government. In 2007, she became the first female Finance Minister of a G7 country. She was appointed managing director of the International Monetary Fund in 2011, the first woman to hold the post, before joining the ECB.

She faced a blow to her leadership of the central bank in 2024, when a staff survey gave a heavily critical verdict on her tenure. The survey suggested many felt she spent too much time discussing topics unrelated to monetary policy.

Lagarde is viewed as a top candidate to become the next chair of the World Economic Forum, according to Financial Times reporting.

In recent media interviews, she has downplayed any suggestion she intends to run for the French presidency, which would come on a very tight timescale ahead of the first round vote in April.

One source told Reuters that Lagarde resigned early to give her more freedom to express opinions on political matters, which would allow her to throw her weight behind a candidate in the election. CNBC has not independently confirmed the report and has contacted Lagarde for comment.
