Venezuela's opposition leader Machado returns after months in exile, as doubts over democracy deepen

2026-10-25 18:26

Opposition leader and Nobel laureate María Corina Machado reappeared in Venezuela Sunday for the first time since December, returning from exile as doubts about the country's democratic future — and the role of the U.S. — grow.

It was her first appearance since the U.S. military deposed the country's then-President Nicolás Maduro in January, replacing him with an interim president who has exceeded the time she was authorized to serve, with no election scheduled.

Machado announced her arrival on social media, writing, "I am here. With you."

Another leading figure in the Venezuelan opposition, Edmundo González, wrote on X, confirming Machado's arrival and saying he would be at her side "very soon."

"Today is a very important day for Venezuelans. Our sovereign voice returns with her to lead and guide this process!" he wrote. "You know it, this is until the end."

On Sunday afternoon, Machado strode into a church in Lara state, west of Caracas, to the applause of dozens who gathered there, shook her hand, and hugged her, with cellphones aloft to record her return.

"María! María!" one person cried, as another shouted, "Welcome to Venezuela!"

"Long live freedom, damn it!" a man yelled.

One woman wiped away tears and called it "a miracle," while another's hands flew to her face as she saw Machado enter the church.

The crowd began chanting "Freedom!" as some supporters pumped their fists in the air.

Machado then left the church, heading to Caracas, with stops scheduled along the way as people reached into her car, one woman promising, "We're not going to leave your side!"

Asked by reporters Sunday about Machado's return to Venezuela, U.S. President Donald Trump said, "I don't know anything about it, but I think she's a very nice woman. She gave me her Nobel Prize. So how can I say anything else? And I hope she's safe. She's a very nice woman. But we're doing really well in Venezuela."

Since Maduro's ouster, acting President Delcy Rodríguez, a longtime Maduro ally, has run Venezuela with the Trump administration's blessing to succeed him.

Rodríguez was initially supposed to serve only 90 days as acting president, but has remained in office and has made no move to call an election.

Machado was briefly detained in January 2025, following a protest against Maduro's planned swearing-in ceremony, and went into hiding in Venezuela for 11 months. After a clandestine escape from the country, she reappeared in Norway that December, where she was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for her peaceful challenge to Maduro's authoritarian government.

Machado and her allies have been demanding a date for a presidential election in Venezuela, but U.S. officials have emphasized economic stabilization first.

In October 2023, Machado won an opposition primary and intended to challenge Maduro in the 2024 presidential election, but the government barred her from running for office.

González took her place on the ballot, and ample credible evidence showed that he defeated Maduro by more than a 2-to-1 margin. But the National Electoral Council, which is stacked with ruling-party loyalists, declared Maduro the winner.

The lead-up and aftermath of the election were marked by widespread government repression, including forced disappearances, torture, and other human rights violations. In the days after the vote, authorities detained more than 2,000 people, including poll workers.

In January, the U.S. military captured Maduro in Caracas and whisked him to New York to face drug-related charges, which he has denied. Many Venezuelans were stunned that the Trump administration chose to work with Rodríguez, rather than Machado, to try to revitalize the crisis-racked country.

Machado had repeatedly vowed to return to Venezuela and, according to her party, she made seven unsuccessful attempts.

One attempt would have seen her take a charter boat, but the operator canceled the trip, alleging the vessel was unfit for navigation. In another attempt, her team said, Machado tried to fly to the Caribbean island of Curacao, from where she could take a speedboat to Venezuela, but her plane was denied landing permits.

Opposition activists have accused Venezuela's government of blocking Machado's return, a claim denied by Venezuelan authorities. Others have also questioned whether the Trump administration has been involved in the efforts to stop her from returning.

Trump administration officials deny they are holding Machado back but have said they have, on some occasions, advised her against returning because of safety concerns.

After Maduro was deposed, Venezuela's highest court said Rodríguez could serve as acting president for up to 90 days—an interim appointment the National Assembly could extend by an additional 90 days. But the ruling party-controlled assembly never voted to extend her term.

Machado and her allies have demanded that the Rodríguez government set a date for a presidential election. In September, Rodríguez told the U.N. General Assembly that Venezuelans will determine the "right moment" for the next election.

U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio has argued that before holding new elections, Venezuela must stabilize its economy and rebuild its institutions, including its courts and electoral council. But at a news conference earlier this month, he said Machado has "every right to enter her country whenever she wants to return."

Machado supporters have held demonstrations in Caracas demanding her return.
