Inside LA’s weird underground comedy scene – where things get intimate

2026-09-13 23:14

This may seem strange in a city that has some of the most famous comedy clubs in the world.

Places like the Comedy Store and the Laugh Factory are fixtures of the Sunset Strip, symbols of our city as recognizable as palm trees and movie billboards.

But in recent years, some of the best shows I have seen were at dive bars, art galleries, and even bookstores.

And these performances may be changing the US comedic landscape, far beyond LA.

Sometimes the weirder the venue, the more the art comes first.

When I moved to Los Angeles nine years ago, the Comedy Store seemed like comedy mecca, its black walls inscribed with legendary names and the promise that someone famous might just “pop in.”

But being a global institution means catering to the masses. Go to the Store on a Friday or Saturday night and sometimes there is not much comedy happening. (Think: generic premises and hacky material, e.g., my nagging wife.)

Comedy lovers know to go on a Tuesday, when comics work out edgier material instead of playing to tourists or casual weekend crowds.

Here’s the secret: There are places in LA where every night is Tuesday.

One of them is Don’t Tell Comedy.

Don’t Tell hosts shows at alternative venues and does not announce the lineup or location until the day of the show.

So one night, I found myself driving east into that barren stretch of DTLA where you feel you’ll either get murdered or end up at a fashionable warehouse party.

I arrived at an art gallery filled with twenty- and thirty-somethings in streetwear and beat-up leather jackets. We settled into seats where the stage was barely higher than the audience.

At a traditional comedy club, you’re always aware of the architecture: You sit in the dark; the performer stands under a spotlight. Here, that hierarchy collapsed.

The comedians were talking to us as much as performing for us. Though we mostly weren’t speaking, it felt like a dialogue.

And here’s the magic: Not every moment had to be perfectly orchestrated. When the room feels less formal, failure does too. A comic can follow an odd thought farther, see how far they can push it.

It’s no surprise that this scene appeals to Gen Z.

COVID shut them out of nightlife during formative years, leaving them hungry for the in-person connection they missed. Raised on phones, they are also sick of being on them. They drink less than their elders and have less money to spend. A two-drink minimum is lighting money on fire if you don’t drink.

What they seek instead is authenticity, connection, idiosyncrasy.

A famous comic popping in is exciting. But experiencing something singular and real may matter more.

I may be a millennial, but I got the hype. Other great shows I’ve seen include Peacock Nights at the Tee Gee in Atwater Village, Jetpack Comedy at Bar Lubitsch in West Hollywood, and Good Heroin at Stories Bookstore in Echo Park.

There is even a show at Plan B, a Sawtelle strip club, called HaHas and TaTas. I aim to go as soon as possible. Ideally on a first date.

And while the experience matters more than the names, Bill Burr and Patton Oswalt recently popped into the Tee Gee.

Even famous comics see where things are headed.

After the show, I walked outside to the smell of chicken frying on fold-out tables along the sidewalk. Nobody seemed in a hurry to leave.

The comedians drifted outside and disappeared into the same loose knot of people who had been watching them minutes before.

None were famous then, but I followed a few comics and watched them later find success. I don’t think that’s unrelated to coming up in these rooms.

They inform the style itself: comedy that is more intimate, conversational and willing to be weird.

So as the entertainment capital of the world (at least until everything moves to Vancouver), Los Angeles comedy will hopefully continue spreading outward.

And it may become more human in the process.

Mary Dahm is a writer and medical technology publicist based in Los Angeles.
