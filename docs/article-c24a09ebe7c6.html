Treasury denies Healey plan to cut welfare bill before election, claiming his comments were misinterpreted – UK politics live

2026-10-29 11:29

Asked earlier if there would be a cut to the welfare bill before the next election, the chancellor said: ‘There will be a reduction in the benefits bill’

Treasury says Healey hasn't committed to cutting welfare bill before election, claiming interview answer misinterpreted

John Healey is rowing back from his answer in an interview in the Today programme implying he wanted welfare spending to go down before the next general election. According to Chris Mason, the BBC’s political editor, Treasury sources are saying his words have been over-interpreted.

This relates to what Healey said in an interview with Nick Robinson for the Today programme. It was broadcast this morning, but recorded yesterday, and the BBC has written it up in a story on its website headlined: “Healey promises reduction in welfare bill before next election”.

For the record, here is the key exchange.

Robinson: Let’s take welfare if we could. £19bn up in the year ahead. That’s the entire police budget for England and Wales. That’s the entire annual defence equipment budget. And yet in your speech you said, well, we’ve got a couple of reports coming next month, then we’ll have a white paper, which in non-Westminster jargon means a consultation, then you’ll have some legislation, then you’ll have a row in the Labour party. You’re not going to make any of these cuts before the next election, are you?

Healey: Well first, I’ve said clearly, the prime minister said as well, we have to bring the benefits bill down. You don’t do that by crude cuts, especially when people are feeling the cost of living pressures. We do have a big report, two big reports being produced next month, that will help us map out the way that we need to change the system for the long run. We are in fact reducing and have been over the last two years the rate of increase in the welfare bill compared to the previous period – I’m afraid to say – of a broken system the Conservatives put in place. We’re bringing back and have brought back face to face assessments which they axed for instance. We’ve been changing universal credits so people aren’t branding themselves as too sick to work in order to make that system work better.

Robinson: But you’re reducing the rate of increase in welfare spending chancellor aren’t you? But the OBR’s forecast is that £1 in every £8 will be spent on welfare by 2030 or thereabouts. You’ve got to make cuts, haven’t you?

Healey: This system is unsustainable. I’ve been clear about that. The prime minister’s been clear about it ….

Robinson: Briefly, because I want to give you the chance to talk about other things – there will be cuts in welfare before the election?

Healey:There will be there will be a reduction in the benefits bill. We’ll do that in a Labour way and we will do that because it is simply not good for Britain or progressive to have a bigger and bigger benefits bill and I feel a moral duty to deal with these youngsters who at the moment have been written off by previous governments.

The answer highlighted in bold is what gave the BBC its story. Healey paused slightly between the first “there will be” and the second “there will be” and it would be perfectly reasonable to interpret it as:

There will be. [Direct answer to Robinson’s question.] There will be a reduction in the benefits bill [etc etc – Healey expanding on the answer.]

But the Treasury is implying what he meant was:

There will be, there will be a reduction in the benefits bill [Healey simply repeating three words as he launches into more general answer, not answering Robinson directly, but just explaining the government’s approach.]

Reasonable people could interpret is either way. But Mason said the Treasury had told him the first interpretation was not accurate, and that if Healey really wanted to commit to getting cutting the benefits bill before the general election, he would have announced that in the budget.

Given that Alan Milburn himself has said that his plans to tackle youth unemployment may require more spending upfront in return for savings later, and given that welfare reforms often take most of a decade to roll out, it does seem unlikely that Healey intended to say the benefits bill would be cut before the election.

But, as Mason pointed out on the Today programme, “correcting” this story means the Treasury is implicitly saying benefit spending won’t go down before the election – which is very helpful to the Tories and Reform UK.

Healey accepts government faces 'hard decisions' ahead over spending

In his interviews this morning John Healey pushed back a bit against the claims that his plans are based on unrealistic assumptions about spending cuts. (See 11.29am.) Speaking to Bloomberg, he said “hard decisions” would be needed. He said:

My cabinet colleagues are demonstrating that they can also exert fiscal discipline and spending control, and we will take that into the spending review.

But the spending review will be framed by the priorities that we set in the 10-year plan for Britain, that’s as it should be.

Hard decisions about the top priorities for Britain in the years to come, judgments that then follow about how we fund them.

Healey's longterm government spending plans would require cuts government probably wouldn't accept, IFS says

Here are some more lines from Helen Miller’s presentation about the Institute for Fiscal Studies’ assessment of the budget. (See 10.45am.)

Miller said higher inflation means John Healey would need at least £17bn to avoid real-terms cuts in some government departments under the long-term spending plans. Echoing a point made by the Resolution Foundation (see 8.36am and 10.21am), she said:

The chancellor left plans for overall departmental spending across the rest of the parliament broadly unchanged in cash terms. In the face of higher inflation, this means that the real-terms value of budgets has been cut. Taken at face value, and alongside the inherited plans to slow down spending growth sharply in coming years, that is a big choice. It effectively implies that some public services will see real-terms cuts in next year’s spending review. Avoiding the cuts implied by both higher inflation and tight envelopes could plausibly require a top-up of £17bn in 2030-31. That’s true even before thinking about the £11bn needed to fund defence plans.

She said she did not believe Healey would implement the future spending cuts implied by his departmental spending plans. She said:

I want to be clear that my concern is not that it would be impossible to deliver the implied cuts to other services – that could be done. But I’m sceptical that this government would be willing to do it. I’m also sceptical because I didn’t hear John Healey starting the kind of pitch-rolling that you would expect if he intended to deliver these spending plans. And I’m sceptical because history advises scepticism. When chancellors pencil in tight spending plans in the future, they tend not to deliver.

She criticised Healey for raiding the reserves to fund the defence investment plan. She explained:

I’m grumpy about how the chancellor decided to fund the relatively small hole in the defence investment plan – that’s the plan that gets defence spending to 2.7% of GDP next year and keeps it there until 2029–30. Much of it came from further cuts to the capital budgets of other government departments. Fine. Priorities change. But the rest came from raiding reserves in future years. This is worth drawing out: John Healey has now spent 25% of the capital reserve set aside for the next three years. He’s also spent 40% of the day-to-day reserve. This is categorically not what the reserves are for. Drawing down reserves is another way that the government has reduced its buffer against future shocks.

She said it was “sad” that the government was having to decide who should get energy support based on council tax bands that are based on property values 35 years ago. She said:

It is also sad that, in the time since the last energy price surge, the government hasn’t bothered to invest in the data that would have allowed a package more targeted at those most badly hit by rising energy prices. But here we are.

She said Healey was at risk of needing to put up taxes next year. She said:

I am left with the impression that the chancellor is hoping the economic forecasts improve enough that he can make good on his plan to cut borrowing without having to also make large cuts to public spending or raise taxes. I hope that happens. And it might – a re-opening of the strait of Hormuz or AI-driven productivity growth really could create sunnier skies. But, equally, things could get worse. If they do, the chancellor now has less insurance.

A clear short-term risk with the chancellor’s choices is that next year – with low headroom and a spending review that looks pretty brutal for some departments – we see another season of damaging speculation about tax rises.

Healey's new 'founder's relief' tax cut for entrepreneurs 'looks bonkers', says IFS

Helen Miller, director of the Institute for Fiscal Studies thinktank, is giving a presentation summing up the IFS’s view of the budget. Arguing that it contained plenty of “bad things” as well as welcome measures, she singled out founders’ relief for particular criticism.

After years of arguing that business asset disposal relief was a bad idea – and just when I thought the relief was on its last legs – a new, similar relief has popped up. The new founders’ relief looks bonkers. It gives a lower rate of capital gains tax to a small subset of people with large gains. That will include some entrepreneurs that make investments with broader societal benefits – although most of that activity would have happened without the relief. But it will also include people that just got lucky. There are very complex rules around who gets it – rules that will incentivise people to change their behaviour in weird ways simply to be able to get the relief. I could go on. But the punchline is that this looks to me like yet another tax break for a chosen group with little economic reasoning behind it.

Tories overtake Reform UK in 'poll of polls' tracker

Since the start of 2025 Reform UK has been ahead of the Conservative party in the Politico poll of polls, a tracker which averages how the political parties are in surveys carried out by all the main polling organisations. Different polling companies use different methods, and so the poll of polls provides a reliable overview. Other poll of poll trackers are available, but Politico’s may be the most widely read in Westminster.

And today it has good news for the Conservatives. For the first time since early 2025, they have regained their lead over Reform UK.

How 2020s are set to be worst decade, by far, since 1960s for rising living standards, according to Resolution Foundation

The Resolution Foundation has now published its full 48-page report on the budget. Some of its main arguments were covered earlier. (See 8.36am.)

Here are five charts from the report illustrating five of its main arguments.

1) The amount the government is spending paying interest on its debt is “high and rising”, the report says. It says:

Debt interest spending is now forecast to reach £147bn in 2029-30, £13bn more than in March. This leaves debt interest spending at 4.1 per cent of GDP in 2029-30 (and peaking marginally higher at 4.14 per cent in 2031-32, the highest on record since 1956-57) and above the total expected spend on universal credit, Housing Benefit and Child Benefit combined in that year.

2) The 2020s are set to be the worst decade, by far, for more than half a century for rising living standards, the report says.

The 2020s are on course to have the lowest growth in incomes of any decade on record when measured using Real Household Disposable Income (RHDI). RHDI is projected to rise by just 1.4 per cent in total over the decade (equivalent to £400 in 2026-27 prices), one-tenth of the growth rate achieved in the 2010s (which itself was the second-worst decade on record). Income growth this decade is set to be much weaker than GDP growth because of rising taxes and import prices.

3) Labour’s cost of living support package benefits the rich almost as much as the poor, the report says.

Taken altogether, the Government’s £4.4bn cost of living package for 2025-26 and 2026-27 is poorly targeted. As Figure 8 makes clear, support is very widely spread. The Household Energy Payment goes to up to 20 million households in England, which are dispersed across the income distribution (87 per cent of the bottom half of the income distribution and 73 per cent of the top live in Council Tax bands A-D). The VAT cut and the Fuel Duty delay are not targeted at all. Taken together, we estimate that 52 per cent of the whole cost of living package will benefit the poorest half of the income distribution this year and next, with the other 48 per cent going to the top half (equivalent to an average £158 for poorer households, compared to £144 for richer households).

4) But, overall, the tax and benefit changes since the government took power have been progressive, the report says.

We estimate that annual incomes will, on average, be £320 higher for households in the bottom quintile in 2029- 30, compared to £2,000 lower for those in the top quintile as a result of the Starmer and Burnham governments’ measures. This is largely the result of policies announced before this budget: the repeal of the two-child limit and the commitment to uprate the universal credit standard allowance at a higher rate than inflation for four years, both introduced from April 2026.

5) All government spending departments apart from defence, health and education are likely to face cuts in spending per head from the end of this decade under current plans, the report says.

Healey denies postponing difficult decisions on welfare

John Healey has denied postponing difficult decisions on the welfare system, insisting the government is making changes “now”.

Thinktanks and opposition parties have made this claim. But, when this was put to Healey in an interview on GB News, he replied:

This was a broken system. And two years ago we started putting back in place face-to-face assessments. We’ve changed terms of universal credit, so people don’t have to brand themselves as too sick to work.

So, those are changes we’re making now. But in the long run, as I’ve argued, and the prime minister has said, too, we have to bring the cost of benefits down.

Healey accused by Resolution Foundation of ‘complete failure’ to target energy bill support at those most in need

Good morning. The day after the budget is often the moment when big initiatives announced by the chancellor the day before with a flourish start to unravel as experts pore over the small print. There is not much of that happening this morning – mainly because John Healey did not have any really big initiatives up his sleeve. Compared with a normal budget, his tax and spending initiatives were mostly relatively minor. That means that the opposition parties have been struggling to find things to attack, and so instead they have resorted to criticising Healey for what he did not do: slash welfare (the Tory/Reform UK solution), or join the EU’s single market (the Lib Dems’).

Healey has given interviews to all the main broadcasters, which are playing out this morning, but they were recorded last night and they don’t have any sensational new revelations. As Rowena Mason reports, one of the top lines was Healey saying he would cut welfare spending before the election.

But there were other lines in the interviews too. I will be posting all the highlights this morning.

The two main public spending thinktanks, the Institute for Fiscal Studies and the Resolution Foundation, are both publishing reports assessing the budget this morning. The RF has issued a news release with its main findings and its assessment of the overall economic impact of the budget measures highlights three problems.

It accuses Healey of a “complete failure” to target help with energy bills at those most in need. It says:

The poorest half of households will gain £158 on average from cost of living support this year and next, just £14 more than the £144 received by the richest half – highlighting a complete failure to target help at those who need it most.

It says long-term public spending plans imply a return to austerity. It says:

Tight spending plans beyond 2028-29 imply departmental budgets will take more of the strain in the future. But if the 3% defence target is met and real health spending continues to grow at 2.6%, pencilled-in spending totals imply that funding for 13 ‘unprotected’ departments will fall to just 4.7% of GDP by 2031-32, close to the post-austerity historic low.

It says people will get poorer between now and the end of the parliament. It says:

Median real annual disposable incomes for working age households are set to grow by a paltry £600 over the course of the parliament. Worse still, those gains are already behind us. Between now (2026-27) and the end of the parliament (2028-29), typical incomes are forecast to fall in total by £1,050 (3 per cent).

This is one reason why so many people continue to think Andy Burnham will call an election next spring (before the electorate feels the full misery of getting poorer) – despite Burnham yesterday saying he would not hold one within the next 12 months.

Here is the agenda for the day.

9am: Jonathan Haskel, chair of the Office for Budget Responsibility, and Ruth Curtice, head of the Resolution Foundation, speak at an event where the RF is presenting its budget analysis.

9.30am: Heidi Alexander, the transport secretary, takes questions in the Commons.

After 11.30am: MPs resume the budget debate in the Commons.

Lunchtime: Andy Burnham is on a visit to a Rolls-Royce factory in the Midlands where he is speaking to the media.
