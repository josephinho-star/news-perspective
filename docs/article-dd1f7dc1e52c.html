Far-right AfD wins historic victory in German state election

2026-09-06 20:22

The Alternative for Germany (AfD) surged into first place in state elections in Saxony-Anhalt on Sunday, exit polls showed, putting a far-right party on the cusp of power at state level for the first time since World War Two.

The AfD won 44.4% of the vote, with Chancellor Friedrich Merz's conservatives trailing at 18.3%, and the center-left Social Democrats and environmental Greens both clearing the minimum threshold to enter parliament, according to an exit poll from the ARD broadcaster.

As counting continued, it remained unclear whether the AfD had secured enough votes to win an absolute majority in parliament. But the election's wider political significance, which saw turnout over 80%, according to the ZDF broadcaster, was clear.

"We have made history in this country," said Ulrich Siegmund, the 35-year-old AfD lead candidate in Saxony-Anhalt, who drew thousands to his campaign rallies and who repeatedly cast victory in the state as a first step towards securing national power in federal elections due in 2029.

His party wants to heavily restrict immigration, restore ties with Russia, quit the euro and promote unashamed German patriotism.

The result marks a significant setback for Merz, whose personal approval rating has sunk to historic lows as voters have turned away from both his planned reforms to revive Germany's sluggish economy and his gaffe-prone communication style.

The AfD party's co-leader Alice Weidel hailed an "historic" result that gave the AfD a clear mandate to govern, but a protracted period of coalition building could be needed before a clear majority is formed.

The mainstream parties have all refused to work with the AfD, which the local office of Germany's domestic intelligence service has classified as right-wing extremist. But it may find a partner in the far-left BSW party, if it manages to clear the threshold to enter parliament.

Although Saxony-Anhalt, formerly part of Communist-run East Germany, is one of the smaller German states, with a population of just over 2 million, the result underlines the AfD's rapid advance since it was created in 2013.

In a fracturing political landscape, the AfD is now Germany's most popular party nationally, polling at 28% according to a recent INSA poll, comfortably ahead of the conservatives at 20%.

The party is particularly popular in eastern Germany. But it won around 20% in the western states of Baden-Wuerttemberg and Rhineland-Palatinate earlier in the year, underlining its growing national appeal.

While hard right parties have gained ground elsewhere in Europe, Germany's Nazi past has contributed to a reluctance among mainstream parties to cooperate with the AfD.

The AfD has dismissed accusations of extremism as scaremongering and an insult to the party's millions of supporters.

During the campaign, the AfD tapped into growing disillusionment with established parties, which it characterizes as tired and out of touch, offering a range of hardline policies and a nostalgic vision of cleaner, safer streets, and taking pride in being German.

Its program includes plans to push schools and cultural institutions towards more traditional values, curb immigration, and overhaul public broadcasting. It wants the children of refugees to be taught in segregated classes.

Rainbow flags in schools would be banned; instead, the German national flag would be hoisted every day. Students and staff would sing the national anthem at school celebrations. Other proposals include citizen patrols, more Russian instruction in schools, and a halt to building new wind farms.

Thorsten Frei, the faction chief of the conservatives in parliament, dismissed suggestions that Merz's position was under threat, though Merz's own approval rating is particularly weak.

Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil said voters were sending a signal to Berlin.

Polling on the ARD broadcaster showed that voters who had deserted Merz's conservatives felt the party had not delivered on its promises, while 87% of the state's voters overall were unhappy with the federal government.
