Buffer ETFs protect investors from the harshest market swings – for a price

2026-11-24 19:27

Stocks are limping to the finish line in 2026, but a series of exchange-traded funds are blunting the pain for investors – with a few trade-offs. Turbulence in the artificial intelligence trade has dragged the S & P 500 to a roughly 6% decline in the fourth quarter and the Nasdaq Composite to a nearly 12% slide in the period. Investors don't have to eat those losses, though. So-called buffer or defined outcome ETFs use a package of options to follow an index, absorb losses up to a certain amount and provide limited upside. Even in a positive – albeit very rocky – 2026, the funds have gathered $14.5 billion in inflows, as of Oct. 31, according to Morningstar. "These are all built around options. You're often buying a put or put spread and selling a call – you're capping upside is the takeaway there," said Dan Sotiroff, associate director, ETF and passive strategies research at Morningstar. The downside protection applies to a period that matches the life of the options, which is typically one year. For instance, a January series buffer ETF may provide 12 months of protection – if you buy it at the beginning of the year. "We have investors who are worried about inflation, worried about rising rates pulling down bond prices, stocks and bonds moving together – how can I think of the defensive part of the portfolio," said Graham Day, chief investment officer at Innovator. The firm rolled out the first defined outcome ETF in 2018. "Advisors are looking for new ways to add a defensive element while preserving the element of upside," he added. Holding up in rocky times The January series of the Innovator U.S. Equity Ultra Buffer ETF (UJAN) buffers losses from 5% to 35% over the outcome period while tracking the return of the SPDR S & P 500 ETF Trust (SPY) , subject to a cap. Year to date, the fund's total return is just shy of 5%, versus the S & P 500's 6.8% total return. It has an expense ratio of 0.79%. Below, UJAN has also managed to soften the blow from the roughest days for the S & P 500 in 2026, according to data from Morningstar Direct. "We found that they're performing as we would expect based on the way they're built," said Sotiroff about buffer ETFs. "You may have additional cost and frictions in there, but many have positive total returns [year to date]." Where buffer ETFs can fit Even as these strategies have held their own in a turbulent year, they're not a good fit for everyone. For starters, retirees and pre-retirees may find them compelling because they're willing to miss out on the sharpest gains in the market in return for some downside protection. "If the market is up 15%, and they keep 10% or 12%, they're happy with that," said Day of Innovator. "They want to make sure they have some certainty on that downside protection." He added that buffer strategies can complement investors' fixed income allocations, particularly at a time when bonds are seeing price volatility. A balanced portfolio that's split 60% toward equities and 40% in fixed income could become a 60/20/20 allocation – reflecting a 20% allocation in fixed income and 20% in a buffer ETF, Day added. But there are caveats for investors hoping to incorporate these funds into their portfolios or use them to replace bonds altogether. "In today's world, you have reasonable interest rates, and now it's a bit of a different calculus when you make that trade-off," said Sotiroff. "Are you willing to give up that interest income to get the downside protection?" Simpler may also be better for some investors, according to Alex Canellopoulos, CFA, CFP and director of wealth at Vista Capital Partners in Portland, Oregon. High-quality government bonds are the tools he uses to manage volatility. Long-dated bonds have been getting beat up as yields rise, but Canellopoulos focuses on a duration of three to seven years, which is less sensitive to fluctuations in rates. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions. "The yields we have now are reason for optimism going forward," he said. "You have a higher cushion that protects you against future rate increases." Key considerations Bear in mind these points if you're considering adding a buffer ETF to your lineup. Know the intricacies of your fund. To get the full benefit of the buffer, you need to buy the fund at the start of the protection period. You should also know the buffer's limitations. "Say you have a 10% buffer fund," said Sotiroff. "If you go through a dramatic drawdown like a 30% drop in the market, you're going to be exposed to the additional downside." Keep an eye on fees . These funds tend to be in the 60 basis point to 80 basis point range, said Sotiroff. Higher fees can take a bite out of your portfolio's returns over the long run. Understand your time horizon. Retirees and pre-retirees may be drawn to these funds because they're shying away from excessive equity risk, but they should consult with their financial advisors on what the implications may be for the rest of their portfolio. "If you have a long-term time horizon, you'd be better taking the risk with stocks and bonds," said Sotiroff. "These are good for people who have part of their portfolio they need to protect and want to do so in a very precise way."
