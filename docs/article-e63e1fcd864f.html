US loses measles elimination status after more than 25 years

2026-11-17 16:10

The U.S. on Tuesday officially lost its measles elimination status for the first time since it gained this distinction more than 25 years ago, with a count of more than 7,700 cases in the past two years and more than 10 deaths reported by states since 2025.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) made the announcement in a media briefing Tuesday, following a three-day meeting the global health organization held with the U.S. and Mexico over their respective measles elimination statuses with PAHO’s Measles and Rubella Elimination Regional Monitoring and Re-Verification Commission.

“The commission concluded that the endemic measles transmission has now been reestablished in Bolivia, Mexico and the United States, and recommended that their measles elimination status be withdrawn, a recommendation which I have accepted,” Jarbas Barbosa, PAHO’s director, said during the briefing.

PAHO officials acknowledged that with this loss of status, the U.S. is officially considered a country where measles is endemic. While PAHO does not recommend any changes to travel guidance with this loss of status, officials noted it does indicate a country should be dedicating resources toward combatting measles.

The Wall Street Journal reported last week, citing anonymous sources, that PAHO concluded the U.S. had lost its measles elimination status.

For infectious disease and public health experts, Tuesday’s announcement was a foregone conclusion. A country technically loses its elimination status after experiencing 12 months of sustained domestic transmission, which the U.S. recorded in January. And since then, the number of infections has only gone up, more than doubling last year’s count.

Since the start of 2025, states including Texas, New Mexico, New York, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin have reported measles-related deaths. For most states, these have been the first measles-associated deaths they’ve reported in decades.

PAHO had initially scheduled the meeting between the U.S. and Mexico for April, but it rescheduled to November to give the U.S. more time to complete viral genome sequencing.

While public health advocates have called this development a “tragedy,” administration officials have sought to downplay the significance of losing measles elimination status.

Ralph Abraham, a former Louisiana surgeon general who briefly served as the principal deputy director at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) earlier this year, said losing this status was “not really” a significant loss.

CDC Director Erica Schwartz has said that losing elimination status will not change the federal response to the measles outbreaks, which has been criticized for being tepid. Health leaders have repeatedly called for a robust measles vaccination campaign at the national level to combat the measles outbreaks across the country, while Trump administration officials have questioned the safety and efficacy of the vaccine or equivocated vaccine injuries with measles deaths.

Following the Tuesday announcement, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. wrote on social media, “We will stop measles outbreaks, protect American families, and work to regain U.S. measles elimination status. I continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles.”

He noted that childhood immunizations were disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic, pointing to other countries that have also lost their measles elimination statuses like Armenia, Austria and Uzbekistan.

“We are working to close vaccination gaps and rebuild public trust. We will answer families’ questions honestly about vaccine benefits and risks and work with trusted health care providers and community leaders to prevent measles, limit its spread, and support timely treatment,” he added.

Kennedy’s own response to the measles outbreaks has been marked by online spats with state leaders like Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro (D), who blamed the secretary and the rest of the Trump administration for worsening the conditions that led to the return of measles. Shapiro claimed that Kennedy went on a “tirade” when the governor urged him to encourage people to get vaccinated.

In her own statement on Tuesday, Schwartz said, “The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does.”

“We know where transmission is occurring and we’ve invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities,” she added. “Losing a designation doesn’t mean we abandon an evidence-based approach. It means we remain focused on stopping outbreaks.”

Any country that loses its elimination status must submit a corrective action plan to PAHO, outlining their proposed strategy for regaining elimination.

“Of course, we are expecting many, many in many countries to increase the capacities of surveillance so that the system can be sensitive enough to detect in a timely basis any new cases,” said Daniel Salas, executive manager of PAHO’s Comprehensive Immunization Special Program.

“But not just that, we need to have a rapid response for each case that is detected, and of course we need more social participation, more national coordination, interstate coordination, to have vaccines on a timely manner as well,” he added.

Salas said he expects the countries will present their corrective action plans to PAHO in the coming weeks.

One year after losing its elimination status, Canada’s measles outbreak was declared over in October, but this does not signify the immediate return of its status as a country where the disease is eliminated.

“The end of this multi-jurisdictional outbreak does not automatically restore Canada’s measles elimination status. Canada can regain this status once transmission of the measles strain associated with the outbreak has been interrupted for at least 12 consecutive months,” a spokesperson for the Public Health Agency of Canada said in a statement to The Hill.

Democratic lawmakers sharply criticized the Trump administration over the status loss.

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.), who is expected to be the upper chamber’s next majority leader when Democrats regain control of Congress in January, said during floor remarks Monday that it took Kennedy and President Trump “less than two years to undo decades of work into eliminating measles in America.”

“The resurgence of this dangerous virus is a direct consequence of the pathological stupidity plaguing this administration,” Schumer added.

Sen.-elect Annie Andrews (D-S.C.), a pediatrician, said Sunday that “no child should have to suffer from a disease we know how to prevent because of political hubris in Washington.”
