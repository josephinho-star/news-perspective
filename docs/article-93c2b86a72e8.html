A 15-year-old boy lost his hand as clashes with police around French schools continue

We are also getting some dramatic news from France, where the school protests continue for another day and hundred of schools are closed today.

A 15-year-old student reportedly had his hand blown off on Monday during a protest in Lens, as reported by AFP.

A police union source said the student in the northern town of Lens appeared to have picked up a teargas grenade fired by riot police, hoping to fling it back, but the device exploded in his hand.

Elsewhere, a 17-year-old was arrested after allegedly throwing a piece of stone at police officers, striking a passerby in the face and making him lose sight in one eye, Le Figaro reported.

The euro has fallen to its lowest level against the US dollar since May 2025 amid concerns over France’s burgeoning public debt and a continued sell-off in government bonds.

Monday’s trade saw the euro drop as much as 0.9%, after four straight weeks of falls against the dollar. 1 euro is currently worth US$1.12.

The yield on France’s 10-year government bonds – the benchmark used for fixed-rate mortgages, credit cards and other consumer loans- is now also nearing 5%, meaning the government is having to pay a significantly higher interest rate to borrow money. That multi-decade high will also affect consumer loans.

Spain’s snap election called this morning too is adding to some of the uncertainty and volatility in the eurozone.

The euro has also dropped further against the pound. It’s now worth 85p.

The French Education Minister said yesterday that 78 teachers and other education staff had been injured in the student protests so far, while in some cases, members of the public have also been caught in the fray.

But students have also accused police of brutality and using excessive, disproportionate force against them. Rights groups have also raised concerns over violent arrests.

Amnesty International said last week: “Images of high school students being subjected to violence by law enforcement are extremely concerning. Many of them are minors, children. They must be protected, not beaten with batons,” the human rights group wrote on X.

Along with this incident we’re hearing of in Lens this morning where a boy reportedly had their hand blown off by a police grenade, Amnesty last week reported that a 14-year-old had been hit by a grenade to the jaw where he lost several teeth, while other students had been arrested under chokehold techniques by police.

The French Human Rights League (LDH) on Monday also raised the alarm.

“We can see that in the instructions given to the police, there is no directive to show restraint, even though they are dealing with children,” LDH president Nathalie Tehio said.

A reminder that students are protesting en masse against the state of their schools – the overcrowded classes, run-down school buildings and shortage of teachers. The protests began on September 17 in the Parisian suburb of Creteil when students there joined their teachers who were striking over the lack of resources and staff.

Germany at risk of conflict with Russia as it steps up its 'shadow war' activities, intelligence chief warns

In other news, Germany is at risk of entering a violent conflict as Moscow steps up an increasingly aggressive “shadow war” against European democracies, its head of foreign intelligence warned, as reported by Reuters.

“The confrontation we are facing with Russia has taken on a new, more dangerous dimension,”Martin Jäger, head of the Bundesnachrichtendienst (BND), told the annual public hearing of intelligence chiefs in parliament.

“Germany is in danger of becoming embroiled in a violent conflict with Russia – the attempted attack in Leipzig is just one example,” he said, describing Russia as “waging a shadow war” with the threat of attacks against targets in Germany remaining “real and imminent“.

German authorities have warned repeatedly that Russia is conducting a hybrid campaign of drone and cyber-attacks, and have blamed it for an attempted drone attack on Leipzig airport in August.

Jäger, however, said Moscow was prepared to use force to achieve its goals, and that Russian intelligence services’ operations in and against Germany were “increasingly aggressive“.

“Russia is putting us to the test and closely watching how we handle this situation. From the perspective of the Kremlin leadership, any sign of wavering invites them to further increase the pressure on Germany.“

Jäger said there was no indication of an imminent large-scale Russian attack on Nato territory.

“However, Russia could engage in low-intensity military activities, particularly in the Baltic states.“

Germany must prepare for more meddling and attempts at intimidation, including threats of nuclear attack, he said.

“Moscow is betting on our weakness”, he added. “We have to stand up to that.“

Jäger said Moscow was trying to compel Germany, Ukraine’s largest donor, to drop its support.

“Russia is determined to bring Ukraine to its knees this coming winter,” he said. “We must expect Russian attacks of a severity and brutality that we have not yet seen.“

Latvian PM Kulbergs on track to hold on to power after elections

Meanwhile in Latvia, prime minister Andris Kulbergs is on track to hold on to power in Riga and will today set about forming a pro-Ukrainian coalition after his centre-right United List party scored an unprecedented victory in last weekend’s elections.

United List won 42 seats in the 100-seat assembly, the biggest parliamentary election victory since Latvia regained independence in 1991, a result Kulbergs put down to “the people truly wanting change”, meaning mainly “economic growth”.

A former businessman and amateur rally driver, Kulbergs, 47, came to power in May after the previous coalition government collapsed amid uproar over its handling of incidents in which stray Ukrainian military drones flew into Latvia undetected.

He will be forced to change coalition partners since one of the outgoing government’s members failed to clear the 5% parliamentary hurdle and looks able to secure a majority with either the nationalist National Alliance or the liberal Progressives.

Populist parties fared well, with United List followed by the Maga-style Latvia First, which finished second after courting the country’s substantial ethnic Russian minority, with the pro-Russian Sovereign Power third. National Alliance was fourth.

Kulbergs pledged continued support for Ukraine and a hard line towards Moscow. “Any economic ties with Russia makes us weak. Yes, it can bring some extra money, but it’s a weakness Russia might exploit,” he told reporters on Sunday night.

He also promised more anti-drone capability for Latvia and to maintain defence spending at 5% of GDP, one of the highest levels in Nato. His pro-Kremlin rivals said Latvia should stop any aid to Ukraine and instead focus on cost-of-living issues.

Four years after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine security remains central to political debate in Latvia, which is sandwiched between the Baltic Sea and Russia and Belarus to the east, with EU and Nato allies Estonia and Lithuania to the north and south.

Kulbergs said Russia was its principal security threat. “Because we are the external border of Europe, we must have a strong economy ... a strong border region,” Kulbergs said. “That is the main task.”

However, soaring living costs, low wages, an ailing healthcare system and long-term population decline also featured prominently. No Latvian government has survived a full term and analysts say Kulbergs could face an uphill battle even holding together his United List, itself a loose alliance of small parties and independents.

Sánchez calls snap election after housing legislation fails to pass

Sam JonesandAshifa Kassam in Madrid

The government’s efforts to address the housing crisis were set out in two decrees that contained a range of measures aimed at improving access to housing, from a ban on evictions until 2030 to the regulation of temporary lettings and lettings by room.

But the decrees were voted down in congress on Friday by the conservative People’s party (PP), the far-right Vox party, and the rightwing Catalan pro-independence party Junts.

The PP, which is leading the polls but which may need to rely on Vox’s support to form a government, said it had rejected the government’s plans as it believed that construction, and not regulation, was the best way to tackle the crisis.

The rejection of the decrees met with an angry response from the crowds who had gathered outside congress, eliciting boos and cries of “shame” and “Your profits; our misery”.

Long-brewing fury over the high cost of renting and the precarious nature of many tenancies exploded late last month following the eviction of Maricarmen Abascal, an 87-year-old woman who was removed from the home in Madrid where she had lived for 70 years. Abascal said she could not possibly afford the 230% rent hike demanded by the investment fund that bought her apartment.

Over the weekend, large demonstrations were held across the country as protesters stepped up their demands for urgent action on the housing crisis. Authorities said 70,000 people had taken part in a protest in Madrid on Saturday, while organisers put the figure at 500,000.

Although polls consistently place the PP well ahead of Sánchez’s Spanish Socialist Workers’ party – and show that support for Vox is growing – the prime minister may be hoping that the focus on housing, and the right’s rejection of his government’s plans to address the crisis, will mobilise leftwing voters.

Pedro Sánchez is clearly hoping to mobilise voters around the housing crisis and the broader message of home, with opinion polls suggesting his left-wing government might otherwise struggle to retain power.

The conservative People’s Party (PP) looks most likely to take first place and could be take power alongside the far-right Vox party. Polling currently puts the PP on roughly 32-34%, well clear of Sánchez’s Socialists (PSOE) on 26-27%. Vox sits in third place on 18-19%, comfortably ahead of smaller left-wing rivals Sumar (5-6%) and Podemos (2-3%).

Vox’s potential entry into a governing coalition would mark the first time a far-right party has held power in Spain since the end of the Franco dictatorship in 1975.

A defeat for Sánchez would bring down one of western Europe’s few remaining left-leaning administrations at a time when populist and conservative parties are making gains across the continent.

Sánchez’s decision also brings forward one of Europe’s most closely watched electoral contests – originally slated for 2027 alongside major votes in France, Italy, and Poland.
