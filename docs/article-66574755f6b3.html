Trump’s latest land grab is an AI hub in the Philippines

2026-11-18 19:40

President Donald Trump was greeted by no less than five beauty queens when he landed in the Philippines on Tuesday, according to local news outlet Rappler. As he stepped onto the tarmac, a child handed him small US and Philippine flags. A band played “YMCA,” a staple at Trump rallies, as more girls in traditional bell-sleeved blouses and young men in wide-brimmed hats danced to the song.

Outside the airport, hundreds of protesters packed streets bearing signs — and at least one prop casket — saying “Trump out.” Others say “Reject Pax Silica,” the name for the US-led international initiative to secure chips and critical minerals used in the hardware fueling the AI boom.

Peace and prosperity for whom?

“Pax” comes from the Latin word for “peace, stability, and long-term prosperity,” the Pax Silica website proudly says. Yet the unspoken question — peace and prosperity for whom? — explains the whiplash between the Philippine government’s warm welcome of Trump and the public fury raging outside.

Trump says he wants to “win AI” — whatever that means. As such, the US needs data centers full of GPUs to do it. With Americans increasingly opposed to data centers and chip factories being built in their communities, the US is now recruiting less affluent nations to take up some of the slack. Lawmakers from both the US and the Philippines are promising a slice of the riches that advances in generative AI are supposed to create. And that’s supposed to be worth the land razed, homes destroyed, and natural resources lost in the name of technological progress. In the Philippines, that’s taking the shape of a proposed 1,620-hectare (4,000 acres) hub for chip factories, processing plants for critical minerals, and data centers.

At least two dozen countries have signed on to the Pax Silica initiative, and Trump was expected to pressure more to join while in the Philippines for events related to the Association of Southeast Asian Nations summit. With Pax Silica, the US aims to insulate itself from China’s dominance in manufacturing semiconductors and processing critical minerals needed to make those chips. Trump held a bilateral meeting with President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. Wednesday before heading to Shenzhen to meet with China’s President Xi Jinping and Russia’s President Vladimir Putin.

Philippine and US officials are expected to sign a framework agreement by the end of the year for the jointly developed Pax Silica Economic Security Zone. The details are still murky and shrouded in NDAs. The Philippines’ Bases Conversion and Development Authority (BCDA) overseeing the plan expects an economic boom from minerals processing and chip manufacturing, with perhaps a data center or two on site. Meanwhile, human rights advocates who oppose the project warn that it will displace Indigenous residents, cutting them off from water resources on which they rely.

“How do we give up our burial grounds, our church, our livelihood?”

“We do not want to give up our community,” Petronila Capiz-Muñoz, chieftain of the Indigenous Aeta Hungey tribe, told Human Rights Watch. “How do we give up our burial grounds, our church, our livelihood? This is where we source our water, this is where we plant.” Capiz-Muñoz said BCDA officials have told residents that they’ll need to relocate by December to make way for development. Small farmers are also afraid the semiconductor hub will push them off their lands, according to Lian Buan, Southeast Asia researcher at Human Rights Watch.

There are layers of land-grabbing to unpack here.

The Aeta are among the first people to inhabit the Philippines, believed to have arrived around 30,000 years ago. And yet the Aeta Hungey tribe has been trying since 1994 to secure an official certificate recognizing its ancestral domain in one of the areas now facing industrial development. But since the tribe hasn’t been granted that certificate, the BCDA can still claim the AI hub will be built “outside titled ancestral domains.”

The agency says the project will be located on public land located at the former Clark Air Base built in the early 1900s when the Philippines was a US territory. The Philippine constitution now only allows its own citizens to own private land. But the BCDA can offer leases lasting as long as 99 years to foreign developers. The agency expects to rake in close to $1 billion USD (about 60 billion in PHP Peso) from lease income over the first 25 years.

The BCDA was in talks with around 30 different companies interested in setting up shop in Clark’s proposed AI hub, then-BCDA President and CEO Joshua Bingcang told reporters in July. He couldn’t name names, he said, because of NDAs — creating the same kind of opacity that has turned Americans against data centers on their home turf.

He did mention, however, that an American energy company was conducting a feasibility study into building a liquefied natural gas (LNG) line and plant to meet the hub’s enormous electricity demand. Bingcang called LNG a clean fuel, but that’s provably false. LNG is primarily methane, a smog-forming pollutant that is an even more potent greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide.

Bingcang said in September that the Philippine government wanted to invite Nvidia, TSMC, and Apple. While one or two data center developers had expressed interest, according to Bingcang, most of the companies interested were “high tech” manufacturing firms. He has also described chip manufacturing as a “clean” industry, echoing corporate spin that’s been around since the start of Silicon Valley.

At the first chip fabs in California, workers — many of them Filipino immigrants — donned “bunny suits” to assemble sensitive chips in environmentally controlled “clean rooms.” Little did they know that the gear they wore offered little protection against the harsh chemicals used in manufacturing, which also leaked out of storage tanks to contaminate soil and groundwater. When studies came out in the ’90s documenting disproportionately high rates of miscarriages among workers, companies pledged to phase out some of the chemicals linked to reproductive health risks. Around the same time, chip manufacturing started to move out of the US to Asia, where similar health problems stemming from the same chemicals cropped up even decades later.

Chipmakers say their fabs have gotten much safer over the years, in part because more work is automated. Even so, the BCDA estimates that the AI hub will create up to 190,000 direct jobs. The BCDA, White House, and Philippine’s Malacañang Palace did not immediately respond to questions from The Verge.

“I don’t want to date myself, but I knew your parents very well. They were great,” Trump told President Marcos during his visit. Marcos’ parents are estimated to have stolen up to $30 billion from government coffers over decades of rule before a people power movement ousted the current president’s father from the same office in the 1980s. But public memory, it seems, can be short.

And skyrocketing demand for more powerful chips used for generative AI has raised fears among environmental and labor groups that history might repeat itself. Companies move fast and break things, leaving a toxic mess in their wake. And when Americans decide they’ve had enough in their own back yards, the industry sets up shop somewhere else where they think their customers aren’t paying attention and politicians are happy to look the other direction.
