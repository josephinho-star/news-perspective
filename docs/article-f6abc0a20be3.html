Dodgers nearly crack Brewers’ ace — then let Game 1 slip away

2026-10-12 03:14

MILWAUKEE –– The Dodgers did almost everything right Sunday night.

Key word: Almost.

In Game 1 of the National League Championship Series, in a marquee pitching matchup between a reigning two-time Cy Young Award winner and this year’s presumptive recipient of such hardware, the Dodgers came in with a clear game plan against the Milwaukee Brewers and their young stud ace, Jacob Misiorowski.

They wanted to stress Misiorowski’s pitch count by working tough, long at-bats. They wanted to shorten their swing against his 105 mph fastball and hit balls the other way. They wanted to get him out of the game early and let their own ace, Tarik Skubal, pitch with a lead.

On almost all accounts they were successful. Misiorowski threw 94 pitches to get 13 outs. He gave up five hits, four of them to the opposite field. And he only managed to go 4 ⅓ innings, looking nothing like the Cy Young-caliber machine he was during the regular season.

There was just one problem: From all that hard work, they only scored one run.

Instead, in a 2-1 loss at American Family Field that put the Brewers in front to open this best-of-seven series, the Dodgers wasted chance after chance against Misiorowski and the Brewers’ stout bullpen, hitting into three double-plays, stranding nine men on base, and squandering an early lead en route to a Game 1 defeat.

Granted, this series-opener was always more important for the Brewers –– who were trying to protect their home-field advantage, and needed to capitalize on Misiorowski’s start given the lack of star power in their rotation behind him.

Still, the result will sting for the Dodgers –– after they seemingly had the game where they wanted it early, only to cede control with their costly string of missed opportunities.

The Dodgers spent Saturday’s off-day workout prepping for Misiorowski’s unprecedented fastball velocity, using a drill Albert Pujols introduced to the team in 2021 –– in which they took swings against a velocity machine from several steps in front of home plate –– to calibrate their approach against his triple-digit heat.

From the start, the strategy paid off. Just like against Braves ace Chris Sale in the NL Division Series, the Dodgers extended at-bats and created stress repeatedly.

In the first, Freddie Freeman singled on a line drive the other way, though that was negated by a Will Smith inning-ending double-play.

In the second, a four-pitch leadoff walk from Shohei Ohtani was followed by more opposite-field singles from Teoscar Hernández and Andy Pages –– the latter opening the scoring and continuing his recent turnaround this postseason.

In the next at-bat, Kyle Tucker continued the grind, battling his way to a 10-pitch walk that kept the rally going.

Suddenly, with the bases loaded and Misiorowski nearing 40 pitches in the second inning alone, it appeared the Dodgers were on the verge of a knockout blow.

It wouldn’t come to fruition.

Instead, Kiké Hernández bounced into another double-play that allowed Misiorowski to escape the danger. His pitch count continued to climb in the third (when Mookie Betts hit a leadoff single) and the fourth (when Teoscar Hernández hit a leadoff single, then was gifted second on a controversial balk call that negated a pick-off), but the Dodgers again failed to add to the lead.

The trend would quickly prove costly.

After three dominant innings to start the game from Skubal –– who struck out six of his first nine batters, including three on three pitches –– Brice Turang got a 2-0 sinker to leadoff the fourth and deposited it in the Brewers’ left-field bullpen.

The solo blast tied the game. It turned the momentum in a sold-out, yellow-towel-waving American Family Field. And even though Misiorowski would get just one out in the fifth before finally departing, his ability to limit the damage set the stage for the Brewers to take the lead.

All around Milwaukee’s home ballpark, signs have displayed the club’s slogan for this October: “Win Our Way.”

In the fifth, the Brewers showed what that meant.

Epitomizing their style of so-called Brewer Ball, the club’s small-ball offense put its identity on display. Garrett Mitchell drew a leadoff walk. Cooper Pratt bunted him to second. Then, with a short swing on a hanging first-pitch changeup, Luis Lara bounced an RBI single back up the middle.

It was something out of seemingly nothing –– a reminder of how the Brewers, with their bottom-10 payroll of $123 million, ranked second in the majors in scoring this year (despite hitting the second-fewest number of home runs).

Milwaukee almost broke the game open after that, later loading the bases with two outs while inflicting their own damage on Skubal’s pitch count. And though Skubal would get out of it, the lone run would prove to be enough.

Skubal didn’t return for the sixth, removed after 80 pitches in the shortest start of his postseason career.

Four Brewers relievers, meanwhile, would make the 2-1 lead stand up, stranding more runners in the fifth (on a Freeman inning-ending double-play), the sixth (when Pages struck out against Abner Uribe with runners on the corners), the eighth (after Will Smith paused his 1-for-19 playoff slump with a wasted walk) and once again in the ninth (when closer Trevor Megill walked two batters with the bases loaded, before finally ending the game on a flyout from Betts).

So often, they almost seemed to get to the Brewers.

But in the end, it was Milwaukee that executed its game plan more clinically, drawing first blood in an NLCS rematch that already looks nothing like last year’s Dodgers sweep.
