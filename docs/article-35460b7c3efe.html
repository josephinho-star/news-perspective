Even the price of toys is on the rise. How Americans are scaling back holiday plans

2026-11-25 16:17

As Amanda Brecht planned how her family would spend Thanksgiving this year, she had to make some difficult decisions.

Because of high gas prices, the 30-year-old decided she wouldn't drive across her home state of Michigan to celebrate with extended family. Instead, Brecht and her husband will spend the holiday with just their three kids. And with food prices rising, they'll be skipping appetizers and serving turkey breast instead of a whole bird.

"Thanksgiving is definitely going to look different this year," Brecht said. "Money doesn't grow on trees. You can only do so much."

With inflation accelerating, Americans like Brecht will see higher prices almost everywhere they look this holiday season. At a time of year synonymous with food, gifts and big spending, many consumers are instead pinching pennies, paring back their plans and making tough choices about how they'll celebrate.

Some are raiding their savings accounts and running up debt on credit cards. They're reporting downbeat outlooks about the economy in surveys. And they've become more selective with spending as inflationary pressures mount.

"Families are facing some tough choices this year," said Alexander Smith, an associate professor who researches behavioral economics at the Worcester Polytechnic Institute. "They might have to cut back on holiday spending in whatever way that they can."

A classic Thanksgiving feast will be harder to pull off as grocery prices rise.

The price of a 10-person Thanksgiving dinner increased 6% to $58.63 in 2026, according to an annual survey from the American Farm Bureau Federation. Faith Parum, the organization's deputy chief economist, said the price increase was a result of higher costs for labor and transportation.

Grocers including Walmart and Aldi have gained traction on social media by offering Thanksgiving meal bundles as a value-focused workaround to higher prices. Walmart's offering promises to feed 10 for $35, and includes a turkey and ingredients to make mashed potatoes, green bean casserole, cranberries, marshmallow-topped sweet potatoes and a pumpkin pie.

Still, some consumers will feel disappointed by having less choice in their menus due to higher prices, according to Joe Bruseulas, chief economist at tax firm RSM.

"We're missing the point here if all we're doing is focusing on the fact that Walmart will sell you a homogeneous dinner meal" at a discount, Bruseulas said. "People feel horrible that they have to go to that because the price of everything has just risen so significantly over the past five years."

Inflation is affecting more than Thanksgiving staples like turkey or stuffing. Food prices on average have jumped around 24% over the last five years, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Some items have seen even sharper increases: Coffee and ground beef, for example, have each climbed by more than 40% in the same period despite pulling back from recent highs.

At the same time, millions of Americans lost SNAP benefits under policy changes included in President Donald Trump's "big beautiful bill." Food banks across the U.S. have seen foot traffic skyrocket this year as the reduction in federal nutrition aid coincides with rising prices in grocery aisles.

The Salvation Army said some of its food pantries in October provided double or triple the amount of groceries than they did in the same month a year ago. Demand is now so high that some food banks in Feeding America's national network are worried about running out of supplies.

"We want to make sure that our food banks are as full as they possibly can be going into the holiday season," said Linda Nageotte, Feeding America's president and operations chief. "Far too many of them have empty shelves."

High energy prices as a result of the war with Iran are also affecting Americans' holiday plans.

Slightly fewer Americans will travel at least 50 miles around the Thanksgiving holiday in 2026, marking the first year-over-year decline since the country was in lockdown from the pandemic in 2020, according to AAA. Stacey Barber, a vice president at the auto association, attributed this year's pullback in part to higher fuel costs.

AAA said that about nine out of every 10 Thanksgiving travelers will do so by car. While gas prices have come down from a May peak, they are still up about 18% year over year and could set a new record for the month of November, according to the organization's data.

Travelers are paying about 31% and 23% more for roundtrip domestic airfare around Thanksgiving and Christmas, respectively, than they did a year ago, according to booking platform Hopper. Airlines have blamed the ticket price hikes on rising jet fuel costs in the wake of the war.

"There will be a significant shell shock when they see the price of traveling," said Hayley Berg, lead economist at Hopper.

Berg said price increases would feel more noticeable on routes previously serviced by Spirit, the budget airline that shut down in May. Travelers are booking holiday flights earlier than they did in past years, a sign that prices are top of mind, she said.

Staying home for the holidays isn't a silver-bullet solution, either, as heating costs jump.

The average American household is expected to spend 7% more on home heating this winter than they did last year, according to the National Energy Assistance Directors Association. For the typical family, that equates to an increase of about $66 and a total bill for the season above $1,000.

To be sure, some families will feel it more than others. Heating oil, which is used mainly in homes in the Northeast, has jumped around 70% year over year. However, natural gas, which is one of the most common heating types in the U.S., costs about the same as last year.

Some U.S. states, like Massachusetts and New York, beefed up their home energy support programs to account for higher prices. But Mark Wolfe, NEADA's executive director, said federal aid offerings haven't been meaningfully expanded this year.

"Some of these increases are caused directly by the decision to go to war," Wolfe said. "Why isn't the federal government helping families pay these higher bills?"

The spirit of giving will take on a different meaning for consumers this year.

Retail sales during the all-important holiday season are expected to grow almost 5% this year and surpass $1 trillion for the first time, according to Bain & Company. But the consulting firm said most of that growth would come from inflation, rather than from consumers buying more products.

And Citi on Wednesday said that increased holiday spending would be mainly driven by higher earners this year. The bank said that's a result of the "K"-shaped economy, a term used to describe a scenario where better-off Americans splash out while others struggle.

Toy prices have climbed more than 9% over the past year and now sit at their highest levels since 2019, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Manufacturers have raised prices in response to higher costs for transportation and plastic, according to Chris Byrne, an independent toy industry analyst. The artificial intelligence boom has driven up prices for the chips that power gaming devices and other electronics.

Byrne said that's causing shoppers to focus more on the quality of gifts rather than quantity.

"The toy industry always reflects the culture at large," Byrne said. "It's almost true economically as well. As inflation is rising, people are being a little bit more cautious about what they're buying."

Just 16% of respondents to a Morning Consult survey said they planned to spend more in the 2026 holiday season than they did a year ago. Respondents who were older or had lower earnings were more likely to say their budgets were shrinking, the survey found.

Some are doing away with gifts altogether. A poll from consulting firm TSG and the Electronic Transactions Association found 5% of Americans did not plan to buy any presents in 2026, up three percentage points from last year.

One shopper, Wendy Cooper, set a limit of one gift for each of her three adult children this year. For her teenage daughter, the 56-year-old Atlanta resident has scoured Five Below, the trendy value retailer, for budget-friendly presents.

"It's super stressful," Cooper said. "You have to weigh: Do you guys want lights on and a place to live? Or do you want gifts?"

Across the board, the Bureau of Labor Statistics' consumer price index reaccelerated after the U.S. first struck Iran in late February. Annual inflation rates have begun rising faster than wages in the same period, leaving consumers with less purchasing power.

Even if Americans keep spending for the holidays, economists caution that high prices during this busy shopping period can further drag on their economic outlooks. In the CNBC Voter Poll early this month, about three-fourths of participants rated the economy as poor or not good. Consumer confidence gauges had dropped significantly this year, but have regained ground in the runup to the holiday shopping season.

From her home in Northern Michigan, Brecht is doing everything she can to make Christmas feel special without breaking the bank.

The graduate student has been delivering DoorDash orders in her spare time and listing clothes on resale platforms to save up money for her kids' holiday gifts. Brecht is watching closely for discounts and will look for potential presents at thrift stores in the coming weeks.

If necessary, Brecht said she would turn to credit cards for some holiday purchases. She isn't alone: TSG and ETA's survey found that about one-third of Americans said they expected to go into debt during the holiday season, a roughly 74% increase from 2025.

"I don't really know how we're going to do it," Brecht said. "I'm just praying for good Black Friday deals."
