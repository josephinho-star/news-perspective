Turkish Parliament approves Saudi-Pakistan defense pact despite opposition concerns

2026-11-06 13:17

Turkey’s parliament has approved a mutual defense agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan that treats an armed attack on one country as an attack on all three, despite warnings from opposition lawmakers that the pact could draw Ankara into regional conflicts, Turkish media reported.

The Mecca Joint Defense Agreement was approved Thursday by 279 votes to 119, completing Turkey’s parliamentary ratification process. A total of 398 lawmakers took part in the vote.

The ruling Justice and Development Party (AKP), its ally the Nationalist Movement Party (MHP) and the opposition Republican People’s Party (CHP) supported the agreement, while the main opposition New Party, the İYİ (Good) Party, the pro-Kurdish Peoples’ Equality and Democracy Party (DEM Party) and other opposition lawmakers voted against it.

Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif signed the 16-article agreement on August 7. Parliament’s Foreign Affairs Committee approved the ratification bill on October 27.

Under its central collective defense provision, an external armed attack on any of the three countries will be considered an attack on all of them. The other parties are required to provide assistance, which can include military support, although its nature and scope are to be determined through consultations.

The pact also provides for intelligence sharing, joint military exercises and training, logistical support, counterterrorism cooperation and closer defense industry ties, including technology transfers, joint research and development projects and joint production.

Fears of involvement in regional conflicts

Opposition lawmakers who rejected the agreement argued that Turkey could become involved in conflicts arising from Saudi Arabia’s confrontation with Yemen’s Iran-backed Houthi rebels or tensions between Pakistan and India.

The New Party, led by former CHP chairman Özgür Özel, has been among the strongest critics of the agreement, accusing the government of risking Turkish military involvement in Yemen.

“The New Party will never remain silent about or support Turkey being made a party to the war and Turkish soldiers being sent to the deserts of Yemen,” Özel said.

İYİ Party leader Müsavat Dervişoğlu similarly opposed the agreement, saying, “We say ‘No’ to turning Turkish soldiers into Aramco’s legionnaires; we say ‘No’ to the Mecca Agreement,” referring to Saudi Arabia’s state-controlled oil giant.

Critics also questioned what additional security benefits the pact would provide to Turkey, which has been a NATO member since 1952 and has the alliance’s second-largest military, and raised concerns about possible conflicts between Turkey’s obligations under the two alliances.

Other objections centered on the absence of a clearly defined common threat, differences among the three countries over which organizations they designate as terrorist groups and a provision preventing any member from withdrawing from the agreement during its first 10 years.

The CHP supported ratification but also called for safeguards. CHP parliamentary group chairman Faik Öztrak said regional security cooperation should not be used as justification for open-ended military operations and stressed that parliament’s authority over deployments abroad must be preserved.

Foreign Affairs Committee Chairman Fuat Oktay of the AKP rejected claims that the agreement could automatically drag Turkey into a war, saying any deployment of Turkish troops for combat abroad would still require parliamentary authorization under Article 92 of the constitution.

Alliance already taking shape

Opposition lawmakers have also accused the government of beginning to implement the agreement before obtaining parliamentary approval.

The three countries held the first meeting of Strategic Political and Defense Committee, a body established by the agreement and comprising the three countries’ foreign and defense ministers and military chiefs, in İstanbul on August 31.

At an emergency meeting in Riyadh on October 5, they subsequently agreed to arrange the rapid deployment of military forces and capabilities to Saudi Arabia, subject to their national laws, as Houthi missile and drone attacks on the kingdom intensified.

Turkey has said its assistance to Saudi Arabia has so far been mainly technical and defensive, including equipment, weapons systems and intelligence sharing.

Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan has ruled out sending Turkish troops to conduct offensive operations in Yemen, while Turkish officials have said Ankara’s possible contribution could include additional air defense equipment and other defensive capabilities.

The pact also states that it does not affect rights and obligations arising from the countries’ existing international agreements, a provision relevant to Turkey’s NATO membership.

Saudi Arabia and Pakistan had already completed their domestic ratification procedures. The agreement will enter into force after the final ratification documents are deposited with the pact’s secretariat.
