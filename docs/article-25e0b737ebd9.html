UN sacked Palestinian staff on back of unproven Israeli allegations, tribunal rules

2026-11-26 00:05

A UN tribunal has cast doubt on the evidence used to justify the sacking of Palestinian UN aid workers in 2024, saying it was based “almost entirely” on unproven assertions from the Israeli government and had damaged humanitarian operations in Gaza.

The judgment, seen by the Guardian, raises fresh questions about the decision by the UN Relief and Works Agency (Unrwa), the main humanitarian organisation working in Gaza, to sack members of staff. It was taken on the basis of Israeli allegations of involvement in the Hamas-led attacks of 7 October 2023.

The nine workers had their contracts terminated in January 2024 “in the interests of the agency” and were told in termination letters that the allegations against them were “very serious and credible”. The dismissals were confirmed after a UN investigation found they might have been involved in the 7 October attacks.

The decision, which was taken by former Unrwa commissioner general Philippe Lazzarini after consultation with the UN secretary general, António Guterres, was apparently made in the hope that appeasing Israel and its allies would allow humanitarian work to continue in Gaza.

Critics warned at the time that the decision, on the basis of allegations rather than evidence, compromised the UN’s integrity and had the opposite effect, giving credence to unproven allegations.

The dismissals failed to prevent the Biden administration ordering a pause in funding, a measure that was echoed temporarily by other backers, leading to a financial shortfall of $450m (£340m).

“I have always said on the record that the Israeli allegations against Unrwa were the mother of all lies,” said Chris Gunness, a former Unrwa spokesperson. “Sacking the workers without any evidence confirmed their guilt unfairly, in the minds of journalists, led 16 top donors to abandon Unrwa and produced the worst financial crisis in the agency’s history, from which it may never recover.”

The Unrwa dispute tribunal that ruled in favour of one of its sacked workers, Abu Awad, described the Israeli allegations as unsupported and of “limited evidentiary value and reliability”.

The tribunal also found that an internal UN investigation had not interviewed the 19 staff members initially accused by Israel of involvement in the 7 October attacks because of “safety and security” concerns.

“The allegations against the applicant and the other staff members, it seems, are therefore based almost entirely on information in Israel’s possession,” the tribunal said.

It cast doubt on intercepted mobile phone conversations and messages offered by Israel as evidence, noting that one case had involved mistaken identity based on a cloned sim card, a common phenomenon in Gaza.

It also said that voice recognition claims made by Israel were dubious and some transcripts of conversations were found by UN investigators not to support claims that they proved links to Hamas or the 7 October attacks.

The allegation against Awad was that he was a “platoon commander” in Hamas’s military wing, the Al-Qassam Brigades, a claim he rejected. He was not alleged to have physically taken part in the 7 October attack and witness statements to the tribunal said he was on an Unrwa staff bus as normal.

The specific allegation against Awad was that he “had been instructed in a telephone call intercepted by Israeli intelligence to monitor the events of 7 October 2023, while simultaneously receiving Hamas intelligence updates related to the attacks”.

The tribunal said the Israeli security services had failed to clarify who was alleged to have made the call to Awad and in what way the mobile phone that received the call was associated with him.

Unrwa did not respond to a request for comment. Asked by the tribunal to provide evidence underpinning the dismissal of the workers, the agency cited the Israeli government as well as a report in the Times of Israel and another on the website UN Watch, a pro-Israel pressure group that has campaigned against Unrwa.

In its ruling, the tribunal said Unrwa’s claim that the cited sources had informed the decision to dismiss the workers was “open to some doubt” because all the supporting documents it had submitted were produced after the disputed decision was made on 26 January 2024.

Lazzarini, a Swiss humanitarian expert who left the commissioner general post in March, said he understood the tribunal’s finding might be appealed so he could not discuss the case in detail. In February 2024, he admitted his decision to sack the workers represented “reverse due process” in that the investigation followed the dismissal.

“My judgment, based on this going public, true or untrue, was I need to take the swiftest and boldest decision to show that as an agency we take this allegation seriously,” he said, adding that “the ability of the entire agency to continue to operate and deliver critical humanitarian assistance was at stake”.

The tribunal said it could not decide on the allegations against Awad but ruled that Unrwa had failed to produce evidence supporting its decision to sack him. It awarded him a year’s pay and $5,000 for “moral damages”.

In the wake of its allegations against the sacked workers, Israel claimed that Unrwa was heavily infiltrated by Hamas and its parliament voted in 2024 to ban the agency from operating in Israel or in the occupied Palestinian territories.

The international court of justice stated in October 2025, however, that “Israel has not substantiated its allegations that a significant part of Unrwa’s employees are members of Hamas … or other terrorist factions”.
