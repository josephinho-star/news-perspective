Hancock’s claim about protecting care homes shown by Covid inquiry to be ‘cruel lie’, victims’ families say – UK politics live

2026-10-20 13:47

Inquiry report shows Hancock's claim about protecting care homes 'a cruel lie', Covid victims' campaign group says

The Covid-19 Bereaved Families for Justice group has issued a statement about today’s inquiry report saying it shows that Matt Hancock’s claim about a protective ring being thrown around care homes (see 12.22pm) was a “cruel lie”.

Today’s report is a devastating account of one of the darkest episodes in our country’s history. It shows that the government knew the care sector was extremely vulnerable to a pandemic and yet did nothing. Failing to act until it was too late. And It shows that Matt Hancock’s claim to have thrown a protective ring around care homes was a cruel lie. Months before telling us that lie, Matt Hancock sat in a room with Boris Johnson and Simon Stevens and made the decision to discharge hospital patients into care homes, without testing. Three men, with no medical expertise between them, made a decision that became a death sentence for our family members.

The most at-risk people in our society, the sick, elderly – many of whom had a full and long life ahead of them – were failed by a government who abandoned them and left them to die. That is the consequence of social care system treated as a second-class service – too difficult for politicians to reform, too easy to ignore. Our family members paid the price. That is why social care reform cannot wait.

Former Bank of England chief economist Andy Haldane says Tory growth policies 'far more likely to succeed' than Labour's

At one point in the summer Andy Haldane, the former chief economist at the Bank of England, was informally advising Andy Burnham. Haldane spent most of his career as an official at the Bank. He is not a party political figure. But at the Bank he was viewed as an independent thinker, he was brought up in the north of England and he is committed to regional growth (which is one reason why Michael Gove hired him as a levelling up adviser).

But any suggestions that Haldane is a Burnham supporter have now been quashed by an article Haldane has published in the Financial Times. In it, he says the Conservaties’ economic policies are “far more likely to succeed” than Labour’s.

In the decade since Brexit, both parties have swung simultaneously right on social policy and left on economic policy. The interventionist economic agendas of Conservative prime ministers Theresa May, Boris Johnson and Rishi Sunak would not have been out of place under New Labour. At the 2024 general election, only a vape wrapper separated the parties’ economic manifestos.

Today, those differences are stark. Andy Burnham’s conference speech was a homage to 1950s-style state socialism. Kemi Badenoch’s speech was also a throwback, but to the smaller state and lower taxes of the 1980s. Which offers the more likely path to growth?

In today’s straitened circumstances, the rightward fork is far more likely to succeed.

Haldane argues the Tories have not always been right. He says George Osborne’s austerity policies were a mistake.

At the time of austerity, UK government debt was not only far smaller than it is today, but the cost of servicing it was lower than at any time in human history. Remarkably, the country’s long-term borrowing costs were negative in inflation-adjusted terms for protracted periods. There will probably never have been a better time for public investment.

Yet funding was cut and a £2tn investment gap opened up relative to international competitors, stunting growth. Veering right was wrong.

But, in the current circumstances, Conservative policies would be best for growth, he says.

Today’s fiscal environment is fundamentally different. Long-term borrowing costs have risen 4-5 percentage points and now sit above estimates of the UK economy’s long-term nominal growth rate. That signals the need for primary fiscal surpluses, on a sustained basis, if the UK’s debt dynamics are to stabilise.

The Labour party’s plan for an expansive (and expensive) state sits uncomfortably with this precarious fiscal backdrop. It also sits oddly with the fact that public-sector productivity has failed to rise this century. Both state financial capacity and personnel capability are at present sources of economic weakness, not strength.

In this debt-laden environment, the pursuit of a larger state is more likely to crowd out than crowd in growth. It also runs the risk of a loss of confidence in bond markets that causes borrowing costs to ratchet — think “Liz Truss-plus” …

The Conservatives’ economic plans are better suited to that task: for example, through cuts to business and employment taxes and regulation to unleash animal spirits; and by slimming government spending to lower borrowing costs, as the IMF and Bank of England have recently highlighted.

Danny Kruger tells Reform UK rally media 'trying to stop us winning'

In Plymouth, the Reform UK rally is just getting under way. Danny Kruger is the first person to speak. He starts by saying that the party has been going through some “turbulence” recently, referring to the ITV undercover exposé (summarised here and here). ITV News found some “rotten apples”, he says, and they have to go.

But he says the journalists behind the investigation are not trying to help. They are “trying to stop us winning”, he says. He goes on:

I’m not here to whine about the media because we’re not the real victims. We’re not the real targets. It’s not us they’re trying to stop. It’s the British people …

It’s not who we are that they hate. It’s what we want to do. We want to take our country back for the people of our country. And they know it. And that’s why they hate us.

Healey accused of missing 'open goal' after he rules out raising capital gains tax in budget

John Healey, the chancellor, has been accused of missing an open goal. The claim came from Tax Justice UK, which issued this statement, from its deputy director Caitlin Boswell, in response to the news that Healey has ruled out raising capital gains tax in the budget. (See 8.47am.)

Ruling out a pro-worker, pro-fairness and pro-growth tax rise is a missed open goal. It suggests this government is out of step with the large numbers of MPs across Westminster calling for this change, alongside leading economists. Let alone millions of people who are sick and tired of their needs feeling secondary to those of the super-rich.

Asking those with unimaginable wealth to pay just a little bit more would benefit us all – it’s people being able to afford the basics, see a doctor when they need one, or afford a safe place to live. These are some fundamental things that shouldn’t be hard to deliver in one of the richest countries in the world.

Tax Justice UK backs a plan to raise capital gains tax set out by the CenTax thinktank in a report two years ago. This would raise £14bn, according to CenTax (the Centre for the Analysis of Taxation).

During the pandemic, the action taken by the UK government and devolved administrations to try and protect workers and users of adult social care came too late.

The failures were the direct result of an absence of proper pandemic planning for the adult social care sector.

This underlines why my recommendations in this report must be acted upon now, before the next pandemic strikes.

Hallett says England should follow Scotland, Wales and NI in having 'invaluable' register for social care workers

Hallett said one of her recommendations was for a voluntary register of care workers in England.

I am also recommending the introduction of a voluntary register for the social care workforce in England.

The existing registers in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland proved invaluable during the pandemic. They allowed governments to identify recently retired professionals who could return to work and to communicate vital guidance directly. England must follow suit.

Hallett says in any future pandemic extra financial support for care workers should be available 'at the start'

Hallett said care workers felt “overlooked and undervalued” during the pandemic.

At least 1.5 million care workers were providing care during the pandemic. Many suffered burnout as a consequence of extreme stress and exhaustion. Visits by health care workers dramatically reduced, and this placed an even greater burden on care workers who stepped in to provide this care. They put their own lives at risk to show compassion to the dying, leaving a long lasting impact on their own physical and mental health.

Hallett said, in any future pandemic, “financial support to protect care workers from economic hardship must be in place at the start”.

I now return to the deeply troubling issue of Do Not Attempt Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation notices, known as DNACPRs. These notices record a medical decision not to attempt CPR in order to try and save a life.

During the pandemic, there were concerns that these notices were improperly imposed on groups of people, such as people with learning disabilities or older people.

I make no apology for repeating what I said in module three, where these concerns were also raised. This should not have happened.

The inquiry has found no evidence of national policies condoning their blanket application. However, the sheer speed of the pandemic meant that discussions about advanced care plans, which record an individual’s preferences for future medical care and can include discussions about DNACPRs, were too often rushed or nonexistent.
