Wildlife protection must be part of climate action, campaigners tell COP31

2026-11-17 15:33

Conservationists on Tuesday urged countries at the COP31 climate summit to include wildlife protection in climate negotiations, saying wild animals play a key role in maintaining carbon-storing ecosystems.

Twenty-five countries, from Brazil to The Gambia, launched the Wildlife for Climate Declaration at COP31 in the Turkish city of Antalya, committing to protect, conserve and restore wild animal populations and their habitats “as a cornerstone of science-based climate action.”

“Wildlife performs incredibly important services in the ecosystems we rely on to capture and store carbon. Without wildlife, these ecosystems cannot function to their full potential,” Matt Collis, senior director of policy at the International Fund for Animal Welfare, told Agence France-Presse.

“But so far, wildlife has been missing from the climate conversation here at COP. What we hope this declaration will do is send a political signal that wildlife needs to be part of that conversation,” he said.

Campaigners say wild animals are not merely victims of climate change but can also be part of the solution, underscoring the need to better align climate and biodiversity policies.

“I think we have increasingly realized that we cannot tackle one crisis without addressing the other,” Collis said.

“Too often, we have seen wildlife simply as a victim of climate change, rather than as a contributor to the solutions.”

The declaration encourages countries to integrate wildlife conservation into national climate plans and better align those plans with biodiversity protection strategies.

A scientific consensus statement underpinning the declaration, signed by 353 scientists from 47 countries, said protecting species such as African forest elephants and sea otters can boost carbon storage in ecosystems.

It also said that if forests currently occupied by tigers lost the big cats, carbon emissions would be 9 percent higher and carbon removals 28 percent lower than observed.

“Can you imagine a world without vultures? A world without lions?” Evelyn Ndlovu, Zimbabwe’s environment, climate and wildlife minister, asked during a panel discussion.

She said wildlife should be considered a “natural ally” in tackling climate change.

“Wildlife are the engineers of our planet,” she said.

“When wildlife disappears, ecosystems are weakened … This is not an argument for treating wildlife as an alternative to rapid or sustained emissions reduction … It is a call to make climate action more complete, more effective, and more faithful to the way our natural world works.”
