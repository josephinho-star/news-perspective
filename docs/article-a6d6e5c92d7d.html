Tens of thousands join education protests in Greece

Back again to Greece where tens of thousands of students have joined the burgeoning youth movement demanding better education better prospects amid an ever worsening cost of living crisis in the Mediterranean EU member.

Greece is no newcomer to protests. The massive demonstrations that roiled the country at the height of its debt crisis are still remembered vividly by the parents of the Gen Z protesters now out on the streets.

But for the tens of thousands who marched through Athens, Thessaloniki and other cities today, it was France, as well as the perceived shortcomings of a long under-funded education system, that had spurred them into action.

“From Greece to France we’re fighting for public and free education,” proclaimed a giant banner erected above a stage in front of the Athens parliament in Syntagma Square.

Singing French partisan songs, Greek students participating in the Athens rally, said the French protests were not only admirable but had been an inspiration.

“No one is doubting their message or concerns, even if [media] coverage is mostly focused on the violence,” said Fotini, enrolled at a university in the capital.

“We’re here to say we’re with French youth. Our parents endured so many hardships, so much sacrifice, and they feel really upset and enraged that we, too, have so few prospects. A lot of us are still forced to live with our families, even after we graduate, because the cost of living and house rents are so high.”

The protests, which included a one-day ‘solidarity ‘ strike by teachers, has seen students erect tents infront of the Greek parliament to protest against the soaring costs of education.

Many have privately run colleges – a trend that has included British universities establishing branches in the capital – in their sights. “No to private universities. Free and contemporary studies for all,” read another boldly painted banner erected in the plaza.

Will Europe’s angry protests lead to real political change?

European community affairs correspondent

Much of the news coverage in recent days has focused on the violence that played out on the fringes of the protests in France and Italy.

The protests themselves, however, point to what seems to be a diverse response to a common crisis: the growing perception, particularly among younger generations, that governments are unable, or unwilling, to deliver when it comes to affordable housing, social equality and robust public services.

“This is the most powerful warning from Europe’s angry youth today, in France and across the continent,” the policy analyst Alberto Alemanno said.

The framing stands in sharp contrast to the many rightwing and far-right voices who have sought to exploit the violence in France and Italy, linking it without evidence to migration while brushing over protesters’ wider demands.

A student protest movement has gripped Serbia for two years. This weekend it will try to take power

On Sunday, a mass student movement, Student List, will represent the most serious challenge Aleksandar Vučić has faced in his 12 increasingly authoritarian years in power, by putting forward their own candidates in parliamentary elections.

They hope to emulate the defeat of Vučić’s ally, Viktor Orbán,in neighbouring Hungary six months ago, in a domino effect that could bring a democratic resurgence to central Europe.

Opinion polls have varied wildly, but many expert observers say a close race is quite possible, with neither side prepared to accept a loss as a legitimate result, raising the risk of post-election unrest and clashes.

In order to win, the students’ list of 250 candidates would have to break the mould of Serbian politics, along with Vučić’s grip on the country.

He stepped down as president last month, five months before the end of his second and last term in office under the constitution, but only with the aim of extending his hold on power as prime minister at the head of his Serbian Progressive party (SNS).

Southern Italy running out of jet fuel, products, medicines as lorry protest continues

Meanwhile, two airports have run out of jet fuel, supermarket shelves across southern Italy are standing empty and pharmacies are running short of medicines as lorry drivers continue to blockade roads in protest against soaring fuel prices caused by the war in Iran.

Italy’s air traffic management agency, ENAV, said on Wednesday that airports in Crotone and Reggio Calabria had run out of aviation fuel because of disruptions to supplies caused by the protests. It warned that pilots would have to refuel at their departure airports or earlier stops, carrying enough fuel for subsequent flights.

The protests began last week, with lorry drivers blocking roads and slowing traffic across the country, particularly in the south, following strikes and sit-ins by farmers and fishers. The disruption has left supermarket shelves empty in several regions, with photographs showing entire aisles stripped of goods.

A video of a lorry driver’s teenage daughter breaking down in tears has gone viral after she said her family could no longer afford to go out at weekends because they were struggling to pay their bills.

Italy’s infrastructure minister, Matteo Salvini, has proposed €430m in financial support, but the motorway blockades and lengthy traffic jams continue, putting the government under growing pressure as anger over the cost of living mounts.

The protests have also sparked criticism of Italy’s public broadcaster, Rai, which has been accused of giving greater prominence to violent student demonstrations in Milan than to the economic hardship facing families and lorry drivers in the south. Critics say the coverage plays into the government’s increasingly hardline approach to protests while diverting attention from the growing cost-of-living crisis.

Why are French police so violent? Their job is to protect the state, not the people

Familiar images of teargas clouds drifting across Paris squares, police baton charges against masked protesters and terrified teenagers floored – and worse – by stun grenades have once again focused the world’s attention on France’s policing methods.

While most of its western European neighbours generally favour police tactics that prioritise crowd containment and de-escalation, France has long responded to social movements that take to the streets with, at times, shocking physical force.

The reasons why are a complex mix of history, politics, state organisation and a particular security culture that has become deeply ingrained over decades, if not centuries, and whose limits continue to be pushed today.

French policing policy is rooted in Napoleon’s ultra-centralised state and in the political philosophy of the Republic. In France, the police are an instrument of the state. Their core task is to protect it, enforce its laws, and maintain public order.

Conceptually, that is a far cry from, for example, the UK, where since the early 1800s the police have been considered servants of the public, whose authority derives from public consent and whose chief responsibility is to keep the peace.

French police officers are agents of state authority. That means protests, blockades and mass disorder are not just civil nuisances, but direct challenges to the authority of the republic. The police’s priority is to reclaim the streets, not to negotiate.

That explains the aggressive tactics, such as systematically encircling and trapping crowds, and their continued use of “intermediate” or less-than-lethal weapons, many of which are banned or heavily restricted elsewhere in Europe.

Macron blasts 'unacceptable' and 'stupid' commentary on French protests from other leaders

Meanwhile, French president Emmanuel Macron blasted “unacceptable” and “stupid” comments on the protests and riots in France from foreign leaders, accusing them of “campaigning at our expense.”

Speaking to reporters from Doha, visibly annoyed Macron said he was frustrated by “visible manipulation of information” about the events in France, “that were unacceptable and stupid from many quarters, including from foreign leaders.”

“For instance, we saw images generated by artificial intelligence depicting scenes that never actually occurred in France. Some people even claimed that we had been overrun by hordes of migrants and Islamists – which is completely false.”

(US president Donald Trump explicitly suggested that, repeatedly, and several EU leaders sort of said similar things, linking the protests with migration, including Hungary’s Péter Magyar and Poland’s Donald Tusk.)

Macron said he was particularly annoyed by political leaders “whether in our own region or on the other side of the Atlantic, gullibly repeating claims generated by AI or echoing narratives that certain circles with to propagate … it is clear that they are campaigning at our expense.”

He said these comments “reveal who loves France and who does not.”

Macron said he would hold a meeting with ministers and intelligence officials on his return on Thursday to get a “detailed account” of what happened and preparations for this weekend’s protests.

Republicans push back on Donald Trump’s wavering support for Ukraine

Overnight, a handful of Republicans have spoken out against Donald Trump’s softening stance on Russia, threatening to pull financial support for his agenda over his “embarrassing” handling of the Ukraine war.

Don Bacon, an outgoing congressman in Nebraska, condemned Trump’s actions – which include a stunning agreement to buy Russian diesel and the pausing of sanctions on Moscow – as “absolutely disgraceful” and accused the US president of cozying up to a “war criminal” in Vladimir Putin, his Russian counterpart.

“It really is disgraceful, and the Republicans that I associate with, there’s a lot of us, can’t stand it,” Bacon told CNN on Monday. “I think the president’s leverage in this Congress is shot.”

Last week, Trump waived sanctions imposed on Russia through a law named after his longtime ally and late senator Lindsey Graham. The legislation, which he signed only last month, was intended to increase financial pressure on Russia, more than four years after it invaded Ukraine.
