FAA chief, lawmakers tussle over $50B funding request: 4 takeaways

2026-11-18 19:24

Capitol Hill lawmakers, travel industry experts and the head of the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) discussed how American aviation needs to evolve on Wednesday, less than a week after the first nationwide ground stop since January 2023.

During The Hill’s Red, Flight & Blue: Future of U.S. Aviation event, FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford took the blame for the ground stop, which stemmed from a telecommunications outage.

He also reiterated the Trump administration’s new request of $50 billion in funding for the FAA to upgrade the country’s air traffic control (ATC) systems.

But Reps. Steve Womack (R-Ark.) and Dina Titus (D-Nev.) on Wednesday joined numerous lawmakers in pushing back on that request.

Here are four takeaways from the event.

Bedford says system ‘safe,’ takes blame for outage

Bedford sought to reassure Americans about the country’s airspace after Friday’s ground stop.

“The system is absolutely safe,” Bedford told The Hill’s Julia Manchester.

The administrator acknowledged, though, the FAA has “reliability challenges” with its “legacy systems.” The agency has cited a router in Arkansas and a radar site in Texas as the two likely causes of the outage.

“This was the 69th installation,” Bedford said of the Arkansas router. “The other 68 had gone well. There was no reason to think that we would see some spontaneous functionality under this router that we hadn’t seen in the previous 68 installations.”

The FAA administrator ultimately took responsibility for the technological issues, which left more than a million passengers stranded across the country.

In doing so, Bedford sought to shield Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy from blame, urging Americans and lawmakers to “lay off” him.

“I’m the guy they hired to get this done. So blame me. If we need to blame somebody, blame me,” Bedford said.

Titus, Womack press for answers before backing more FAA funding

To enhance an “old” and “unstable” system, as Bedford put it, Duffy in recent days has upped how much he wants from Congress for airport and ATC upgrades from $30 billion to $50 billion.

“Congressional approval of these funds will equip the FAA to deliver to the American people a brand-new air traffic control system that is safer, smarter, faster, and more efficient,” Duffy, Bedford and Office of Management and Budget Director Russell Vought wrote Tuesday in a letter to the chairs and ranking members on the House and Senate Appropriations panels.

But lawmakers on both sides of the aisle have challenged that request, and Titus and Womack added to that chorus on Wednesday.

“When they come and say they want $50 billion, and they say it’s Congress’s fault for not giving them the money, that’s not a really good way to begin the negotiation,” Titus, a member of the House Transportation and Infrastructure Subcommittee on Aviation, told The Hill’s Sarah Fortinsky at the event, which was sponsored by Airlines for America.

The Nevada Democrat also questioned how the FAA has spent money it received for upgrades via the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021 and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025.

Those two pieces of legislation allocated a combined $37.5 billion to the agency for airport and ATC system upgrades.

Titus pointed out that Duffy “keeps switching” his request for funding without explaining what exactly caused the outage and how the FAA will spend newly requested money. Without that, she noted lawmakers will be “a little reluctant” to back $50 billion for the agency.

Womack struck a similar tone, saying he wants a “sequential plan” and “granular data” from the administration on why its funding request increased.

The Arkansas Republican, who chairs the House Appropriations subcommittee on transportation, added he wants detailed answers on the cause of Friday’s debacle.

“We are, what, five days removed from a situation that really turned the country upside down. … I’m not satisfied that we have a complete forensics analysis and a satisfactory conclusion as to exactly what happened,” he told Fortinsky.

Duckworth backs extra funding with ‘accountability’ for FAA

Sen. Tammy Duckworth (D-Ill.) said the FAA’s funding request does not “scare” her, adding the National Airspace System needs multiple “redundancies” instead of relying on “duct tape and bubble gum and string.”

But the Illinois senator, the top Democrat on the Senate Commerce Subcommittee on Aviation, Space, and Innovation, said that money must come with “accountability” for how the executive branch spends it.

“The plan so far that’s been briefed to me is, ‘Oh, we’re just going to spend money here, we’re going to spend money here.’ … You need an integrated strategic plan for how you’re going to roll this out,” she told Manchester, after noting she spoke to Bedford on Tuesday night.

She also said the FAA has implemented only a “handful” of the 53 safety recommendations the National Transportation Safety Board issued after the fatal January 2025 crash in which American Airlines Flight 5342 and an Army Black Hawk helicopter collided in midair.

Sen. Ted Cruz (R-Texas), the chair of the Senate Commerce Committee, said Tuesday that Senate and House leaders had reached a preliminary aviation safety agreement, which includes expanded requirements for planes to use specific location surveillance technology while flying.

Yet Duckworth warned the Army is opposed to Cruz’s Rotorcraft Operations Transparency and Oversight Reform (ROTOR) Act, which first proposed the expanded requirements for the use of Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast.

“The Army’s own internal investigations show that some of the things in the ROTOR Act should be taking place, and they’re still opposing it,” she told Manchester.

Industry experts warn about potential shutdown

Multiple aviation experts warned about the impacts another government shutdown would have on the industry, as lawmakers face a Dec. 11 funding deadline.

“You’re going to tell me you’re not going to pay your own air traffic controllers and your TSA [Transportation Security Administration] officers and all of that as we’re going into the holidays?” Chris Sununu, the president of Airlines for America, told Bill Sammon, The Hill’s senior vice president of editorial content.

Sununu, a former Republican governor of New Hampshire, said that would be “such a Grinch move” from lawmakers. But he expressed confidence that “common sense will prevail.”

During last fall’s government shutdown and the shutdown of the Department of Homeland Security last winter and this spring, call-outs by unpaid air traffic controllers and TSA officers led to widespread flight delays and cancellations.

Nick Daniels, president of the National Association of Air Traffic Controllers, noted ATC towers are “critically staffed” already.

“We only have 11,000 certified professional controllers. … We should have approximately 14,000 certified controllers that should be in the field,” he told Fortinsky.
