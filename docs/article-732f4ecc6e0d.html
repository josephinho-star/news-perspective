For Democrats, it is time to get serious

2026-11-15 18:00

Democrats have spent months arguing that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is unfit for his position and should be removed from office. As someone who spent 15 years working for eight secretaries of Defense, I believe they were right.

Hegseth’s defenders might argue that Hegseth’s norm-shattering behavior may be merely aberrant or illegal, and that the executive branch’s leeway to conduct extended military operations without congressional approval has long been contested. However, there is little doubt that Hegseth has overseen extrajudicial killings, human rights abuses, and violations of the law of armed conflict in Venezuela and Iran. If those actions don’t constitute “high crimes and misdemeanors,” those words no longer have a coherent meaning.

When Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) forced a vote on whether to impeach Hegseth, Democrats had one job: show up to vote. Instead, 11 Democrats did not turn up for the vote, which allowed the Republicans — by a seven vote margin — to bury the measure in committee for the rest of this Congress.

The missing Democrats offered a flurry of excuses for their absences, but their explanations were unserious and unacceptable. Most of them got stuck in traffic on the way to Capitol Hill from a party celebrating their recent midterm victories. Even Speaker Emerita Nancy Pelosi (D-Calif.) missed the vote — she was in California receiving an award.

These explanations wouldn’t fly if they were coming from a first-term city councilor missing a routine vote, let alone members of the world’s most important legislative body considering the impeachment of a Cabinet secretary waging an unauthorized war costing the lives of American service members and nearly $50 billion in taxpayer dollars to date.

The Democrats’ failure in this instance has arguably strengthened Hegseth and handed President Trump the opportunity to proclaim, however ludicrously, that the vote was an “EXONERATION” for his embattled Defense secretary.

Some Democrats have suggested the public shouldn’t be concerned about this episode, as they will simply impeach Hegseth at the start of the new Congress in 2027, when Democrats will control both chambers. This is cold comfort. With Massie departing Congress, a renewed effort to impeach Hegseth will be viewed as a purely partisan exercise. Moreover, if conviction and removal from office is the Democrats’ true objective, the Senate arithmetic may also be less favorable next year.

Yes, there will be more Democrats in place, but the body won’t include lame ducks like Sens. John Cornyn (R-Texas), Joni Ernst (R-Iowa), Thom Tillis (R-N.C.), or Susan Collins (R-Maine), who, with nothing left to lose politically, may have been willing to defy the president in a parting act of courage. This possibility has now been squandered.

This episode also raises uncomfortable questions about the readiness of Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-N.Y.) and his leadership team to assume the governing responsibilities with which the voters have entrusted them. If Jeffries and his whip operation cannot ensure every available member is present for such a consequential vote, it begs the question of whether he has the toughness and discipline to be a worthy successor to Speaker Pelosi, who long ran the caucus with exacting control.

Zooming out from this individual episode, Democrats must remember that, after the discord of the Vietnam era, it took a generation of Democrats to persuade voters that the party could be trusted with national security once again. Senators like Sam Nunn (D-Ga.), Carl Levin (D-Mich), and yes, Joe Biden (D-Del.), along with leaders like Secretary of State Madeleine Albright and Defense Secretary William Perry, rebuilt public confidence through their steady comportment and command of the issues.

That credibility was hard-won, and it can be lost far quicker than it was earned. Democrats cannot appear cavalier about a matter as grave as impeaching the secretary of Defense and expect voters to regard them as responsible stewards of America’s interests.

Democrats were able to win the recent midterms almost by default; they should recognize that voters turned out not so much to support them as cast their ballots against Trump’s follies on the Iran war, tariffs, the cost of living, and so much else. The Democrats are yet to put forward a coherent vision of their own, including on international affairs and national security, and the upcoming fights over the National Defense Authorization Act and funding restrictions on the Iran war — for which the administration wants another $70 billion — will be a key test of how Democrats handle themselves.

Republicans, for their part, are putting their most hard-nosed figures out front over the next two years, including new White House chief of staff Stephen Miller and incoming Minority Leader Jim Jordan (R-Ohio). One need not admire their politics, or even view them as competent, to recognize that these individuals have never been reluctant to show up for a fight. Democrats must match that level of focus and commitment if they are to carry out the mandate they won this November.

As President Obama urged in his 2009 inaugural address, Democrats must “set aside childish things,” like half-hearted excuses for missing pivotal votes, and start getting serious about doing their jobs. If not, their governing majorities may be short-lived and their chances of taking back the White House in 2029 will be imperiled.

Greg Pollock served in a series of leadership positions in the Office of the U.S. Secretary of Defense from 2010-2025, including twice serving as an acting deputy assistant secretary of Defense. He is now an adjunct professor at Georgetown University’s School of Foreign Service and a non-resident senior fellow at the Center for Climate and Security.
