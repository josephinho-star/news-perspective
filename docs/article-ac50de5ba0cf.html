High school football’s latest circus involves two brothers in Ohio, two Dodges and a $360K house

2026-11-21 17:55

The ugly side of high school football reared its ugly head in Ohio this week, and it didn't take place on the field.

Instead, it took place in court. That's where the really ugly stuff happens, right? It's never on the field. It's always — always — in the courts.

For those who missed it, two brothers on a powerhouse Ohio football team were ruled eligible, and then ineligible, and then eligible again, for Friday night's playoff showdown. Massillon Washington football stars Brayton and Brydon Feister were allowed to play at the last minute for last night's D-II regional final game against Bishop Watterson.

Massillon lost, 22-5. The season is over. But that's not the point.

Should these two brothers — one committed to Oregon and the other with a pile of D-1 offers — have been allowed to play at all?

That's the question the state of Ohio had been grappling with all week, and it's a situation that's all too familiar to anyone who's ever dipped their toe into the world of high school football.

Ohio high school football saga full of twists and turns

Context is obviously important here, so I'll try to summarize this situation the best I can ...

The Feisters transferred to Massillon last spring after spending last year at Akron's Archbishop Hoban High School.

According to Ohio High School Athletic Association (OHSAA) court filings, the move followed a disciplinary dispute at Hoban stemming from the state wrestling tournament in March.

According to OHSAA, the family refused to complete the disciplinary consequences and promptly relocated to Massillon a month later. OHSAA attorney Joseph Fraley also wrote that the family had been in contact with multiple schools about potential enrollment.

Still with me?

In Ohio, transferring athletes generally must sit out the second half of the season and the playoffs unless they qualify for an exception. One covers a legitimate family relocation, but the OHSAA must determine that the move created the need to transfer.

You also must also fill out documents to prove it wasn't for athletic reasons.

That's where things get ... blurry. Again, this is nothing new. I grew up during a time down here in Florida in which you couldn't just switch schools willy-nilly. You played where you were zoned. If you wanted to play for a certain school, you had to live in that zone.

Anyway, this led to months of court battles trying to determine if the brothers would be allowed to play for Massillon this season. At first, they were denied. Then a Court of Appeals issued an injunction on Oct. 16, allowing them to play the rest of the season ...

Until Thursday, when OHSAA suspended both brothers for up to 30 days while it investigated possible recruiting violations. That's where this story takes a really nasty turn. This suspension was a separate recruiting investigation.

According to the Canton Repository, it had to do with three separate purchases worth hundreds of thousands of dollars, along with recruiting questions. A source told the Repository the family had been asked to provide documentation to explain apparent cash purchases of their Massillon house and two vehicles.

Public records show the family purchased a $360,000 house in Massillon without a mortgage being recorded. They also show the family bought a $238,655 residence in Green, Ohio last year, and took out a mortgage for $226,722. It has not yet been paid off, according to public records.

The two other purchases included a Dodge Charger and Dodge Durango. The investigation has not yet produced a final finding that the family received improper benefits.

On Friday, Bo Feister, the boys' father, told News 5 that the family submitted 18 rounds of information to OHSAA, including deeds, titles, and bank statements. He also accused the association of harassing his family.

In a Nov. 5 Facebook post, Feister accused someone in a black truck of "following" his family, and claimed the person was operating under OHSAA direction.

"Our family is devastated. Our children are terrified. And frankly, we never imagined we would find ourselves in a position where we would have to publicly beg our own community for help protecting our children," Feister wrote.

Hours before Friday's playoff game, an appeals court reaffirmed its earlier order allowing the brothers to play and ordered the OHSAA to explain why it should not be held in contempt.

This is the ugly side of high school football

Whew. We all caught up now? What a saga.

Here's the sad part: this is nothing new for high school football. That's the reality. There's a reason a little show called "Friday Night Lights" was so popular back in the day.

School districts vs. parents. Alleged recruiting violations. Eligibility questions. Court battles. Injunctions and appeals. It's been around forever.

I grew up in a high school football world, long before this (horrible) NIL and transfer world we're currently in. There was a huge story years ago in my town about a kid playing for a school, but not actually living in the town. If I remember correctly, he lived two counties over, but his aunt had a house here and he was allegedly living with her.

You get the gist.

Stuff like that happened all the time growing up. It's been going on for years. I'm not saying that's what happened in Ohio, because I don't know. Obviously, OHSAA has some serious questions for the family. The family, obviously, pushed back — hard. A court agreed with them, at least for now.

This is the dark side of sports. The one we don't want to see. The one we know exists, but choose to ignore because, frankly, it's a huge bummer.

I love high school football. I've written about it all year. I used to cover it religiously in a previous life. I maintain that nothing beats a Friday night in a small town at a packed high school football stadium. It's the best. You don't get that feeling anywhere else.

If you've experienced it, you know.

But I'd be lying to you if I said it hasn't lost some of its luster over the past decade or so. It certainly has down here in Florida. Rick Scott signed HB 7029 back in 2016, and it essentially paved the way for open enrollment. It was a huge deal, and a turning point for high school athletics.

It was the transfer portal before the transfer portal became a thing. Then along came NIL in college sports. And now, you basically have a local high school football team filled with star players from all over the state — some of them working out future NIL deals during the season.

That big offensive lineman you watched throw a couple pancakes last night? He might not be from your town, and could very well be richer than you in about eight months. That's the truth.

What does any of that have to do with the situation in Ohio this week? It's all part of the ugly side of high school athletics.

Whether it's eligibility questions, alleged recruiting violations, potential harassment, or the corporatization of amateur athletics, it doesn't matter. It's all nasty. It's all ugly. It's all sad.

Yes, high school sports is losing its innocence.

In reality, I'm not sure it ever really had it.
