Work begins on 8-foot fence around LA’s notorious, drug-ridden MacArthur Park in $2.3M crackdown

2026-11-18 00:18

Los Angeles is hastily constructing a flimsy 8-foot fence around its notorious MacArthur Park in a $2.3 million bid to crack down on rampant drug dealing, crime and years of disorder.

Workers erected a silver chain-link fence Tuesday around the roughly 35-acre park in the heart of Westlake, one of LA’s poorest neighborhoods, while homeless people and their belongings remained scattered along the surrounding sidewalks.

The temporary fencing marks the start of a long-awaited project to surround the historic park with a permanent green metal barrier, allowing officials to lock its gates overnight in an effort to curb drug activity, vandalism and encampments.

The park will close nightly at 10 p.m. and reopen at 6 a.m., with police expected to sweep the grounds before closing, according to plans presented to the Los Angeles Board of Recreation and Park Commissioners in October.

Fences have become a familiar sight at Los Angeles parks struggling with homelessness, including Echo Park Lake, which was fenced off following a massive encampment clearing in 2021.

MacArthur Park has become one of the city’s most notorious examples of public spaces overwhelmed by drug trafficking, crime and homelessness.

The California Post has spent more than a year documenting the deteriorating conditions at the park, including an exclusive investigation that uncovered staggering amounts of trash and hazardous waste removed during city cleanup operations.

The Post also exposed the park’s deadly fentanyl crisis, documenting rampant open-air drug use, frequent overdoses and a notorious stretch dubbed “Fentanyl Alley,” where people were regularly found passed out on sidewalks.

The investigation also uncovered a city-funded nonprofit that distributed syringes, safer-smoking kits and the overdose-reversal drug Narcan.

The Post also reported on a major federal crackdown in May, when the Drug Enforcement Administration and LAPD targeted a drug trafficking network operating around the park.

The park falls under the watch of Democratic Socialists of America-backed Councilwoman Eunisses Hernandez, who has repeatedly defended her approach to homelessness, emphasizing housing, social services and harm-reduction programs over enforcement alone.

Hernandez has supported city-funded programs distributing needles and other supplies to drug users in the area. She also backs the new fencing project, telling Spectrum News in November 2025 that it responds to community demands for a cleaner, safer park.

“The community has been asking for a clean, safe space where everyone can access,” Hernandez said. “This will allow the park to be able to breathe.”

Raul Claros, founder of California Rising who challenged Hernandez for her council seat in the last election cycle, told The Post that residents want more than just a fence to reclaim the troubled park.

“The community has been clear on its demands,” Claros said, calling for an end to the distribution of free drug pipes and needles around the park, with services instead provided at St. Vincent Medical Center.

He also called for permanent park rangers, increased LAPD patrols around surrounding residential streets and keeping Wilshire Boulevard open as the fencing project moves forward.

The permanent barrier is expected to feature 17 pedestrian entrances, allowing the park to remain accessible during daytime hours while restricting overnight access.

Construction is expected to continue through fall 2027, with the park remaining open during the work.

The post reached out to Hernandez for comment on the new fence.
