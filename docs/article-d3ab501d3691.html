Warning Burnham’s social care plan could cost far more than he says, and take ‘decades’ to implement – UK politics live

2026-09-28 08:55

Burnham's social care plan could cost far more than he says, and take 'many decades' to implement, leading expert claims

Andy Burnham’s plans to reform social care could cost far more than he is claiming and take “many decades” to be implemented, a leading expert has claimed.

Andrew Dilnot told the Today programme this morning that he was “puzzled” by some of what Burnham said yesterday, and he implied that he was unconvinced by what Burnham was offering.

Dilnot, a former chair of the UK Statistics Authority, is one of the nation’s leading economists and chaired a commission on social care reform for David Cameron’s government in 2010. Although ministers were initially positive about his plan to cap the amount individuals can be required to pay for their social care, successive governments were worried about the cost and the plan was never implemented.

In his interview, Dilnot said that he did not understand why Burnham told the BBC yesterday that his plan would cost less than £18bn a year when what he seemed to be describing – an NHS-style care service, with care free at the point of delivery – would cost a lot more.

Dilnot said he was “puzzled” by Burnham’s answer to the £18bn question because “if we were to make social care free in the same way that health care is largely free, then I think we will be talking about an amount of money well in excess of £18bn.”

Dilnot said that making the English social care system similar to Scotland’s – where personal care is free, but where people who need to go into a care home can still face huge costs for the accommodation element of their care – might cost roughly half of the £18bn mentioned. But that was not what Burnham seemed to be proposing, Dilnot said. He explained:

[Burnham] did talk about making social care free, and what he implied was that he wanted social care to be completely free.

And that’s not at all the case in Scotland, where although the policy is called free personal care, we still have exactly the problem that Mr Burnham himself has talked about of people needing residential care faced with very substantial bills.

And so I think what he’s really talking about probably is social care that looks very like the NHS, that will be entirely free.

What he hasn’t described is how he would raise the funds that he would need for that, and that would be a very substantial sum of money.

Dilnot said that, when Burnham gave a big speech on social care just after becoming PM, he left the impression there could be legislation before the next election.

After yesterday, Dilnot said, reform seems further away. He explained:

It sounds as though there’ll be some more details [of the plans] in the prime minister’s speech tomorrow at his party conference, but I think even that will continue to remain vague.

I think what we learned yesterday was that, if anything, change is now further away than had been implied or suggested.

Dilnot also said the full reform proposed by Burnham could take decades.

I also think that there was an indication in what [Burnham] said that he’s imagining something that could take many years, even many decades before it came to fruition.

So they are now, I suspect, looking at ways of beginning a move in this direction without imagining they can get there very quickly.

To keep the list going, here are further announcements from the party over the past 24 hours.

British businesses will have more opportunities to win contracts overseas and bring jobs, investment and growth back to communities across the UK under new plans announced by chancellor John Healey.

The new UK Export Finance (UKEF) pilot product, Flexible Finance, is the latest step in the Labour government’s mission to back British industry and help businesses seize opportunities in some of the world’s fastest-growing markets. Under the scheme, select buyers operating in economies such as Brazil, Morocco and Mexico, will be able to access loans on more flexible terms, creating new incentives to buy British goods and services.

The government has committed up to £115m to build this new vessel as part of its drive to reindustrialise the UK and support our shipyards.

It will lead a new national system for monitoring the UK’s seas, bringing together marine surveys, data and technology to help manage fisheries and the marine environment more effectively.

At 85 metres long and able to accommodate more than 40 people, the vessel will carry out a wide range of scientific work. This includes assessing fish stocks, mapping the seabed, monitoring pollution, and responding quickly to marine incidents.

6) Three new floating docks for the Royal Navy’s submarine service at HM Naval Base Clyde. See 7.54am.

8) A new legal route for refugees that prioritises Afghan women and girls and Palestinians. There are more details in this story by Jessica Elgot and Rajeev Syal.

Labour members would rather do deal with Lib Dems than Greens, poll suggests

Labour members would rather do a deal with the Liberal Democrats than the Green party in the event of a hung parliament, according to YouGov polling for Sky News.

In its write-up, Sky says:

Some 85% [of Labour members] would back Labour doing a deal of some kind if they were the largest party but didn’t win a majority.

This includes 75% backing a deal with the Lib Dems, 66% with the Greens, and 6% with the Tories.

It is not clear from these figures if Labour members favour the Lib Dems over the Greens for policy or personal reasons, or because they assume the Lib Dems would have more seats, implying a bigger potential majority.

YouGov, Survation and More in Common have all published MRP polls in recent days suggesting that, if there were an election now, Labour would be the largest party, but short of a majority.

The polling for Sky also found that 67% of Labour members backed Burnham saying the next election will not be held until 2029.

Getting rid of triple lock would not raise enough now to fund PM's social care reform plans, Resolution Foundation chief says

There is increasing speculation that Andy Burnham might announce plans to scrap the pensions triple lock as one means of funding his social care reforms. Yesterday Darren Jones, the former chief secretary to the PM under Keir Starmer, told the BBC that this was “an option the government should consider alongside others”. According to reports in the Daily Telegraph and in the i, other Labour figures are also privately arguing this would be a good idea. One “insider” told the i that getting rid of the triple lock but making social care free could be sold as “taking [something] away when you’re in your 60s but giving it back when you’re 80”.

But Ruth Curtice, chief executive of the Resolution Foundation thinktank, and a former Treasury official, told the Today programme this morning that getting rid of the triple lock would not raise enough to fund the sort of proposal Burnham has in mind any time soon.

The triple lock is a policy that has the value of pensions growing by more than earnings. That’s particularly unsustainable. But obviously taking it away would hurt the very generation who would benefit from free social care.

I don’t think one would be enough to pay for the other. For example, in this forecast period, we think cancelling the triple lock would save about £800m, maybe a bit less.

The numbers for the triple lock are very big in the long run. They’re not enough to deliver a free social care system in five or 10 years’ time.

Tories accuses Healey of offering 'reheated announcements' and 'hot air' in conference speech

Andrew Griffith, the shadow chancellor, has accused John Healey of offering the Labour conference “reheated announcements” in his speech later.

Referring to Labour briefing overnight that three new floating docks will go ahead on the Clyde (see 7.54am), Griffith said:

These are more reheated announcements without clarity on where the money is coming from. Additional docks were announced months ago in the defence investment plan, which still has a £4.7bn black hole.

John Healey resigned warning that Labour’s failure to fund defence was making Britain unsafe. Now that he is chancellor, he still won’t fund the 3% target he walked out over.

Labour are running scared of making the tough choices needed to pay for Britain’s defence. All they can offer is reannounced docks and more hot air from Healey.

Chancellor John Healey to promise ‘new age of industrialisation’ in speech to Labour conference

Good morning. John Healey, the chancellor, is the main speaker at the Labour conference. He was a surprise appointment to replace Rachel Reeves, he is unveiling his budget four weeks on Wednesday, and some of the pre-briefing suggests when the budget does come, it will be a relatively low-key affair, with a lot of decisions postponed until the spending review next year. And so we are definitely not expecting huge announcements at lunchtime today.

But there will be some striking language. Healey is going to promise a “new age of industrialisation”.

According to the overnight briefing from Labour, he will explain that in these terms.

My politics were shaped by the industrial heartlands of South Yorkshire. Today, on the site of the Orgreave pit, is Rotherham’s Advanced Manufacturing Park. Our coal mines are not coming back. But this is how Britain’s industrial past is being remade now for the modern world. This is the new age of industrialisation. A new confidence in Britain.

And he will also announce that the government is commissioning “three new floating docks for the Royal Navy’s submarine service at HM Naval Base Clyde” to provide maintenance for nuclear submarines.

Known as Programme Euston, plans for the new docks were first set out in 2023 and are expected to upgrade Faslane’s facilities for the next generation of British submarines, the Press Association reports. The Ministry of Defence has previously said they are expected to come into service in the early 2030s, and the docks form part of a wider £15bn upgrade programme for the Royal Navy’s shipyards.

10.30am: Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary, addresses the conference.

11.10am: Jonathan Reynolds, the business secretary, speaks at the start of a debate on workers’ rights and reindustrialisation.

Other cabinet ministers speaking at fringe events include Lisa Nandy, the culture secretary, at a Think Labour event; Pat McFadden, the work and pensions secretary, at an IPPR event; Streeting at a BAE event; and Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary, recording a What’s Left? podcast.

After 3.20pm: Alex Norris, the justice secretary, and Shabana Mahmood, the home secretary, speak at the conference in a debate covering crime.
