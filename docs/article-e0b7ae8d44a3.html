Cranberries have long ruled Thanksgiving — but a juicy newcomer is spicing things up

2026-11-25 03:18

With Thanksgiving this week, I’ve been thinking that maybe it’s time to rebrand the red corner of our holiday tables. I proudly present the plump pomegranate. The cranberry has long monopolized the holiday spotlight, and it’s time to consider the pomegranate — which is infinitely more versatile.

The cranberry is part of the American Thanksgiving I grew up with. The pomegranate is part of the Iranian cooking my parents brought with them from Tehran. One of my favorite things about Thanksgiving is that a dish can come from halfway around the world and feel right at home next to the turkey.

And if you think the daughter of two Tehranis can’t give the cranberry a fair hearing, let’s put the evidence on the table. For starters, the rich cranberry requires a heavy helping of sugar to cut the bitter tartness it’s famous for, while pomegranates deliver a juicy pop of tart and sweet notes with every bite.

And while the nutrient-dense, low-calorie, antioxidant and fiber-packed cranberry is great too, this other red fruit has been around for at least 5,000 years – pomegranates have been a prized and preferred fruit among the Assyrian, Persian, Greek, Roman, Islamic and Chinese empires.

And with fall upon us, California pomegranates are in season through Thanksgiving.

As a kid and teen on the East Coast, I knew fall had arrived when my late grandfather, Elias, brought me bushels upon bushels of juicy red pomegranates to eat after school.

In my early adolescence and adulthood in Southern California, I learned to watch for them at the farmers markets where you can still find them come Thanksgiving.

I’d happily try a slice of juicy turkey with fesenjan, the Persian stew made with walnuts and pomegranate molasses, then topped with fresh pomegranate arils. One summer in college, my mom offered to make me a welcome-home meal.

“Anything you want, delbar,” she said, calling me by my nickname. Of course, I asked for fesenjan. She scoffed as I’d picked one of the most labor-intensive Iranian dishes one could make. It was her first time making it and it was worth asking for.

In Gurinder Chadha’s film What’s Cooking?, four ethnically diverse families in LA’s Fairfax district make Thanksgiving dinner. When one mother puts chili paste on the turkey, her daughter asks why it has to taste like everything else they eat.

At our house, I’d be the one asking where the saffron went. At our Thanksgiving table, the turkey came with stuffing scented with cumin and saffron and jeweled rice topped with candied orange peel, pistachios and barberries. I still like a little cumin in my candied yams and a dash of turmeric in my mashed potatoes.

All of this reminds me that our culinary heritage truly shapes what we crave when we gather. And I’m certain no matter which corner of our world a person hails from, there is something here that resonates.

Perhaps the real argument here, then, is realizing the beauty in blending culinary cultures.

I’d miss the saffron if it disappeared from our stuffing. It’s a flavor my parents grew up with in Iran, and now it’s part of what Thanksgiving tastes like to me.

And I believe that’s symbolic of our ability, as humans, to transcend our differences and instead complement one another’s strengths. Your way of doing something might actually bring out the best in mine, and mine might do the same for yours.

So, while I’ve spent this entire column seemingly trying to run the mighty cranberry off the Thanksgiving table, I’ve realized I don’t actually want to declare a winner. Instead, I want to stage a merger.

This Thanksgiving, I’ll be stirring a little pomegranate molasses into the cranberry sauce, letting it cool, and then folding in fresh pomegranate arils with a dash of cinnamon, cloves and perhaps even orange zest. The cranberries provide a lovely tartness, while the pomegranate molasses adds depth, the spices add warmth, and the arils provide that delightful little pop with each bite.

I started out trying to unseat the cranberry. Turns out I just wanted it to marry Persian.
