Die Linke wins Berlin election, putting Elif Eralp in contention for mayor

2026-09-20 20:11

The far-left Die Linke party emerged as the clear winner in Berlin’s state election on Sunday, according to exit polls, making its lead candidate, Elif Eralp, a top contender to become the German capital’s next governing mayor.

Eralp and her party campaigned on promises of sweeping measures to address Berlin’s housing crisis and reduce rents, including a controversial proposal to expropriate apartments held by large corporate landlords.

The daughter of Turkish immigrants, Eralp also presented herself as the candidate best placed to counter the rise of the far-right, anti-immigration Alternative for Germany (AfD).

“I believe it would indeed send a powerful signal if someone with a migration background were to become governing mayor of Berlin,” she said on the eve of the vote.

Celebrating on Sunday, she exclaimed: “In our city, love and justice have triumphed over hatred.”

Eralp — whose party wants to reduce social inequality and the power of big business — has been compared to Zohran Mamdani, the charismatic left-wing outsider who became mayor of New York.

“Naturally, the success of a democratic socialist in New York — the heartland of capitalism — is a major success,” Eralp said on Friday, calling Mamdani “a great role model.”

Die Linke’s victory, with about 25 percent of the vote according to exit polls, was a historic first in Berlin and dealt a blow to the center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU) of embattled Chancellor Friedrich Merz, which came second with about 20 percent.

To govern, however, Eralp must now persuade other parties — most likely the center-left Social Democrats (SPD) and the Greens — to join her in a so-called red-red-green coalition.

SPD lead candidate Steffen Krach has already made clear that he opposes Die Linke’s expropriation proposal. The party traces some of its roots to the ruling party of communist East Germany.

Germany’s mainstream parties have many other differences with Die Linke, particularly over security policy. The party opposes military aid for Ukraine and calls for a European security order without NATO.

Die Linke has also faced criticism over anti-Israel statements by some district-level figures — an especially sensitive issue in Germany because of its history of Nazi rule and the Holocaust.

Eralp has repeatedly emphasized her desire to support and foster Jewish life in Berlin.

However, fierce criticism of Israel from some local branches of Die Linke, particularly in Berlin’s Neukölln neighborhood, which is home to many Arab and Muslim immigrants, has drawn negative headlines.

A performance at a local Die Linke campaign event in Neukölln in August caused outrage after a performer advocated an “intifada” — uprising — and chanted “Death to the IDF!” in reference to the Israel Defense Forces.

Eralp distanced herself from the incident, but it drew fierce criticism from the Greens and the SPD and again prompted the press to question whether Die Linke was fit to govern.

During the campaign, the Greens appeared to favor a coalition with Die Linke over the other obvious possibility of joining forces with the conservative CDU.

However, there are dissenting voices within the SPD, which has a history of close ties to organized labor and working-class voters.

The SPD chose to end a left-wing coalition after the previous election in 2023 and is currently the junior partner in a coalition with the CDU.

Eralp now has a few days or weeks to convince her potential coalition partners that she has her party’s Berlin branch under control and is suited to govern.

Eralp’s parents, who were active in socialist politics and trade unions in Turkey, fled to Germany following the 1980 military coup, when her mother was eight months pregnant.

Born in Munich, Eralp studied law in Hamburg and later worked to defend migrants’ rights. She was hired as a staffer for Die Linke’s parliamentary group in the German parliament in 2010 and moved permanently to Berlin soon afterward.

She lives in the trendy Kreuzberg district with her husband and two children and often emphasizes that, like most Berliners, they rent their apartment rather than own their home.
