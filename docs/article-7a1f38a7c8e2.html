Holiday scammers hook older Americans first, then return later to drain their savings, expert warns

2026-11-26 11:00

As the holiday season gets underway, scammers are targeting older Americans with costly schemes that could deplete their entire life savings, often returning to exploiting the same victim more than once.

The risk of elders falling victim to these schemes rises during the holidays. A November 2026 U.S. Secret Service public advisory shared with Fox News Digital stated that scammers consider the season one of their most profitable times of the year.

"USSS is better known for its protective mission," USSS Investigative Analyst Bre Whalen, CFE, told Fox News Digital. "Most people are unaware USSS conducts FI crimes investigations throughout the world, such as Operation Maple Star. Because of its partnerships with the banking community, we've developed close contacts over time."

Why holidays are lucrative for scammers

Federal authorities warn that scammers exploit the holiday rush and heightened emotions as older adults shop online, track packages online, travel and donate to charities.

An AARP holiday survey released in November found that 84% of U.S. adults had encountered a scam or fraud attempt ahead of the holidays. Nearly 39% of those who purchased something through an online advertisement said they were scammed, according to the survey. Around 47% received fake package-delivery alerts, while 35% bought or received a gift card with a stolen balance.

Americans ages 60 and older reported around $3 billion in fraud losses in 2025, according to an October 2026 Federal Trade Commission report. The agency estimated that older victims lost as much as $119.5 billion last year, with victims reporting only a fraction of the scams.

Why older Americans are repeated targets

The Justice Department reports that scammers target older Americans an average of nine times. After taking money in an initial scheme, fraudsters often contact victims again, posing as officials promising to recover their funds.

"Criminals will go back to where the money is," Amy Nofziger, senior director of fraud victim support at AARP's Fraud Watch Network, told Fox News Digital.

"It's just why some banks get robbed every single time — the same bank — because the criminals go where the money is, and older adults hold the majority of wealth in the United States."

The process can still be challenging, experts said. Winning the confidence of victims can be difficult after they’ve been targeted and scammed.

"Victims don't trust or believe what we're saying because they've been coached by these scam centers to not answer law enforcement and think we're not real," Whalen stated.

One holiday scam leads to another

The Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network said that after a successful scam, some fraudsters will share victims' personal information on the black market. This allows other criminals to target the same person, but with different schemes.

For instance, when an older shopper places an order for a fake Black Friday ad or donates to a fraudulent holiday charity site, scammers take their information and place them on "sucker lists." The lists mark these victims as easy targets, with repeated attacks that could ultimately wipe out their life savings.

"Criminals are very adept at sharing information," Nofziger said. "A lot of these criminals don't work in isolation. They work in crime rings. Perhaps Criminal A steals from a victim on a Monday, and then five Mondays later, they give or sell that information to Criminal B to try to manipulate them on another scam, knowing they still might have resources available to steal."

Recovery scams bring criminals back

Promises to recover stolen money or lost funds are one of the most damaging schemes to older Americans during the holiday season.

After a victim loses money, another scammer may call and claim to work for a government agency, or pose as an attorney or a fraud-recovery investigator. The caller then promises to investigate the crime, find the money and get a refund.

Fraudsters will also exploit tax and financial deadlines while pushing victims to wire an immediate "processing fee" before Dec. 31 to recover lost funds or unlock a refund.

Federal authorities warn that legitimate agencies never require upfront fees to recover stolen funds, but victims often fall prey to these schemes when they become outraged and want swift justice.

"For the initial victim, they're so angry, they're so upset, they want to get justice for this," Nofziger said. "They think that this is their way out, oftentimes not really understanding that it's just a secondary scam that's happening."

Scammers are exploiting holiday fears with AI-generated emergency calls that mimic relatives and payment-app requests designed to steal money.

One scheme, known as the "grandparent scam," happens when criminals pull videos from social media accounts or public sources. They clone a relative's voice using artificial intelligence, and then pose as loved ones asking for emergency funds or Christmas cash.

Fraudsters are also using full AI video deepfakes, sometimes posing as physicians, to bilk funds from unsuspecting older Americans.

In December 2025, an 82-year-old Florida grandmother lost more than $200,000 to an AI deepfake scam, when someone pretended to be a doctor in a Facebook community focused on autism.

The grandmother said she had been saving her money for her autistic grandson’s future, and the fake physician urged people to invest.

"It looked completely real — his face, his voice, everything," she told First Coast News. "I wanted to save more money for my grandson, and so I fell for it hook, line and sinker."

"I got very emotional and so angry with myself that I had difficulty functioning. I lost 20 pounds and couldn’t sleep because it went through my mind over and over again."

Shame can keep victims silent, which ends up leaving them vulnerable to repeat attacks, Nofziger said, adding that victims may feel guilt for being scammed in the first place.

Nofziger urges families to discuss these scams openly, while taking steps to avoid victim-blaming.

"The scammers are masters of manipulation, and they know how to manipulate human beings and their feelings," she said. "The victim has been manipulated and technically, you know, brainwashed by this criminal for months and years."

"It took years to get there. It might take a long time to unravel that emotional grasp that these criminals have on their victims... The person who should be embarrassed is the criminal who stole the money from them."

She added that unraveling a scammer's manipulative hold takes time and outside support, whether from local law enforcement, support groups or counselors.

Can victims get their money back?

Victims are urged to contact their banks, payment providers, and local law enforcement immediately if they suspect fraud. Banks can possibly stop or recall some transfers, but it largely depends on the payment method and how quickly they report the scam.

Cryptocurrency, gift cards, cash and gold are more difficult to recover since criminals typically know how to move them quickly. Once a victim is ensnared, scammers can move the money through multiple accounts and intermediaries, such as money mules, to hide transactions.

USSS advises victims to immediately report any suspected fraud to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov.

"Current fraud info is provided real time; in most cases we have just minutes to act on this information before the fraud is committed," Whalen said.

"We work closely with local police and banking industry, although we are still working to make efforts to partner with some of the banking sector that is slow to respond to real-time data."

Anyone of any age, including non-members, can contact the free AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline at 877-908-3360 for guidance, support groups and to learn about other steps to take.

Victims and families can also contact the National Elder Fraud Hotline at 833-FRAUD-11 or visit the Justice Department’s Elder Justice Initiative website.
