No 10: Hegseth ‘wrong’ to say most non-US Nato soldiers didn’t fight in Afghanistan – UK politics live

2026-10-19 15:39

No 10 says US defence secretary Pete Hegseth 'wrong' to say most non-US Nato soldiers in Afghanistan didn't fight

Downing Street has delivered a rare public reprimand to a member of Donald Trump’s administration, saying Pete Hegseth, the US defence secretary, was “wrong” to suggest that most non-American Nato soldiers in Afghanistan did not do any fighting.

Hegseth made the comment in a speech at the University of Tennessee, where he recalled serving in Afghanistan himself. He said:

I was a captain in Afghanistan and we would go to the headquarters in Kabul and there would be 50 flags at the headquarters. It was a beautiful thing to see, 50 different countries contributing to the Nato alliance and the Isaf [International Security Assistance Force] mission there.

Except there were maybe five that contributed. It was actually closer to one or two that by the end were actually involved in kinetic operations. In fact the patch Isaf, we joked it meant ‘I Saw Americans Fighting’.

Asked about Hegseth’s comment at the Downing Street lobby briefing, the PM’s spokesperson said Hegseth was “wrong” in what he said about the Nato operation in Afghanistan.

The spokesperson explained:

The facts speak for themselves when it comes to the UK’s and Nato’s contribution to Afghanistan. Thousands of British and Allied families made huge sacrifices to play their significant part in that war and uphold our values, and more than 450 British personnel never returned.

Their sacrifice and that of other Nato forces was made in the service of collective security and in response to an attack on our ally. We are incredibly proud of our armed forces and their service and sacrifice will never be forgotten, and it is wrong to diminish their role.

While the UK government disagrees with much of what Donald Trump and his team say and do, there is normally a reluctance to criticise them in public. However, this does not seem to apply when the reputation of British servicemen and women has been impugned. Keir Starmer tried hard to maintain a good personal relationship with the US president, but when Trump made a similar claim about Nato allies avoiding frontline fighting in Afghanistan, Starmer condemned what he said as “insulting and frankly appalling”.

Welsh first minister invites Burnham to agree 'new partnership for growth' with Wales

Rhun ap Iorwerth, the Welsh first minister, will give a speech later inviting Andy Burnham to agree to a “new partnership'” between Wales and the UK government on growth. The two governments should also avoid “duplicating activity”, he will say.

According to a report for Nation.Cymru, ap Iorwerth will say:

I will invite the prime minister to agree a new partnership for growth between our two governments – a practical agreement about how our two governments work together to unlock Wales’s economic potential …

My message to the prime minister will be a simple one – let us spend less time designing new programmes and duplicating activity. Let us spend more time unlocking opportunity and supporting investment, and more time delivering the growth we both want to see in all parts of our respective nations.

The Green party has reacted with alarm to reports suggesting the Alan Milburn review of Neets will recommend lower sickness benefits for people under the age of 25.

Pointing out that the recent review from Prof Peter Fonagy into the prevalence of mental health conditions, autism and ADHD argued cutting benefits was bad for mental health, the Green party’s co-deputy leader Mothin Ali said:

It seems that when you commission an expert like Prof Fonagy rather than a Blairite former minister, you get a far more coherent analysis of the issues affecting young people and their mental health …

The Green party has a proper jobs guarantee for young people – real jobs, with real training and rights at work. It would be funded by those who can afford to contribute, not by cutting support for those least able to cope.

Streeting rejects call from Tory MP for UK to sell Diego Garcia military base to Trump

During defence questions, Edward Leigh, the Conservative MP and father of the Commons, asked Wes Streeting about the Diego Garcia military base on the Chagos Islands. Given that it was “to all intents and purposes an American military base”, why didn’t the government just sell it to Donald Trump for £6bn, and invest the money in defence?

Streeting said that he didn’t think that was the right solution.

In an earlier answer, Streeting defended the government’s plan to transfer sovereignty of the Chagos Islands to Mauritius as a response the legal doubt over the UK’s claims of ownership. He said:

Having looked at this whole issue with a fresh pair of eyes, I’ve reached the same conclusion as every prime minister since Boris Johnson, including this one, which is the capabilities provided by that base makes it extremely unwise and a risk not worth taking to have legal uncertainty. That is why efforts to negotiate a treaty continue.

Israeli general election highlights flaws with PR, David Blunkett claims

This morning Daisy Cooper cited Andy Burnham’s support for proportional representation as evidence that the Liberal Democrats are “winning the battle of ideas”. (See 11am.)

But only up to a point. There are a couple of reports around this morning that show that, while Burnham may personally favour PR, he is yet to convincingly win the argument for it in the parliamentary Labour party.

In an article for PoliticsHome,Harriet Symonds and Matilda Martin point out that some of Burnham’s most senior ministers prefer first-past-the-post. They say:

PoliticsHome understands that Louise Haigh, deputy PM and Burnham’s close ally, has concerns about shifting to PR.

Other Cabinet members have previously voiced reservations about the idea.

In 2022, Angela Rayner, now secretary of state for housing, communities and local government, said she was a “first-past-the-post girl” who was “yet to be convinced by PR”, telling The News Agents podcast that it could allow extremist parties such as the British National Party (BNP) to win House of Commons seats.

And David Blunkett, the former cabinet minister who has been one of Burnham’s political mentors, has written an article for the i restating his longstanding opposition to PR. He says:

In my view, the argument can’t be that “every vote must count”. Each vote already does count, because in order to have winners and losers, people engage in the process which inevitably means that some voters’ preferences will be cancelled out by a greater number preferring another candidate.

Not everyone can have prizes in a democracy. Some candidates lose, some electors are frustrated and annoyed that their candidate didn’t succeed. Hence, the first set of questions about what it is we think this change is going to improve. The second question is why we would believe that electoral reform would change people’s interest or engagement with politics?

Later this month we will have a demonstration of what is the purest form of proportional representation in the world. Namely, the Israeli general election. If there were ever an example of how tiny minorities representing not only outlandish views, but providing a blockage on progress, compromise and long-term outcomes, it has to be this form of PR.

Streeting tells MPs that US still providing 'vital support' to Ukraine, and it 'does know which side it's on'

Wes Streeting told MPs that the US is still on the side of Ukraine in its war against Russia.

During defence questions, the Lib Dem MP James MacCleary asked about reports that the Ukrainian military were left exposed last week after President Trump reportedly intervened to restrict access to the Starlink internet service for its weapons.

It is worth pointing out, after what was unquestionably a challenging week for relations between the United States and Ukraine, that the United States has and does and will continue to provide vital support to Ukraine, whether that’s intelligence sharing or indeed the work we do on procurement with and through the United States, as well as the diplomatic track.

Referring to a meeting he had with Pete Hegseth, his US counterpart, last week, Streeting went on:

This is worth reporting to the house; Pete Hegseth, my US counterpart, was keen to thank the United Kingdom for our leadership of Ukraine.

That would suggest to me the United States does know which side it’s on.

Streeting confirms army has to had cancel some training exercises as cost-cutting measure

Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, is taking questions in the Commons.

When the Reform UK MP Danny Kruger asked him if he agreed it was “absolutely scandalous” that non-essential training had been cancelled by the army, Streeting said that training exercises were taking place for the rest of this year. But he accepted that some future training had been cut.

When I arrived in the Ministry of Defence, there was an in-year overspend that would have seen us overcommitted by the end of the year.

And, as I did at health, I make absolutely no apology whatsoever for sending a very strong message very early on that, while this government is delivering the biggest sustained increase in defence spending since the end of the cold war, I also expect my department and our armed forces to live within their means.
