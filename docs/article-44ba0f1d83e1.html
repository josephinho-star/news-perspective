4 essential lessons we learned from the government’s dangerous war on ‘disinformation’

2026-11-19 10:00

In 2021, the White House issued a national strategy in which it asserted that "a crisis of disinformation and misinformation often channeled through social media platforms" was fueling "extreme polarization" that could lead to domestic terrorism. White House personnel sent messages to social media platforms suggesting their failure to remove or limit speech would lead to violence. Public scrutiny focused on these efforts when the Department of Homeland Security launched its short-lived Disinformation Governance Board in 2022.

A newly released staff report from the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board ( PCLOB) examines this ill-informed campaign against "disinformation" on social media. In keeping with PCLOB’s mandate to ensure the federal government's efforts to prevent terrorism are balanced with the need to protect Americans’ privacy and civil liberties, the report evaluates the attempt to link disinformation to domestic terrorism and analyzes what policies were in place to prevent speech suppression.

After a four-year investigation, PCLOB was provided no evidence to support the contention that disinformation fueled domestic terrorism. Nevertheless, former government actors encouraged social media companies to label questionable content as false, limit users’ ability to find it, and, in some cases, completely remove the content or the speakers’ accounts from their platforms altogether.

In January 2025, President Donald Trump issued an executive order to end this practice. But we are in danger of repeating the mistakes of this disturbing chapter if Americans fail to learn four key lessons from it.

First, speech suppression is an extreme response to perceived disinformation

As PCLOB’s report explains, there is a range of government responses to disinformation that vary in their intrusiveness and civil liberties impact. Affirmative government speech is, and has historically been, a generally accepted and appropriate reaction to information the government considers to be wrong and dangerous.

The government may state what it believes to be true and do so with the full authority it can bring to bear. But government efforts to encourage third-party social media platforms privately to "enforce their own terms of service" by removing or throttling speech with which the government does not agree are highly concerning.

Publicly available documents now confirm that government actors took steps to encourage social media platforms to suppress broad types of speech without considering whether the speaker was foreign or domestic, and without considering whether the speech was protected. They did not, for example, only target speech that constituted a "true threat" or incited what the Supreme Court calls "imminent lawless action."

Compounding the civil liberties concerns, these government efforts to suppress speech largely occurred privately — often through direct communication between government officials and social media platforms. Were it not for lawsuits that forced discovery of these communications, the public likely never would have known the scope of the government’s involvement.

Second, avoidance of explicit government coercion is insufficient to protect civil liberties

Government lawyers responded to the lawsuits and the revelation of the (at best, unseemly) emails as they would be expected to do: by issuing guidance to ensure future communications were lawful. That primarily meant advising agencies and actors not to be "coercive."

The clear thrust of many of these policies was that the executive branch believed that it was both appropriate — and in many instances advisable — for government actors to persuade social media companies to monitor or limit certain speech so long as the communications were framed as suggestions.

It is of course questionable whether social media platforms would ever really find an email from the White House to be a suggestion, no matter how many disclaimers are appended to it. But even if they did, that does not make such communications good policy.

The government may state what it believes to be true and do so with the full authority it can bring to bear. But government efforts to encourage third-party social media platforms privately to "enforce their own terms of service" by removing or throttling speech with which the government does not agree are highly concerning.

And it would undoubtedly be terrible policy for the government to send to social media platforms secret lists of statements it believes to be wrong, accompanied by secret lists of accounts purveying these wrong statements. Whether such actions are lawful or not, the government should not be in the business of policing Americans’ speech.

Third, efforts to counter foreign malign influence can quickly turn to countering disinformation more broadly

The social media engagement practices that were utilized first to counter disinformation from malign foreign actors were, unfortunately, easily translated to countering domestic terrorism. The risk that the need to fight terrorism would be used to justify curtailing civil liberties is well recognized. Indeed, it is the reason that the 9/11 Commission recommended the creation of PCLOB.

And it is why the commission recommended that "[t]he burden of proof for retaining a particular governmental power should be on the executive, to explain [both] that the power actually materially enhances security and that there is adequate supervision of the executive’s use of the powers to ensure protection of civil liberties. … If the power is granted, there must be adequate guidelines and oversight to properly confine its use."

Here, it was not at all clear that disinformation fueled domestic terrorism, or that it did so on any significant scale. Nor is it evident that government efforts to suppress such speech would enhance national security.

Fourth, adequate policies and oversight do not currently exist to confine the government in any future efforts

Trump’s executive order sets a clear direction against government efforts to pressure social media platforms to curtail speech. But prohibitions on attempts to suppress Americans’ lawful speech are not clearly stated in current agency policy and are not codified into law. That makes it relatively easy for the government to reverse course in the future and resume the types of counter-disinformation efforts described in PCLOB’s report.

Americans should not have to suspect — or fear — that the government is encouraging private companies to censor their speech. To effectively protect a nation, the government must perpetually prove itself worthy of trust.
