The Atlantic hurricane season has been so quiet it’s unprecedented

2026-09-30 16:40

We’ve never had an Atlantic hurricane season without a single hurricane through the end of September—until now.

That record underscores how remarkable the season has been with an exceptionally strong El Niño—likely to become the biggest in living memory—keeping a lid on tropical cyclone activity in the Atlantic while supercharging it in the eastern Pacific (and giving Hawaii one of its busiest seasons on record).

“No hurricanes through September is unprecedented in the modern record, and it’s a little surreal to look at the maps every day this month and see the deep tropics basically shut off,” says meteorologist Michael Lowry.

There isn’t a single other hurricane season in the reliable records where there wasn’t at least one hurricane from sometime between the June 1 start of the season and the end of September. That record is particularly jarring because September is usually when tropical cyclone activity peaks in the Atlantic: at that time, ocean waters have become plenty warm after summer, and the cross-cutting winds that can stymie storms tend to relax.

Though ocean waters across the Atlantic are amply warm right now, those cross-cutting winds—known as wind shear—aren’t abating. That’s thanks to El Niño. This cyclical climate phenomenon happens because of an eastward shift in warm ocean waters in the Pacific, although it has worldwide effects on the weather. The movement of warm waters, in turn, changes where heat enters the atmosphere, which alters the motions of atmospheric currents. In the Atlantic, that means there is more wind shear and air that pushes downward, both of which stymie cyclone development.

It’s possible this record could later be overturned. As Lowry wrote in his blog, Tropical Storm Fay, which began as a tropical depression far out in the Atlantic on Sept. 19, came close to reaching the hurricane threshold of 75-mile-per-hour winds, and one satellite estimate measured winds of 84 mph on Sept. 20. But that method of measuring winds tends to produce falsely high readings, Lowry notes. The National Hurricane Center will examine the data more closely after the season to see if it needs to revise Fay’s status to a hurricane.

If it is designated a hurricane, it would still set the mark for the latest hurricane formation ever in the Atlantic in reliable records. Either way, it’s a hurricane season unlike any Lowry can recall.

“We expected a notably down year activity-wise across the Atlantic due primarily to the record-setting El Niño, but none of us in the hurricane space could've confidently predicted it'd be this dead through September,” Lowry says.

As we move away from the peak of the season, it becomes harder for hurricanes to spin up. So, assuming Fay doesn’t get the hurricane nod later, we could potentially go the rest of the season—until Nov. 30—without a hurricane. It’s never happened before, but if it did, this would be one of the likeliest seasons to do it.

As disorienting as the current weather is, it’s a relief to communities that have been hard-hit in recent years. “After a decade of active to hyperactive hurricane seasons with some of our costliest and deadliest U.S. hurricanes,” Lowry says, “this is one record I'm relieved to see!”
