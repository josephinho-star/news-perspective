Look who’s out to push weight-loss drugs like Ozempic off the market

2026-11-26 01:12

A staggering 12% of American adults — including Oprah Winfrey, Elon Musk, Serena Williams and Whoopi Goldberg — take semaglutide drugs like Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound and Mounjaro to lose weight or treat their diabetes.

But access to these innovative drugs could be over soon, if the trial lawyers get their way.

Adding to the concern, on Nov. 9, the Food and Drug Administration announced these drugs may be linked to increased risk of vision loss by people already at risk.

The FDA clarified that agency scientists were unable to identify any causal link.

Its review of over 134,000 clinical-trial patients concluded vision loss occurred in 6.4 cases per 100,000 people taking the drug for a year, a number that should put users at ease.

The agency alerted patients and doctors to be on the lookout for any changes in vision.

The warning is an important reminder: These weight-loss drugs impact many organs, and the effects need to be continuously monitored; they’re not beauty shots.

But when data indicate a drug is dangerous, the agency pulls it from the market, as it did Fen-Phen, Seldane, Baycol, Bextra and Meridia, to name a few.

The FDA’s approach to semaglutides is in stark contrast to what the litigation-industrial complex is doing — i.e., bombarding the public with ads with messages like: “We fight for people harmed by weight loss drugs” and “The fee is free unless you win.”

With big money to be made if a jury returns a verdict against the manufacturer, an entire industry has sprung up to recruit claimants, finance litigation and file lawsuits.

Companies that aggregate patients to be claimants spend billions on online and television ads, starting with words like: “Have you or a loved one been diagnosed with . . . “

Taking any medication involves some risk. But the greater danger is this lawsuit tsunami will drive relatively safe drugs off the market or scare patients who need them to stop taking them.

More than 4,000 state and federal lawsuits have already been filed against weight-loss drug makers, all demanding jury trials.

Some allege vision loss, others gastrointestinal injuries.

But jury trials are a crapshoot, because jurors — who understandably find parsing the statistical claims of each side difficult — are inclined to sympathize with ailing plaintiffs.

It’s up to the trial judge to help jurors focus on the evidence and keep bogus claims out of the courtroom.

But that doesn’t always happen.

An onslaught of litigation forced the maker of Bendectin, an anti-nausea drug, to pull the product — yet in 2010, an appeals court found no scientific evidence supporting the claim it caused birth defects.

A few years later, tens of thousands of plaintiffs were rounded up to sue four manufacturers of Zantac and similar heartburn medications, alleging a link to cancer.

But in July 2025, the Delaware Supreme Court unanimously reversed a lower-court ruling, castigating the trial judge for failing to act as a “scientific gatekeeper” against junk science.

Now the litigation-industrial complex is at it again, threatening to drive weight-loss drugs off the market. Its ads would make any consumer uneasy.

In 2019, the Federal Trade Commission claimed that ads run by several law firms and plaintiff-recruiting companies caused patients at risk of stroke to stop taking Xarelto or Pradaxa blood thinners, causing 66 to have strokes and seven to die.

Thousands of lawsuits against semaglutide manufacturers are now consolidated into two cases in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania.

Kudos to the presiding judge in these two cases, Karen Marston, for acting as a scientific gatekeeper to keep phony claims out of her courtroom.

The trials haven’t started yet, but already she’s ruled that plaintiffs alleging gastroparesis, a serious gastro ailment, must have had a stomach emptying test (the gold standard for identifying that illness) at the time of their diagnosis or they’ll be dropped from the case.

Simply having hired-gun experts attesting to their diagnosis will not be accepted.

Marston is now preparing another pre-trial ruling under which plaintiffs lacking proof of a causal connection between their ailment and the drug they took will have their cases summarily dismissed.

Protecting patients and medical innovation at the same time is a tall order.

It requires the FDA to monitor drugs on the market, physicians to judiciously advise patients and judges to keep junk claims out of their courtrooms.

Most important: Don’t let the trial lawyers kill our access to new medications just to score a big payday.

Betsy McCaughey, a former New York lieutenant governor, is chairman of the Committee to Reduce Infection Deaths.
