Australia politics live: MPs to honour Haines with ‘civility pledge’ and orange cockatoos; access to contraception and UTI treatment to become cheaper and easier

2026-10-25 22:37

MPs to place orange cockatoo sign on Haines’ seat in parliament

Crossbench MPs will honour Helen Haines today by placing an orange cockatoo sign on her seat in parliament as lower house members, including the prime minister, Anthony Albanese, will pay tribute and send well wishes to her this morning.

The orange cockatoo is synonymous with Haines. She explained in 2024 that it came about after volunteers for her predecessor, Cathy McGowan, “were like a flock of cockatoos” with their orange shirts on.

The association and symbolism has grown from there, representing a community flocking together to build a vibrant democracy. Volunteers have crafted cockies out of old yard signs and injected much fun, intrigue and conversation into the independent campaigns of Indi.

Haines remains in a serious but stable condition in a Melbourne hospital after she lost both legs following an alleged attack near her electorate office in north-east Victoria a week ago.

The MP’s husband, Phil Haines, provided an update at the Alfred hospital on Wednesday, saying “both her heart and her mind are in good shape”. It’s understood Phil will attend parliament today.

Haines was the first federal politician to sign a civility pledge as part of the Compassion in Politics movement to make politics more respectful. Crossbench MPs will sign the simple pledge and encourage others to do so today.

Federal Labor flags concern as state support plunges

Confidence in Victoria’s Labor government is plunging weeks out from an election, as federal minister Mark Butler expresses concern about the state party’s uphill battle.

The health minister conceded on Monday that the November 28 Victorian election would be “a tough one” for Labor, as fresh opinion polls show a 10 per cent negative swing compared with support on election night in 2022.

He told ABC News Breakfast:

After 12 years and some of very difficult years for Victoria, particularly through COVID, it’s no surprise that this is going to be an uphill battle for [Victorian premier] Ben Carroll and the Labor team

Primary support for Labor has slumped four points to 24 per cent since Carroll ascended as premier in July, according to the latest Newspoll conducted in October and published by The Australian on Monday.

Labor’s dive in the polls solidifies opposition leader Jess Wilson’s box seat position to become the next premier, with primary support for the coalition surging to 33 per cent.

That represented a lift of two per cent, while One Nation’s vote edged one point to 20 per cent.

Labor’s two-party preferred vote has plunged 10 per cent since the 2022 election, according to the Newspoll, placing the coalition ahead 55-45.

Anonymous online voices allow people to ‘do and say anything’, Tehan says

Liberal MP Dan Tehan has raised concerns about the impact of anonymous online accounts on inflammatory political discourse.

He told ABC Radio National:

The problem we’ve got at the moment is the anonymous voices online.

I’m a big believer in freedom of speech and always have been, but I’ve always strongly believed that you have to put your name to your words. That’s the key.

In the community, those who disagree tend to do so respectfully, he said.

What you tend to see online is the people who are there, anonymously, will just do and say anything.

Now, whether that then heightens it, I don’t know, but … it’s always the question of balance.

Auditor-general examining if detention in Victorian police cells breaches human rights

The Victorian auditor general is investigating the detention of people in police cells, after Guardian Australia revealed the practice routinely led to the force breaching its own guidelines.

The Victorian auditor general’s office (Vago) confirmed it was set to examine whether Victoria police and the department of justice “manage prison and police cell capacity to support human rights and the efficient functioning of the justice system”.

Guardian Australia reported in November last year that the process of “decanting” was resulting in people being moved hundreds of kilometres across Victoria as the government grappled with tougher bail laws and a shortage of prison beds and corrections workers.

This was also leading to people spending more than the 14-day target in police cells, and in some cases meant they were serving their entire sentences there.

Police cells are designed to hold people for short periods, such as after arrest and for bail hearings. However, in 2025 there were media reports of police holding prisoners in police cells beyond the recommended 14-day limit.

When cells at one station are full, police may transport individuals to other stations. In some reported cases, police have transported individuals hundreds of kilometres away.

This can mean that prisoners miss their court appearances and do not have easy access to their lawyers and health services. This undermines both prisoners’ human rights and the proper functioning of the justice system.

Vago noted that Victoria’s recent bail reforms were also likely to increase pressure on prison beds and staff capacity.

It said in a statement to Guardian Australia that the audit was under way and was expected to be tabled early next year.

Safety concerns and threats have changed ‘dramatically’ for politicians: Butler

Cabinet minister Mark Butler has said this sitting week offers an opportunity to discuss ways to keep MPs, their families and staffers safe.

Asked by ABC Radio National if much had changed during his time in parliament, Butler said:

It’s changed dramatically in my time.

I’ve been here for almost 20 years and security has changed, and the federal police, in my experience, have been very, very responsive to our requests when there are threats or the possibility of me going to an event.

But just the number and the nature of threats of violence and death, not just to MPs, but to their children and to their partners and to their staff, is like nothing we saw 20 years ago.

Politicians to sign ‘civility pledge’ following alleged attack on Haines

The speaker of the house, Milton Dick, will call for civility in parliament today, and some politicians are expected to sign a “civility pledge”.

Independent MP Helen Haines was the first to sign this pledge before she was allegedly attacked last week.

Fellow independent Kate Chaney, on Monday told ABC radio she would be adding her name:

I think there’s something really symbolic about the fact that Helen was [the first] federal MP to have signed this. It is a very simple thing.

It just says ‘in my political life I pledge to act with honesty, respect, and compassion’.

That feels to me like a simple and poignant way to honour the contribution Helen’s made to the parliament and continue to try to embody those values that she personifies in the way that I do my work in parliament.

Cheaper contraception and UTI treatments as pharmacy program expanded

Australian women will find it cheaper to access the pill and UTI treatment after visiting a pharmacist, as the federal government expands a trial program to lower healthcare costs.

The 12-month trial, to begin from early 2027, will allow Australian women to access pharmacy treatment for uncomplicated urinary tract infections (UTIs) and initiation or re-supply of hormonal contraception.

The federal government will cover pharmacist consultation costs, of up to $38 per visit.

This program was previously restricted to concession card holders, but the health minister, Mark Butler, and minister for women, Katy Gallagher, announced on Monday that it would be expanded to benefit an estimated 900,000 women.

For too long, women’s experiences of the health system haven’t received the attention they deserve. We’re determined to change that. We’re making practical changes that give women more choice, make health care more accessible and help save women thousands of dollars over their lifetimes.

We want to make it easier for women to get care that fits into their lives.

The Pharmacy Guild of Australia has championed the move, describing it as “landmark reform”.

But doctors argue that women with less straightforward symptoms risk misdiagnosis when heading to a pharmacist rather than a GP.

Royal Australian College of GPs president Michael Wright said:

If the diagnosis is wrong, patients risk receiving antibiotics they don’t need while the real cause of their symptoms is missed or treatment is delayed.

MPs to ‘try to do justice’ to wounded fellow MP Helen Haines

Parliament will mark a solemn start to the sitting week as politicians pay tribute to independent MP Helen Haines, who was rammed by a vehicle outside her electoral office.

The popular crossbencher was allegedly targeted in a deliberate attack when she was struck by a four-wheel drive on the main street of the Victorian town of Wangaratta on 19 October.

The prime minister, Anthony Albanese, will deliver a speech to parliament on Monday, reflecting on the alleged assault and its impact, and invite colleagues to do the same.

Fellow independent MP Monique Ryan described Dr Haines as the parliament’s “most gentle, principled and kind” member ahead of her planned remarks in the lower house.

“People who love her will try to do justice to Helen’s integrity and leadership,” she said.

“I hope that all members will rise to the occasion as Helen would do were she with us.

“We’ll feel her absence deeply.”

A fundraiser for charity Save the Children, set up on Wednesday by Haines’ family in lieu of flowers, had surpassed $1.2m in donations by Sunday afternoon.

Hanson says One Nation cartoons ‘not going anywhere’ despite criticism

Pauline Hanson says One Nation’s controversial cartoon series “will not be going anywhere”, after growing outrage over its offensive portrayal of federal politicians including previous depictions of some being run over by motor vehicles.

Hanson said her party’s Please Explain animation series had “nothing to do with the tragic attack on Helen Haines”, and claimed major party politicians wanted the series “censored”.

The series has garnered further criticism after videos from 2023 and 2024 were re-circulated online and discussed on Sunday morning political talk shows on the ABC and News24.

In one 2023 video, titled Under the Bus, Hanson appears to drive a bus over independent senator Lidia Thorpe, who had been depicted as lying under the vehicle’s tyres. In another from 2024, titled The State of Melbourne, former Victorian premier Daniel Andrews is shown being run down by a car driven by cartoon character Bluey, with the video ending with suggestions Anthony Albanese was also about to be hit.

Those cartoons, published years before the attack on Haines, have been criticised for portraying violence against politicians. Shadow minister Ted O’Brien said on News24 the cartoons were “not timely”, and while admitting he hadn’t seen them, questioned whether they were “appropriate in the current climate”.

But Hanson, posting on social media on Sunday afternoon, rejected criticism of the other clips. She claimed “the Labor and Liberal Dirt Units are already back at work, attempting to use this tragedy to silence their critics and deflect from their own failings. Too many media organisations are happy to help them fling the dirt.”

The Please Explain cartoons have nothing to do with the tragic attack on Helen Haines. They will not be going anywhere. Liberal and Labor politicians want our cartoons censored because they don’t like the scrutiny.

Those seeking to exploit this tragedy to silence their critics should be ashamed of themselves.
