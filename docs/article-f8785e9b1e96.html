Michael Goodwin: Rudy Giuliani defines the Presidential Medal of Freedom — from ‘America’s Mayor’ to his lasting impact on NYC crime

2026-11-25 03:15

When President Trump said last year he would give Rudy Giuliani the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the former mayor had just been so seriously injured in a car crash that he received last rites.

In a statement explaining his decision, the president saluted Giuliani as the “greatest Mayor in the history of New York City, and an equally great American Patriot.”

Trump got it right on both counts, and the 82-year-old Giuliani got the highest-possible civilian award Tuesday in a private White House ceremony.

“I am honored and humbled to receive the Presidential Medal of Freedom from my nation and from our Commander-in-Chief, President Donald J. Trump,” Giuliani said in a statement.

The two men are longtime friends, and Giuliani served as one of Trump’s lawyers in the disputes over the outcome of the 2020 election. Later, the former mayor was disbarred for his actions in New York and Washington, DC.

He also was charged in a $148 million defamation case brought by two Georgia women who worked at an absentee-ballot office. Their complaint centered on Giuliani’s claims they manipulated the results to favor Joe Biden.

The case was settled before trial, and neither side disclosed details of the terms, though Giuliani reportedly got to keep his Manhattan and Florida apartments and some personal items, such as Yankees World Series rings and a Joe DiMaggio jersey.

As time passes, a more complete picture of Giuliani’s life properly reduces those lapses to footnotes in an otherwise long and outstanding service as a dedicated public official.

In the years before Oprah Winfrey dubbed him “America’s Mayor” for the heroic way he led New York through the horrors and aftermath of 9/11, Giuliani distinguished himself as the nation’s leading crime fighter.

As he revolutionized urban policing from City Hall and sharply reduced violent crimes, especially murders, he was widely regarded as the Eliot Ness of his era.

In the early 1980s, Giuliani had been in the top ranks of the Justice Department under President Ronald Reagan.

In a surprising move in 1983, he requested to return to New York (he was born in Brooklyn) and become the US attorney in the famed Southern District prosecutor’s office.

It has jurisdiction over federal criminal and civil cases in Manhattan, The Bronx and several northern suburban counties.

One of his first big moves came when Giuliani pioneered federal efforts to help the outgunned NYPD and city prosecutors combat the explosion of street crime and open narcotic sales.

With police ranks still depleted from the city’s 1970s brush with bankruptcy, a commissioner under Mayor Ed Koch had banned young street cops from making narcotics arrests because of fears they were not fully trained and possibly could be persuaded to look the other way.

Dubbing it “federal day,” Giuliani said that on select days, any drug dealers arrested would be taken to federal courts rather than state courts, with federal laws providing for harsher penalties.

He also extended the net to include cases of sexual harassment on crowded subways.

The major cases during his six-year run involved prosecutions of Mafia leaders, Wall Street traders and corrupt union officials.

That he wasn’t playing politics became clear when he brought a federal case against the leaders of the Teamsters, the only major union to endorse Reagan in the previous election.

He also turned on Sen. Al D’Amato, regarded as something of a political patron, during the Southern District’s takedown of Wedtech, a corrupt Bronx company that used payoffs and donations to win lucrative military contracts.

He called D’Amato as a witness because the senator had helped the company get contracts and received donations from firm officials.

Among those convicted during the racketeering case were Reps. Mario Biaggi and Robert Garcia.

Bronx Borough President Stanley Simon also was convicted, and Bronx Democratic boss Stanley Friedman was later convicted in the infamous parking-violations case Giuliani brought.

Those and other cases, some of which collapsed, propelled Giuliani into the political ring.

In 1989 he won a contested GOP primary for mayor against cosmetics heir Ronald Lauder.

That same year, Manhattan Borough President David Dinkins pulled off a huge upset by defeating incumbent Koch in the Democratic mayoral primary.

The Giuliani-Dinkins mayoral election was especially nasty and the voter turnout of more than

2 million was the largest in city history until it was surpassed in Zohran Mamdani’s victory last year.

Dinkins won narrowly to become the city’s first black mayor, and Giuliani won their rematch four years later.

Murder had set terrifying new records during Dinkins’ single term, averaging more than 2,000 a year, and Giuliani’s anti-crime message carried the day.

Based on his experience, and by hiring Bill Bratton as his first commissioner, Giuliani declared war on violent crime and public disorder.

They adopted the revolutionary “broken windows” theory of policing, and by making precinct and borough commanders responsible for crimes and quality-of-life issues in their territory, Giuliani and Bratton delivered near-miraculous results.

In the first term, overall crime was cut in half and yearly murders declined by nearly 70%.

To this day, the weekly citywide crime stats reported by the NYPD use 1993 — Dinkins’ last year in City Hall — as the high-water mark for every category.

The latest report, for example, shows that murder has fallen by 85.9% this year compared to the same period 33 years ago.

Giuliani’s marriage to Donna Hanover infamously exploded during his second term, but his endorsement still helped lift Michael Bloomberg to victory during the fraught and fearful days after the 9/11 terror attacks on the World Trade Center.

Bloomberg hired as top cop Ray Kelly and they largely followed the Giuliani-Bratton playbook.

The result was a steady decline of crime over two decades, and a corresponding rise in the quality of life and a booming city economy.

The contrast with Mamdani couldn’t be greater, but he could have his own golden era of public safety if he properly funded the police and fully trusted Jessica Tisch, the NYPD commissioner he inherited.

Tisch was hired, guided and mentored by both Bratton and Kelly.

As such, she is the heir of the commissioners who led the troops and transformed the badlands of New York into America’s safest big city.

That crime continues to fall to record lows on Tisch’s watch adds further luster to the trio’s legacy.

Imagine the results if Mamdani were to break from the socialist street rabble that still views the police as the enemy.

If he were to grow up and provide Tisch with sufficient funding and trust her commitment to keeping the streets safe, another decade of NYPD miracles could be the result.
