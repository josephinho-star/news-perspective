That six-month haul, recorded between April and September, was nearly three times what the companies earned during the same period last year, when profits totaled less than $47 billion, according to a New York Times analysis.

The conflict, which began Feb. 28, severely disrupted oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway that previously handled the equivalent of roughly one-fifth of global oil consumption.

Oil shipments through the Persian Gulf recently surpassed prewar levels, according to the Wall Street Journal, though that supply boost is yet to ease costs at the pump.

ExxonMobil, Chevron, BP, Shell and TotalEnergies together posted $56.3 billion in adjusted third-quarter net income, with each company beating Wall Street forecasts, Bloomberg reported Friday.

ExxonMobil’s refining business raked in a record $9.05 billion in third-quarter profits — nearly five times its year-earlier haul, according to Bloomberg.

Chevron, meanwhile, earned $4.93 billion from refining, fuel marketing and related operations — about quadruple its profit a year earlier and the division’s best quarterly showing on record.

Since the US and Israel launched strikes against Iran on Feb. 28, international oil prices have climbed nearly 60%, according to Bloomberg.

Refineries that remain operational have enjoyed fatter margins as the Iran war and Ukrainian strikes in Russia have knocked roughly 10% of the world’s fuel-making capacity offline, according to the news outlet.

Ukraine recently paused its attacks on Russian oil refineries at the behest of President Trump, who blamed the strikes for high prices.

American motorists are paying an average of $4.20 a gallon for regular gasoline, up from $2.98 before the Iran war began in late February, according to AAA.

Derek Reisfield, co-founder of MarketWatch, warned that diesel prices could remain elevated because of longstanding constraints on US refining capacity and the difficulty of expanding production.

“There is no short-term fix. This problem has been brewing for a long time,” he told The Post.

Reisfield warned that sustained high oil prices could eventually hurt the industry’s long-term prospects by encouraging investment in alternative energy sources, including nuclear, geothermal, solar and wind power.

“The potential bad news for the oil industry is this capacity gets put in place and reduces the fundamental demand for oil as an energy source over time,” he told said.

Trump singled out ExxonMobil and Chevron in August, urging the companies to return some of their profits to the public and cut retail fuel prices.

“When you look at one company where they made 12 times what they made the year before, they ought to give some of that back to the public,” he told reporters in the Oval Office on Aug. 3.

“And they better cut the retail price, the consumer price.”

The president, described himself as a champion of free enterprise but expressed frustration that the industry’s enormous earnings were coming at consumers’ expense.
