Data broker opt-out steps retirees need before the holidays

2026-11-19 19:30

Many retirees think they're protecting their privacy by staying off social media, removing their information from one people-search site or paying for credit monitoring. But a single data broker opt-out request may leave your home address, phone number and family details exposed elsewhere. With the holidays approaching, now is a good time to find out what's available about you online and take steps to remove it.

People-search sites like Spokeo, Whitepages and BeenVerified can make personal details available to strangers. Some information is free to view, while more detailed reports may require payment. And here's where it gets concerning. A scammer who knows your grandson's name or where your daughter lives could use those details to make a fake emergency call sound convincing. Here's where that information comes from, how to remove it and what you can do to help protect your family before the holidays.

Where your personal information comes from

You don't need a social media account to have your information collected. Data brokers gather information from public records, including property deeds, voter registration records where accessible and court filings. They may also obtain details from commercial databases, online activity and companies that collect personal information. Over time, those details can become part of a profile showing where you live, previous addresses, phone numbers and possible family connections.

According to the Federal Trade Commission, opting out of one people-search site doesn't remove your information from others. Your details may also reappear when public records change or websites collect updated information. For older adults, the financial risks can be especially serious. An FTC report released in October 2026 found that adults ages 60 and older reported approximately $3 billion in fraud losses during 2025. Although older adults reported losing money to fraud less frequently than younger adults overall, their reported individual losses tended to be higher.

The data broker opt-out steps to take now

You can start removing your personal information yourself without paying for a service.

- Search for your information. Look up your name on major people-search sites such as Spokeo, Whitepages, BeenVerified, Intelius and TruthFinder. Try searching your phone number and home address, too.

- Find each site's opt-out page. Look for links labeled "Opt Out," "Remove My Information" or "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information." You can usually find these in the website's footer or privacy policy.

- Submit removal requests. Follow each site's instructions. Some may require email confirmation or other verification. Share only the personal information necessary to complete the request, and make sure you're using the legitimate website.

- Keep a record of your requests. Save the website addresses and dates when you submitted them. This makes it easier to follow up if your information remains visible.

- Check again regularly. Return after about a month to see whether your information has been removed. Then check every few months because new listings may appear.

- Freeze your credit. Contact Experian, Equifax and TransUnion to request a free credit freeze. This can help prevent identity thieves from opening new credit accounts in your name.

A credit freeze won't remove your information from data brokers, and credit monitoring generally alerts you to possible problems rather than preventing your details from appearing online. These tools serve different purposes.

One thing to remember: Removing your information from people-search sites doesn't erase public records. Your details may still appear in property records, court documents or even listings connected to family members. That's why it's important to check more than your own name. Doing this yourself can take hours, especially when you're dealing with multiple websites. And because data brokers continue collecting information, the work may need to be repeated.

Free tools that can help remove your information

Before paying for a data removal service, there are two other options worth knowing about.

Google's Results about you

Google offers a free tool called Results about you that can help you find personal information appearing in Google Search. Depending on eligibility, you may be able to request the removal of results containing your home address, phone number or email address.

Here's how to get started:

- Open the Google app and tap your profile picture, then select Results about you. You can also access the tool through Google's website.

- Select Get started or Settings.

- Enter your name and the personal contact information you want Google to check.

- Review any matching results and request removal of eligible listings.

- Check the tool for updates on your requests.

Keep in mind that removing a Google search result doesn't delete the information from the original website. You may still need to contact the site directly.

California's free DROP privacy service

If you live in California, you have another option. The state's free Delete Request and Opt-out Platform, called DROP, launched in January 2026. It allows eligible California residents to submit a single deletion request covering more than 600 registered data brokers.

Beginning Aug. 1, 2026, covered data brokers were required to start processing requests through the system. Removal can take time, and some information may be exempt under the law. You can submit a request and check its status at California's official DROP website at privacy.ca.gov/drop/. Other states also have privacy laws that may give residents additional rights to request deletion of their information.

How a personal data removal service can help

If you don't want to spend hours contacting websites and tracking removal requests, a personal data removal service can help.

These services contact data brokers on your behalf and submit requests to remove your personal information. Depending on the provider and plan, features may include:

- Automated removal requests sent to participating data brokers and people-search websites.

- Recurring removal requests to help address personal information that reappears online.

- Progress tracking so you can monitor the status of removal requests.

- Family protection options that allow you to include additional family members.

- Custom removal requests for websites that may not be covered by standard automated services.

Some services offer ongoing monitoring and follow-up requests, which can save time compared with handling each website yourself. It's important to understand that no service can guarantee every piece of personal information will disappear from the internet. Public records may remain accessible, and some websites may fall outside the service's coverage. Still, reducing the number of places where your information appears can make your personal details harder to find.

Bring this conversation to the Thanksgiving table

Thanksgiving brings families together, making it a great opportunity to talk about protecting everyone's personal information. You don't need to make it a lengthy discussion. A few simple checks can help everyone understand what scammers may already know about them.

1) Check what's online about your family

After dinner, sit down with a family member and search your names on a few people-search sites. Look for home addresses, phone numbers and family connections. If you find something you don't want publicly available, discuss how to request its removal. Check your parents' information, too, with their permission. Even if you've removed your own details, your name or address may still appear in a relative's profile.

2) Agree on a family code word

Choose a private phrase that family members can use if someone calls claiming there's an emergency. Avoid birthdays, pet names or other details someone could easily discover online. Agree on the phrase privately rather than sharing it in a group chat. A code word adds another layer of protection, but it shouldn't be your only way to verify someone's identity.

3) Verify unexpected emergency calls

If someone claims to be a grandchild or another relative who urgently needs money, don't send anything until you've checked the story. Hang up and call your loved one directly using a phone number you already know. If you can't reach them, contact another trusted family member. Scammers can now use AI voice cloning to sound like someone you love. That means even a familiar voice may not be enough to prove who's calling. Learn more about how AI voice scams can clone your family's voice and what you can do to protect yourself. And be especially suspicious if someone demands payment through gift cards, cryptocurrency or wire transfers.

4) Make a family plan before someone calls

Agree that nobody will send emergency money based solely on an unexpected phone call or text. If something sounds suspicious, contact another family member before taking action. Scammers often pressure people to respond immediately or keep the situation secret. The more your family talks about these tactics ahead of time, the easier it becomes to recognize a suspicious request. Scammers may already know more about your family than you realize. A private code word adds protection, but always confirm an unexpected emergency by contacting your loved one directly before sending money.

Here's what concerns me. You can be careful about what you share online and still have your home address, phone number and family details available on people-search sites. Removing that information once is a good start, but it can show up again. That's why I recommend checking these sites regularly or using a trusted data removal service to help handle the work. And with Thanksgiving bringing families together, this is a great time to talk about scams and make sure everyone knows what to do if a suspicious call comes in. A few simple steps now could help keep your family's personal information out of the wrong hands.
