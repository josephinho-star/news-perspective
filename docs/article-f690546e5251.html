Australian politics live: Chalmers to ‘save more than we spend’ in budget update: King Charles ‘deeply saddened’ by Meldrum’s death

2026-11-23 22:49

Labor will ‘save more than we spend’ in budget update: Chalmers

The government will save more than it spends, says Jim Chalmers, but it won’t be a “slash and burn” budget update.

Unlike the federal budget, where we get plenty of notice of when it will be handed down, the government only gives us a few days notice for the mid-financial year economic and fiscal outlook (or Myefo).

We so far know that it will be somewhere between the beginning of December and the handing down of the Bondi royal commission.

There’s still a fair bit of work to do to finalise it all, but we’ve made enough progress to be confident that at this stage there will be a modest net save in the budget.

We’re not going to slash and burn in the budget where that would impact the payments that people receive or where it would impact some of the more sensitive parts of the economy. But we haven’t focused the savings on just one portfolio. There will be a number of savings in the mid-year update. They will be substantial, as I said. These are serious times.

Nobody is confirming yet, but it seems a deal between the government and the Greens is nearly done, to pass the digital duty of care legislation this week. It could happen as soon as today, when the Senate commences after 12pm.

Neither Labor nor the Greens were saying anything this morning, but Guardian Australia understands things are pretty close to being finished. Any deal would have to be approved by both party rooms this morning, and we’re expecting to shortly hear from the Greens after their party meeting.

Communications minister Anika Wells said yesterday she had been “speaking a lot” with Greens’ senator Sarah Hanson-Young, and that the government was “working through what [Greens amendments] might look like”.

The Greens had been pushing for a private right of action, to allow Australians to directly sue big tech companies, and increased fines - no confirmation yet, but we hear there could be movement on some of those issues.

“We understand the argument. We’ve got a lot of considerations to settle,” Wells said yesterday.

‘For this joint party room, it’s been a tough year’: Angus Taylor

Angus Taylor has acknowledged it’s been a tough year for the Coalition and a tough year for the public as he addresses the party room for most likely the last time this year.

The opposition leader has let the cameras in as he gees up his party room, before the members get down to business and discuss their plans for the week.

He says the party needs to come back ready for a big year and a potential election in 2027. (The election needs to be held at the latest by May 2028.)

I know for Australia, this has been a tough year. And for this joint party room, it’s been a tough year too.

So it’s important we take a good break, get time with family and friends, and then come back in January and hit the ground running. Because the reality is there may be an election next year and we have the opportunity and the responsibility to win that election.

Party rooms meet with key legislation hanging in the balance

Labor, Coalition and Greens members are all about to be – if they aren’t already – meeting in their respective party rooms, as four key bills hang in the balance.

Negotiations are still under way. Here’s who’s at the negotiation table for what legislation:

Digital duty of care: Labor and Greens. Sticking points: Greens want the right for the public to sue tech platforms, and tie penalties to a percentage of global revenue.

Gas reservation scheme: Labor and Coalition. Sticking points: Coalition has concerns the planned oversupply in the domestic market will disincentivise new gas investment.

Trusts tax: Labor and Greens. Sticking points: Greens want to stop private schools and non-tax paying charities being exempt from the trust changes.

Private health insurance: Labor and Greens. Sticking points: Greens want pensioners exempt from changes that would decrease private health insurance rebates for over 65s.

We know the government and Greens are close to a deal on digital duty of care.

King Charles and Elton John pay tribute to Molly Meldrum

King Charles and Elton John have joined a cast of thousands paying tribute to Molly Meldrum, who passed away yesterday, aged 83.

John, who was interviewed by Meldrum on multiple occasions on Countdown, shared a photo and a heart felt message on social media:

Molly Meldrum was an Australian legend and an institution. His love of music propelled so many Australian artists to success and I had so many great moments interviewed by him.

An enormous loss for Australian music.

The ABC has also published a tribute from a palace aide on behalf of King Charles, who was one of Meldrum’s more famous Countdown interviews.

The King was deeply saddened to hear of the passing of Molly Meldrum.

His Majesty’s interview with Molly is a moment he and many others will never forget – he hopes that interview will once again raise a few smiles as Australians honour Molly’s legacy as a true giant of the music industry.

Joyce warns of risks to humans and wildlife after Sydney fire ant infestation

One Nation MP Barnaby Joyce has warned Australia’s agriculture industry is at risk after a second infestation of fire ants was found – and destroyed – in Sydney over the weekend.

He told News 24 on Monday evening:

This is incredibly important for Australia, for the people of Sydney on this instance, for the people of Sydney to deal with this fire ant incursion.

Otherwise your children, if you put them down in a park on a Sunday afternoon while you’re having a picnic, just like the sharks at the beach, someone’s going to get bitten.

Joyce said the ants posed an anaphylactic shock risk to people who go to parks or sporting fields in Sydney, and warned cattle, koalas and other native fauna were vulnerable.

The MP added the fire ants “even eat echidnas” – the federal environment department has warned fire ants can kill and scavenge the mammals.

Greens want digital duty of care bill passed this week

Labor and the Greens are in deep conversation to pass the digital duty of care bill and the government is considering the minor party’s demands. One of those – to allow private citizens the right to directly sue tech platforms if they experience harm – was even recommended by a Labor-chaired committee.

I would hope we get the digital duty of care package resolved in the very near future and before Parliament rises.

This has been productive, these discussions with the government, and we think material outcomes for Australians have been achieved.

The Greens are also talking capital gains tax discounts for trusts, and reducing health insurance premium rebates for over 65s. Those discussions aren’t quite as far along.

There’s a lot of tax bills floating around. That’s complicated discussions. We’ll continue to talk, but our goal at the end of the day is not to give corporate Australia a big Christmas present …

The government keeps asking, and we keep telling them the same thing: you exempt all pensioners [from private health insurance rebate changes], and there’s a pathway through.

Tax gas and stop subsidising fossil fuels: Greens 2c to budget savings debate

The Greens aren’t happy that Labor has foreshadowed little extra cost-of-living support in the mid-year budget update – other than yesterday’s announcement to cap PBS medicines to $25 until 2030.

The Greens leader, David Shoebridge, told the media this morning that people need more relief and the government should find its savings elsewhere.

It’s not new – the Greens have been pushing for a tax on gas exports, and calling for subsidies for fossil fuels to end for a while now. The government shows no indication of listening this time, despite growing public support for the measures.

We’ve got a treasurer coming out and saying there’s going to be no relief, and in fact looking to have an austerity budget. This is the exact wrong time to have an austerity budget, take services and support away from ordinary Australians.

We know that there are revenue sources the government can tap into: a tax on export gas, getting resource sector to pay its fair share, stopping $16.3bn in fossil fuels subsidies to corporate Australia, and that would provide the headroom so that Australians could get a bit of relief.

Coalition sceptical of Chalmers’ net savings budget promise

The shadow treasurer, Tim Wilson, jumps into the RN Breakfast hot seat and casts doubt on Jim Chalmers’ promise that the government will save more than it spends in this mid-year budget update.

He says the government hasn’t offered struggling businesses much hope.

Every time the government has said they’re going to deliver savings, they’ve gone on and saved $2 and spent $3.

Asked to respond to Chalmers’ criticism of the Coalition, that all they do is push back against the government’s savings measures, Wilson whips out a favourite insult:

The treasurer likes to engage in his spin. He has a PhD in it.

(Chalmers’ PhD is in political science and international relations.)

Wilson says the opposition has a proposal to save more than $31bn in the budget – much of which would come from cutting climate and housing programs.

Chalmers ‘not focused on the columns or the commentary’

Facing a hefty amount of criticism for his management of the budget in difficult economic landscape, Jim Chalmers says he’s not focused on the columns and commentary.

Probably best for his mental health.

I’m not focused on the columns or the commentary or that kind of speculation that you see in parts of our media from time to time.

I always take feedback on board … I take responsibility for the difficult reforms that we’re pushing through, and I understand we’re doing that at a time when people are under pressure, and that will be reflected in the polls. And that those polls will be reflected in the columns and in the commentary. But that’s not my focus.

Nvidia stock market reaction shows global market ‘volatile’, says Chalmers

The treasurer, Jim Chalmers, also doesn’t want to make any personal judgements on Jensen Huang or the decision to reinstate him, but he says the reaction does show just how volatile the market is.

Speaking to RN Breakfast, Chalmers says the decision is up to Huang and the board (and I’ll add Donald Trump).

You can see in the quite extraordinary reaction on the stock market just how volatile global markets are right now. I think it’s a bit of a symbol of the quite extraordinary volatility, unpredictability that we’re seeing play out in markets in the AI sector, but more broadly as well.

Asked whether it’s a vulnerability for decisions by these companies and individuals to have such huge knock-on effects, Chalmers says again, that it reflects the “volatility we’re seeing around the world, not just on markets.”

‘It’s made news’: Wong on Jensen Huang’s return to Nvidia

Well, John Howard might have called his book Lazarus Rising (for his several attempts at the Liberal leadership before finally getting it to stick), but we all know who the real modern day Lazarus is: Jensen Huang.

The Nvidia chief executive was reinstated just days after he resigned following revelations of extramarital affairs, including one US authorities suspected of being a Chinese spy.

Wong is asked whether she was surprised by the decision. Naturally, she doesn’t really want to weigh in.

Look, ah – certainly it’s made news. But, ultimately, a decision for the board and the company. My focus today is more broadly on how I end my speech to the Senate – the ministerial statement on securing Australia’s future in a changing world. Obviously, artificial intelligence is a big part of that.

Australian rental affordability plummets to 14-year low

Despite a slight improvement in prices since 2025, no areas of Australia are affordable, according to the national Rental Affordability Index released on Tuesday, AAP reports.

Homes across the country now cost at least 23% of the household income. The outlook is even worse in capital cities – in Sydney, Perth and Hobart, the figures soar past 30%.

Rental prices are considered “acceptable” if they are between 20 and 25% of household income, and “affordable” at anything below that.

Sydney’s affordability has improved by one per cent since 2025 as property prices decline, providing a glimpse of hope for those struggling to pay their bills.

All capitals bar Canberra and Adelaide improved in affordability, as did all regional areas.

‘We all wish a consensus statement had been reached’: Wong

Penny Wong knows a thing or two about not landing a deal at Cop.

While defending her colleague, Chris Bowen, who might be coming back to Australia with his tail between his legs, Wong says she was in the hot seat as a climate minister the last time the UN summit couldn’t reach an agreement – back in 2009.

The proposed Cop31 agreement, which didn’t even mention the words fossil fuels, was rejected by the EU, the UK and some others.

Wong tells News Breakfast that despite all this, Australia has “kept the faith” with the Pacific – a key reason Australia put its hand up for Cop in the first place.

Well, we all wish that a consensus statement had been reached. But I’ve been a climate minister and I’ve been at conferences before where we have not managed to reach an outcome. I was famously at Copenhagen, where we had world leaders there and we still were not able to land an agreement.

I know what Chris Bowen has done has been to work extremely closely with the Pacific all the way through this process. And to keep faith with them. He sought to do that the whole way through, and I’m sure did so in Turkiye as well. And the reason we sought to participate here was because we wanted to keep faith with the Pacific on climate.

Want to counter the dominance of the US and China?

It’s all about the growing middle powers, says Penny Wong.

It’s something we’ve been hearing more and more about, especially during the visits to parliament of Canadian prime minister, Mark Carney, and European Commission president, Ursula Von Der Leyen.

Wong, starting her media rounds on ABC News Breakfast, says:

The work that we are doing with other countries to secure our position, and how important that is – whether it’s the UK, Canada, the European Union. Or, also, our work in the region – our work with Japan.

These are important elements about how you assure Australian security and make sure that we have different options, different pathways, to pursue our interests.

Coalition objects to security agreement with Vanuatu

The Coalition has come out against a security agreement with Vanuatu because of objections to “binding conditions relating to the achievement of renewable energy and climate targets”.

In June, the prime minister, Anthony Albanese, and his Vanuatu counterpart, Jotham Napat, signed the Nakamal agreement in Canberra that rules out the use of its territory for foreign military bases.

Under the $500m deal, Vanuatu stated it would “not permit its territory to be used for any foreign military base or infrastructure”.

But in dissenting comments released last night, Coalition members of a parliamentary committee scrutinising treaties said they did not support ratifying the agreement in its current form.

We acknowledge the legitimate concerns Vanuatu and other Pacific Island nations have regarding climate change and its impacts on their communities, economies, and national security.

However, Coalition members object to clauses … that limit the ability of future governments to change current climate and energy policy settings, by creating binding conditions relating to the achievement of renewable energy and climate targets.

The Albanese government has forged a number of agreements, including new alliances with Fiji and Papua New Guinea, to deny China a military foothold in the Pacific.

A government spokesperson said the Coalition was “putting culture wars ahead of regional security. It is remarkable that the Liberals and Nationals continue to disrespect Pacific priorities.”

Penny Wong says abandoning the US would “surrender a central source of Australian strategic weight” at the moment the nation needs every tool available during global turmoil.

In a major foreign policy speech on Tuesday, the foreign affairs minister will take aim at opponents of the US alliance who present a retreat from the mutual defence pact as a “road to autonomy”.

Such a policy would surrender a central source of Australian strategic weight at precisely the moment we need every opportunity for influence available to us.

What they would do is give us fewer choices and leave us more vulnerable, reducing our capacity to contribute to a regional balance in which our sovereignty can be protected.

The Greens are opposed to the Aukus security pact, under which Australia is set to acquire nuclear-powered submarines, in addition to the presence of the joint Pine Gap base in the Northern Territory.

Wong will also elevate artificial intelligence as a foreign policy challenge, warning the emerging technology is reordering lives, economies and militaries in ways not yet foreseen.
