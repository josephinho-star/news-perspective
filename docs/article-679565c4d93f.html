New Yorkers are fleeing sky-high rents for spacious homes 90 miles away — and they’re staying put

2026-11-23 11:00

New Yorkers fed up with sky-high rents and cramped apartments are finding a new place to call home — just 90 miles down the road.

Philadelphia, long overshadowed by its bigger, pricier neighbor to the north, is winning over Big Apple transplants with spacious homes, private backyards and a slower pace of life — often for a fraction of what they’d pay in New York City.

The numbers back up the trend. More than one in four people moving to the Philadelphia metro area in 2025 — 27% — came from the New York region, according to a January report from Bank of America Institute based on customer account data.

More recently, Philadelphia was also the most popular destination for New York-area house hunters looking to relocate in the second quarter of 2026, according to Redfin. The price difference helps explain the appeal: The typical home at the time cost $832,000 in the New York metro area, compared with $309,000 in Philadelphia, the brokerage found.

For 35-year-old Leah Lawler, the move meant trading a Queens apartment for an entire three-story rowhouse in South Philadelphia — and paying less for the privilege.

After nearly seven years in New York, including stints in Sunnyside, Woodside and East Elmhurst, the former “Saturday Night Live” production staffer decided last Thanksgiving that she’d had enough of the city’s relentless financial pressures.

“I was doing a lot of remote work, and it just felt silly to be living in New York broke, working remotely,” she told The Post.

In South Philly, she found a two-bedroom rowhouse with 1.5 bathrooms, three floors and a private backyard — for $200 less a month than she had been paying in Queens.

Back in East Elmhurst, she’d been living in a garden-level apartment with a partially converted garage, a shared backyard and an additional $200 monthly parking bill.

Now, her 7-year-old English bulldog, Buster, has an enclosed yard to himself, while she has more room than she knows what to do with.

“It’s actually, like, too much space. For one person and a dog,” she said.

But the biggest difference, she said, isn’t measured in square feet.

“I didn’t realize how stressed I was in New York until I got here,” she said.

After leaving her high-pressure television production work behind, Lawler began taking remote assignments and working at a local restaurant, where she says she’s found a close-knit community.

Even everyday errands feel easier.

“Everything in New York is a whole thing,” she said. “Like, going to CVS to pick up your prescriptions is a whole thing. Whereas here it’s easy. You’re in and out.”

For New York nightlife veteran Steve Lewis, 73, and his wife, Michelle Pack-Lewis, 60, leaving Brooklyn meant swapping a $5,000-a-month rental for a $600,000 home in Philly’s Center City.

The couple moved to Philadelphia in April after living in Greenpoint, where their roughly 1,100-square-foot apartment was costing them $60,000 a year in rent.

Their new home, which Steve estimates spans about 1,400 square feet, boasts 18-foot ceilings and exposed brick walls.

“We were older, and we had, well, semi-fixed income, and we didn’t want to spend $5,000 a month on rent,” Lewis told The Post.

Lewis, a longtime New Yorker who once ran nightclubs in the city such as Limelight, is now co-authoring a book about the legendary Danceteria nightclub with Rudolf and John Argento. His wife works as a tech-marketing consultant for NXT Humans, a Philadelphia-based AI advisory and digital agency.

The couple had previously spent time in Pennsylvania to help care for Michelle’s father in Lancaster. After his death, they began searching for a city that could offer the cultural attractions they loved about New York without the same financial burden.

They considered other destinations but ultimately settled on Philadelphia, where they could still enjoy restaurants, theaters and a walkable urban neighborhood.

“I love this town,” Lewis said, adding that he tells his wife as much nearly every day.

Their daughter still lives in New York, along with friends and doctors they continue to visit. Amtrak makes the trip manageable, Lewis said.

But he’s in no hurry to return permanently.

“I love Philadelphia, and I will live here until I can’t,” he said.

Real estate agents told The Post some New York buyers are also looking toward Philadelphia and its surrounding suburbs as they plan for children, seek larger homes or try to stretch their purchasing power.

Andrea Edrei, a Philadelphia real estate broker, said buyers can still find starter homes in neighborhoods such as Northern Liberties for around $500,000 — a price that would be difficult to match in many sought-after New York neighborhoods.

But, like anywhere, location matters.

In upscale Rittenhouse Square, she said, buyers should expect to spend well over $1 million.

And while some Philadelphia neighborhoods have seen relatively flat home prices since the pandemic, Edrei said the suburbs have experienced some of the biggest price increases.

She also pointed to potential savings from new construction properties that qualify for Philadelphia’s property tax abatements, as well as cheaper and more accessible parking.

“The tradeoff becomes quality of life for families with kids and pets, tax savings, more bang for your buck in real estate and overall, a more relaxed way of life,” she told The Post.

Jana Adler, a real estate broker who works in both New York and Philadelphia, recently helped a Brooklyn couple find a roughly $800,000 home in the Philadelphia suburbs after they had been considering a budget of around $1.2 million in Brooklyn.

The couple, who currently live in Park Slope, are planning to start a family and wanted to be closer to relatives in Pennsylvania.

The husband, a trial lawyer, expects to continue commuting to New York about twice a week.

Adler said Philadelphia’s suburbs have become an attractive option for buyers seeking larger homes, desirable school districts and access to Amtrak, making the lawyer’s trips to New York Penn Station possible.

Others, however, prefer Center City, where they can retain a walkable, urban lifestyle without paying New York prices.

But lower prices don’t necessarily mean less competition.

Adler said well-priced homes in desirable neighborhoods can still go under contract within a week, even as some overpriced properties linger on the market.

And the move comes with tradeoffs.

Philadelphia may offer more room for the money, but New Yorkers accustomed to Manhattan’s nonstop energy, shopping and cultural offerings may find the transition difficult.

Edrei, who previously lived in New York, acknowledged that the city’s appeal is hard to replicate.

“The shopping, culture, restaurants and energy are tough to leave behind,” she said.

For Lewis, though, Philadelphia offers something he felt he’d lost in New York: a sense of community.

“Everybody says hello to us. Everybody waves,” he said.

And for Lawler, the move has been about more than saving a few hundred dollars a month.
