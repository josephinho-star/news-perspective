Iowa residents urged to shelter amid lead exposure risks after controversial Army detonation

2026-11-26 09:53

Angelica Stabile, Fox News

Published Nov. 26, 2026, 4:53 a.m. ET

See more of our coverage in your search results.

The US Army blew up about five tons of lead azide, a toxic bomb-making chemical, in a controlled detonation at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown on Wednesday, raising concerns over airborne heavy-metal exposure.

The mission was reportedly necessary because the substances were “unstable” and liable to explode if tampered with.

While a Trump administration official reported that the detonation exceeded expectations by achieving maximum mitigation, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) warned that residents within 20 miles should continue to shelter in place to avoid inhaling lead dust, and should follow EPA safety guidance “until given the all clear by officials.”

“[The] EPA has been on the ground in Iowa BEFORE the controlled detonation, we will be here DURING the detonation, and we will be here for as long as necessary AFTER detonation to ensure communities are safe,” the Environmental Protection Agency said prior to the blast.

According to Mayo Clinic, even small amounts of lead can cause serious health problems. Long-term buildup of lead in the body can get stored and slowly re-enter the bloodstream over time.

At very high levels, lead can damage the kidneys and nervous system in both children and adults, leading to complications in the brain, seizures, neuropsychiatric symptoms and unconsciousness.

Children under 6 years old are the most susceptible, as lead poisoning can severely impact development of their bodies and brains.

According to the Iowa Farm Bureau, Iowa leads the nation in producing corn, pigs, eggs, ethanol and biodiesel — commodities that end up in everyday household products.” The state also ranks No. 2 in soybean production and cover crops.

One Iowa farmer, Doug Seyb, spoke with PBS about his concern that the detonation could impact his nearby farm, including his cows out at pasture and local ponds that fill the farm’s water tank.

The public fears that lead could eventually enter the food supply through crops grown in contaminated soil and livestock raised on contaminated water.

Dr. Stephanie Widmer, New York City emergency medicine physician, medical toxicologist and Fox News medical contributor, confirmed that lead being dispersed into the air is “not a small deal.”

“Lead has no beneficial role in human physiology,” she said. “In fact, lead in the human body can really mess with biology.”

People who eat fresh produce already take in trace amounts of heavy metals like lead, which is widespread in soil — but detonating a “tremendous amount” of the toxic metal raises the risk of dangerous exposure, Widmer warned.

“If there is an enormous amount that is going to be contaminating the food supply, the water, the air that people are breathing in, this is very concerning,” she said.

Widmer suggested that Americans near the detonation site should continue to listen to guidance to shelter in place, keep windows closed, and turn on ventilation or air purifying systems.

“We know that lead is dangerous for humans, but we don’t know how much, or if any, lead they’re being exposed to,” she said. “It could be none. It could a ton.”

The EPA reported no levels of lead in the air 30 minutes after the detonation, but Widmer noted that just because the blow is over doesn’t mean there is no lingering impact.

“Lead inhalation is one of the most efficient ways that lead can be dispersed into the human body,” she said, adding that even if it’s not inhaled, it can be dispersed into the soil. “It can stay in soil, it could stay in water,” the doctor said.

“The very concerning thing is that lead can have negative effects on the human body without even showing any symptoms,” she said. “There’s still going to have to be testing. Follow-up is really key here.”
