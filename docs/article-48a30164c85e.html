Don’t get duped by Amazon’s fake Black Friday ‘deals’ — how to spot a real bargain

2026-11-27 15:09

Black Friday is one of the best parts about Thanksgiving — but while shoppers comb Amazon for Charlotte Tilbury dupes, they may be getting duped by “deals,” too.

That’s according toGroundwork Collaborative, a nonprofit that studies economic injustice. It recently dropped an explosive exposé that outlines how Amazon bamboozled troves of consumers in 2024 and 2025 by claiming some items were “deals” when really, they were full-price buys.

After analyzing two years’ worth of Black Friday and Cyber Monday sales, the D.C.-based think-tank uncovered thatone in three “bargains” had no actual savings.

How did Amazon pull this off?

Allegedly by pricing items higher than usual, then slapping on a sale price so shoppers assumed they were getting a deal. The advocacy group found that out of the over 350 deals examined, 9% were more expensive than the product’s lowest price in the previous 30 days.

And nearly 60% of those products examined were even cheaper 90 days before the sale.

“The most shocking discovery in the report is that a ‘deal’ can cost you more,” Lindsey Owens, president and CEO of Groundwork Collaborative, told The Post. “Of the over 350 deals Amazon marketed on its own deals pages, about one in 10 cost more during the sale than they did in the weeks before it, averaging a 19% overcharge.”

The report claims that shoppers lost money across multiple categories, including Toys & Games, Electronics, and Home & Kitchen.

The worst offender? Allegedly, the Sports & Outdoors section.

Groundwork Collaborative revealed that 50% of Amazon-promoted “deals” were no cheaper than the product’s lowest price in the previous 30 days.

The nonprofit said that Amazon’s Toys & Games section wasn’t far behind, with 42.6% of “deals” that were essentially a lie.

The report lists Electronics as the third-biggest offender at 38.6%. Home & Kitchen had 37% of faulty deals and Clothing Shoes & Jewelry had 28%, according to the agency.

Amazon directed The Post to a statement the company made where it called the report “deeply flawed” — denying all claims.

But Owens told The Post that Groundwork Collaborative stands by its study — and that Black Friday is a prime example of manipulative pricing.

The ‘Shopping Super Bowl’

Add-to-cart junkies have long considered Amazon to be something of a shopping Super Bowl when it comes to Black Friday.

As the e-commerce site exploded onto the scene, it started scooping up business from traditional big box stores, especially as Black Friday became a virtual pastime instead of a street brawling free-for-all in the headphones aisle of RadioShack.

By the pandemic, Amazon became the dominant destination for holiday shoppers as more consumers cemented their online buying behavior. In 2020, sales by independent businesses on Amazon jumped 60% during the Black Friday-to-Cyber Monday shopping period — exceeding $4.8 billion.

Today, Amazon has tapped about 40% of online shopping and has about 200 million Prime members. Which is why it’s so alarming that consumer advocates like Owens claim that the ultimate superstore is driving up prices.

A doctored list of Black Friday and Cyber Monday “deals” could mislead customers, especially with vague keywords like “low prices” and “holiday specials.”

“The concerning part is that if the largest retailer in the country cannot ensure its deals are genuine, that reveals plenty about the market,” Owens told The Post.

Shoppers spot an item with a crossed-out “list price” or “typical price” next to a bright red “Black Friday Deal” badge, signaling a major markdown.

However, the nonprofit found that Amazon was essentially duping customers. Rather than offering a true holiday discount, the retail giant’s sale prices were often no lower than the regular prices offered in the weeks leading up to the event.

To uncover the markup mirage, the group used Keepa, a third-party service that scrapes real-time pricing data multiple times a day to track Amazon’s frequent price fluctuations.

But shoppers can easily beat Amazon at its own game.

Before hitting “buy,” Owens urges bargain hunters to check an item’s price history using free tracking tools like Keepa or CamelCamelCamel to verify they are actually getting a deal.

Still, it’s not all doom and gloom for deal-seekers. Owens told The Post that while consumers shouldn’t take corporate hype at face value, Amazon still offers plenty of legitimate markdowns during Black Friday and Cyber Monday.

“We did find genuine deals, so people should shop the sales. We just recommend that people don’t assume the word ‘deal’ automatically means it’s the lowest recent price,” Owens said.
