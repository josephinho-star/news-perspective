New Jersey city moves to cap rent hikes at just $20 — what tenants need to know

2026-11-06 18:39

Renters in one New Jersey city are getting a major break on housing costs — with annual rent increases capped at just $20 after voters overwhelmingly backed a measure to restore one of the state’s toughest rent-control protections.

Residents of Elizabeth, a city just south of Newark Liberty International Airport, voted Tuesday to reinstate a decades-old rule limiting annual rent hikes on rent-controlled apartments to 3% or $20, whichever is less.

The measure passed with roughly 68% support, according to unofficial election results, marking a victory for tenant advocates after years of legal battles with landlords and city officials.

“I’m heartened that my hometown overwhelmingly voted to approve a measure that ultimately would make Elizabeth more affordable,” Isaac Jimenez, a member of the Freelizabeth campaign committee, told The Post.

“Rent control is affordable housing, and most typically how people experience affordable housing. This is just the beginning for an era of tenant organizing and tenant power in Elizabeth.”

But there’s a catch: The voter-approved ordinance is set to expire in December 2027, leaving its longer-term future in the hands of city officials.

“The initiated ordinance would indeed expire [December] 2027 at which point the City Council would have to renew or amend the ordinance,” Jimenez told The Post. “We hope the council will remember this result when that time comes.”

Under the restored rule, a tenant paying $1,500 a month could see their monthly rent rise by no more than $20 over a 12-month period, rather than the $45 increase permitted under a straight 3% cap.

That’s a potential savings of $300 over the course of a year compared with a $45 monthly increase, assuming both increases take effect for the full 12 months.

The city’s median gross rent is $1,523, according to US Census data.

The $20 limit had been part of Elizabeth’s rent-control ordinance for decades before city leaders scrapped it in 2022, arguing that the restriction was outdated.

The change allowed landlords to raise rents by up to 3% annually without the previous dollar ceiling.

Tenant advocates pushed back, launching a petition campaign in 2025 to force a public vote on restoring the cap. Of course, the launch coincided with a yearslong discussion about affordability in America overall.

The effort, organized by Make the Road New Jersey and the North New Jersey Democratic Socialists of America, faced multiple legal challenges from landlords.

A judge eventually validated the petition signatures, but the ruling came too late for the question to appear on the 2025 ballot.

City lawmakers also attempted to reinstate the cap themselves, only for a judge to invalidate their vote because the meeting lacked proper public notice.

Tuesday’s referendum finally put the issue directly before voters.

But landlords warn the unusually strict cap could come at a cost.

“The $20 cap creates a fundamental imbalance between the cost of providing rental housing and the revenue available to maintain it,” David Brogan, executive director and CEO of the New Jersey Apartment Association, told The Post.

“Property owners face rising taxes, insurance, utilities, labor and maintenance costs, none of which are subject to similar restrictions. Over time, that financial pressure can undermine property values and discourage investment in Elizabeth’s rental housing.”

Brogan warned that the restriction could also leave landlords with fewer resources to maintain and improve their properties.

“When operating expenses rise faster than rental income, property owners have fewer resources available for renovations, upgrades, and long-term improvements,” he said.

The debate echoes concerns raised across the Hudson River, where New York City’s rent regulations have fueled a similar clash between tenant protections and landlords’ operating costs.

Roughly 57,000 rent-stabilized apartments sat vacant across New York City in 2025, with landlords blaming restrictions on their ability to recover renovation costs while tenant advocates accused owners of deliberately keeping units off the market.

In Elizabeth, the newly approved restriction applies only to apartments covered by the city’s rent-control ordinance, not every rental property.

Elizabeth’s City Council adopted an ordinance in October extending the city’s broader rent-control law through Dec. 31, 2027.

With the ordinance set to expire at the end of next year, Brogan urged city officials to consider the financial pressures facing property owners when deciding its future.

“We want city officials to recognize the economic realities of providing housing and, more importantly, to view property owners as partners in maintaining and improving Elizabeth’s housing stock,” he told The Post.
