Turkish authorities ban feeding stray animals on the streets in eastern province

2026-09-25 14:18

Authorities in Turkey’s eastern Erzurum province have banned feeding stray animals on streets under a decision by the provincial Animal Protection Board, the governor’s office announced on X.

The board made the decision at a meeting chaired by Governor Aydın Baruş. It said feeding could cause stray animals to gather in certain areas, settle and breed there. District municipalities will monitor compliance with the ban.

Neighborhood heads, police and gendarmerie units must identify stray animals and report them to the relevant municipalities. District governors will send monthly reports on inspections and related work to the Erzurum Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks.

The board suspended operations at a shelter run by the Pasin Ovası Municipalities Union until it meets the relevant regulations. During the suspension, the union’s member municipalities of Hınıs, Horasan, Karaçoban, Karayazı, Köprüköy and Tekman will arrange to use the Erzurum Metropolitan Municipality shelter.

Oltu Municipality was ordered to fix shortcomings at its shelter, which is under construction, and apply for an operating license. Erzurum Metropolitan Municipality will train veterinarians and animal collection teams employed by district municipalities. District agriculture and forestry offices will track breeding among dogs adopted through the provincial office and ensure their offspring are registered.

The decision comes amid a debate over Turkey’s treatment of stray animals following changes to its Animal Protection Law in 2024. The changes gave municipalities and other local bodies greater responsibility for collecting stray dogs and providing shelter and care. Rules issued afterward set out how stray animals should be collected, taken to shelters and cared for, the Agriculture and Forestry Ministry said.

The Turkish Union of Bar Associations warned in August 2025 that the collection of stray animals had become widespread. It cited cases of animals dying in shelters with inadequate care, along with reports of neglect, insufficient food and poor conditions during transport.

In its general legal guidance, the Animal Rights Federation (HAYTAP) has argued that feeding stray animals is not a crime under existing animal protection law. It has urged local governments to work with volunteers and supported designated feeding sites and set feeding times. Those positions were not issued in response to Erzurum’s decision.
