Most Americans favor stricter gun laws: Gallup

2026-11-12 21:13

Most U.S. adults support more restrictive gun laws, a new Gallup poll shows, including nearly all Democrats and a majority of independents.

In the survey, 56 percent think firearm laws should be stricter, 27 percent think they should be held the same and just 15 percent say they should be made less strict. The share who believe laws should be stricter has held roughly steady since late 2022, but noticeable peaks in Gallup’s data followed especially high-profile mass shootings.

When Gallup asked the question in March 2018, just after the shooting at a high school in Parkland, Fla., support for stricter gun laws jumped to 67 percent. And after the tragic shooting at an elementary school in Uvalde, Texas, support again jumped to 68 percent.

The share who favor less strict gun laws has also climbed, with fewer and fewer Americans satisfied with the status quo. The less strict laws supporters reached a trough in March 2018, after the Parkland shooting, but rose to a record-high 15 percent in Gallup’s latest poll.

Gallup continues to find large partisan divides in opinions on gun legislation. 89 percent of Democrats support stricter laws, nearly as high as their peak of 91 percent in 2021. 54 percent of independents want stricter laws, down from 63 percent in 2025, and just 16 percent of Republicans favor stricter laws, down from 26 percent in 2025.

Even while opposing stricter laws, most Republicans do not want to loosen restrictions. Fifty percent in Gallup’s poll favor keeping laws the same, up from 48 percent in 2025, though down from 59 percent in 2024. Still, a growing share of 31 percent support less strict laws, more than double the share who said so in 2024.

One specific policy that draws more support among Republicans, though less support overall, is a ban on manufacturing, possessing or selling assault weapons. More than a quarter, 28 percent, of Republicans support an assault rifle ban, compared to 75 percent of Democrats and 54 percent of Americans overall.

The U.S. has attempted to strengthen firearm restrictions over the past decade, but only some measures have broken through.

After a 2017 mass shooting in Las Vegas, the deadliest in U.S. history, the Trump administration in President Trump’s first term banned bump stocks, attachments to semiautomatic rifles that allow them to fire more rapidly. Even the National Rifle Association (NRA), which typically opposes any gun restrictions, announced it supported efforts to ban bump stocks.

But in 2024, the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s bump stock ban, saying the government exceeded its authority and Congress could have acted instead. After the decision, Senate Republicans blocked a bipartisan push to outlaw bump stocks, calling it a “political exercise” in an election year.

Assault weapons restrictions and bans have received more traction at the state level, including earlier this year in Virginia, though the ban is already tied up in court. The Supreme Court has yet to rule on the constitutionality of an assault weapons ban.

The Gallup poll was conducted Oct. 1-19 among 1,200 U.S. adults. It has a margin of error of 4 points.
