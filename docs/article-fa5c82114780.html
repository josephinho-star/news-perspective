Tom Brady and Terry Bradshaw roast Aaron Rodgers for McCarthy breakup; both feuded with their coaches

2026-11-27 15:44

It was open season on Aaron Rodgers when Super Bowl ring stockpilers Terry Bradshaw and Tom Brady cooked the former Pittsburgh Steelers quarterback from their NFL analyst perches on Thursday.

Brady, working the Philadelphia Eagles' shocking comeback win over the Dallas Cowboys for Fox, lectured Rodgers for his handling of the Mike McCarthy claims and how the sanctity of an NFL locker room and appreciating and covering for teammates and coaches is a must.

Bradshaw, in studio for Fox, said he was not surprised Rodgers went public with "all his gripes and complaints" because "in my opinion, he's a strange guy, anyway."

The former Steelers quarterback, who helped the franchise win four Super Bowls in the 1970s and 1980s, also went on a rant about how athletes should not "turn on our teammates, we don't turn on our coaching staff, we don't turn on our ownership."

It was a good basting of Rodgers on Thanksgiving. But it also lacked some important historical context.

My take here is not a defense of Aaron Rodgers. But it is a defense of what is true.

And while it is true Tom Brady was the most accomplished NFL quarterback of all time and Bradshaw was a Hall of Famer on one of the NFL's greatest dynasties, there is history worth pointing out in this discussion about Rodgers.

Both talked extensively about the need to defend teammates while failing to recognize that during his 34-minute appearance on "The Pat McAfee Show" on Wednesday, Rodgers never once criticized a teammate.

He aimed his criticism entirely at Steelers head coach Mike McCarthy and the organization's handling of his benching.

Rodgers, in fact, explained he confronted McCarthy directly because he understood he was the only player in the locker room with the status to do it, while younger teammates with less job security couldn't, even though he claims they shared his frustration.

"I played for my teammates, I played for my coaches," Brady said. "There's nobody that wins when the family feuds."

To which he could have been asked if anyone wins when half the family suffers in silence because the guy running it is falling asleep, or ordering ice cream deliveries, or being late, or skipping practices?

And, yes, it can be argued Rodgers threw a proverbial stick of dynamite at McCarthy, and his teammates in the locker room had to scurry to snuff it out when reporters asked them questions.

But, amazingly, not one current Steelers player said Rodgers was saying things that were untrue despite having that opportunity. And this:

At halftime of NBC's Sunday Night Football game, former Steelers coach Mike Tomlin said Rodgers is "beloved" by his teammates.

"That is real," Tomlin said in a portion of the interview that did not air on NBC. "The guys in that locker room love and appreciate Aaron Rodgers."

Both Bradshaw and Brady criticized Rodgers for raising the Steel Curtain of confidentiality and exposing McCarthy's failings. Both thought the Rodgers criticism was untoward.

And that's a fair opinion in a vacuum.

It is not the same thing, but in 2017 television cameras caught Brady publicly berating Patriots offensive coordinator Josh McDaniels in front of teammates, a full stadium, and the audience at home.

And it is indisputable that Brady's relationship with Bill Belichick during their final year was a public soap opera. There were widespread media reports during the 2019 season of tension and a looming split between Brady and Belichick and it reached national news heights.

Both denied the reports publicly. So Brady definitely didn't do anything close to what Rodgers just did with his scorched-earth interview.

But how do we think the voluminous reports of a rift got out? It was because while Brady wasn't publicly talking about his frustrations, his confidants were. They served as his voice.

Also, it happened to a degree in Tampa Bay – again with extensively reported disagreements between Brady and Bruce Arians over offensive philosophy and play-calling.

We saw a replay of what happened in New England because, once again, people close to Brady spoke to reporters about the quarterback's frustrations.

As for Bradshaw, well, his defense of Steelers ownership and its coach was impressive. But there is some history worth considering. Bradshaw actually stayed away from the Steelers organization for about 20 years because he had a serious dislike for his coach, Chuck Noll, and owner Dan Rooney.

Bradshaw didn't think it was good for Rodgers to air dirty laundry about McCarthy publicly, but in the wake of winning four Super Bowl victories, Bradshaw vented to the press about Noll's conservative play-calling and harsh criticism.

"We show class on the way out," Bradshaw said on air. "Aaron Rodgers didn't do that."

Bradshaw apparently overlooked that his breakup with Noll was one for the ages, with him telling reporters he was "bitter" that Noll told other reporters the quarterback was "ready for his life's work," meaning his days after football.

When Noll died in 2014, Bradshaw skipped his funeral, pointing to his distaste for the coach spanning 40 years. When Rooney died in 2017, Bradshaw skipped his funeral as well, explaining he didn't really know him "that well."

But there was Bradshaw on Thursday talking about the Rooneys in glowing terms.

None of this context was applied to the analysis of Aaron Rodgers, in which quarterbacks with a history of disagreeing with their coaches, gave Rodgers no grace. It seems, for them, style trumps substance.
