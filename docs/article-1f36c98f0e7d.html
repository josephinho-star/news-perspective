New poll shows Talarico, El-Sayed hold narrow edge in Texas, Michigan Senate races

New polling shows that margins in Texas and Michigan’s key Senate races remain razor-thin, with Democrats just narrowly in the lead.

The new poll from Emerson College and The Hill puts James Talarico, the Democratic challenger in Texas, up by just one percent against Republican nominee Ken Paxton.

The prospect of flipping a Senate seat in deep-red Texas has long seemed a Democrat pipe dream. The last meaningfully competitive Senate race in the state was 6 years ago, when former Texas congressman Beto O’Rourke came within three percentage points of ousting Republican senator Ted Cruz.

But it’s seemed closer to becoming a reality ever since Paxton, the scandal-plagued Texas attorney general, unseated incumbent John Cornyn in a primary race with President Trump’s backing.

In Michigan, the poll found that progressive firebrand Abdul El-Sayed is faring slightly better against Republican Mike Rogers, a former congressman, with El-Sayed winning 48 percent of respondents’ support compared to Rogers’ 46 percent. Rogers, the poll found, wins out with non-college educated voters and older Michiganders, while El-Sayed is ahead with independents.

The Texas poll found that Democrats haven’t won over as many voters in the state’s race for governor, with challenger Gina Hinojosa lagging sitting governor Greg Abbott by 3 percent.

Jon Kyl, a longtime Republican congressman from Arizona, died on Thursday, his family said in a statement.

Over three decades in Congress – first as a member of the House of Representatives, and later of the Senate – Kyl established himself as a quiet titan of Arizona politics. His family attributed his cause of death to complications from a neurological disease.

Kyl announced in a statement late last year that he would be withdrawing from public life after being “diagnosed with a neurological disease manifesting as dementia.”

Kyl first joined the House of Representatives in 1987, an office he held before winning election to the Senate in 1995. He held his Senate seat until his retirement 2013, briefly returning by appointment in 2018 after the death of fellow Arizona senator John McCain.

House Republican releases campaign ad critical of Trump’s immigration policies

A Republican congresswoman has released a campaign ad criticizing Donald Trump’s approach to immigration enforcement, in the latest sign that a key plank of the president’s 2024 re-election campaign has become a liability for his congressional allies ahead of November’s midterm elections.

“Mr President, some of your immigration enforcement efforts have gone too far,” Florida’s María Elvira Salazar says in a 30-second ad. “In July, 50% of the 50,000 people that were detained had no criminal record. Be careful what your advisers are telling you about immigration policy.”

Salazar represents a competitive Miami-area district that is nearly three-quarters Hispanic, and where Trump won by a 15-point margin two years ago after a campaign in which he promised to carry out “mass deportations”. While his administration has implied that those targeted would be undocumented immigrants who committed crimes in the United States, in reality, a substantialportion of the people arrested since Trump returned to the White House in January 2025 have no criminal record.

Salazar won re-election by a 20-point margin in 2024, but this year is facing a significant challenge from Eliott Rodriguez, the Democratic nominee and a former television news anchor. A poll conducted for his campaign late last month found him tied with Salazar.

“The same Hispanics who helped you get to the White House in 2024 feel betrayed today,” Salazar said in the ad. “You can be for immigration what Lincoln was for slavery and Reagan was for communism. I’m not talking about giving them amnesty. I’m talking about giving them a dignified life in this promised land.”

Salazar’s ad is the latest instance of Republican lawmakers seeking to put distance between themselves and Trump before the 3 November elections, in which Democrats are vying to take back both the House and Senate. With gas prices high due to the war with Iran and Trump’s public approvallow – including on key issues like handling of immigration – Democrats increasingly believe their candidates are competitive even in heavily Republican seats.

Kennedy Center says building closed temporarily due to 'acute risks to public safety'

A top official at the Kennedy Center said that he closed the institution temporarily due to “acute risks to public safety resulting from continued structural deterioration” of the aging building.

Matt Floca, the Kennedy Center’s executive director and chief operating officer, said in a federal court filing seen by the Associated Press that the building will be closed for at least seven days to “assess existing safety risks in those portions of the main building exhibiting severe structural deterioration”.

The temporary closure, which occurred without notice yesterday, is separate from a vote on Tuesday by the Kennedy Center’s Trump-picked board to close the performing arts center indefinitely for repairs.

It was based on an inspection of the roof terrace canopy and a partial ceiling collapse earlier this month, Floca said. The closure may be extended after evaluation next week, he said.

The announcement of the closure came in response to a court case filed by Democratic congresswoman, Joyce Beatty, who is a trustee and sought an emergency court hearing to prevent what she called the building’s “unlawful closing’.

Beatty, an Ohio Democrat and an ex-officio member of the Kennedy Center board, has led efforts to block Donald Trump’s name from being added to the facade of the building. Beatty told MSNOW she had a verbal confrontation with the president on Tuesday during a virtual board meeting.

The Trump-aligned board, chaired by the president, voted to close the center after a federal judge blocked the institution from returning Trump’s name to the building.

Trump, who was named the Kennedy Center’s chairman last year by loyalists on the board, said yesterday that his efforts to restore the aging building deserve recognition, “because frankly, if we don’t do that, it’s going to close. It’ll end up being ripped down. It’s in very, very bad shape, very dangerous shape.”

Congress has allocated $257m to cover the repairs, but Trump said they only would happen if the board was permitted to move forward with plans to add his name to the building.

Anti-vaccine advocates take victory lap ahead of address from RFK Jr. in D.C.

As the death toll from the worst measles outbreak in decades mounts, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is headlining the Washington, D.C., conference of the anti-vaccine organization Children’s Health Defense, which he led for nearly two decades.

Longtime anti-vaccine activists celebrated their unprecedented role in the national conversation, at times expressing astonishment over the ways anti-vaccine sentiment has been elevated to the highest levels of the US government.

Kennedy, now serving as secretary of the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), brings unusual attention to the conference, said Mary Holland, current CEO of Children’s Health Defense (CHD). “We have an opportunity today to speak to our colleagues and our friends in mainstream media,” she said at the event on Thursday.

The organization laid out its policy agenda, including plans to challenge state-level immunization requirements for school attendance, to recruit younger people to the aging organization, and to combat the installation of cell phone towers for fears of 5G and 6G.

“Never, never surrender,” said Andrew Wakefield, a former gastroenterologist whose medical license was revoked after he was found to have fabricated research indicating a link between vaccines and autism.

Recent polling suggests that one-quarter of Americans now have doubts about the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccines following a years-long campaign against immunizations by once-fringe actors. But the majority of Americans also disapprove of the Trump administration’s handling of the widening measles outbreak, which killed three people in Texas and Arizona last year and four people in Pennsylvania recently.

Being called the “largest anti-vaccine conference, probably in the world” is “a red badge of courage,” said Sen. Ron Johnson, a Republican from Wisconsin. Johnson and Sen. Rand Paul, a Republican from Kentucky, rehashed grievances over the Covid pandemic and the immunizations that were rapidly deployed to stem the tide of illness and death.

Paul repeated incorrect statements that only elderly people died from Covid, but he also suggested that deaths among those who were “thin and healthy” may have been attributed to genetic factors, proposing genetic testing to determine vulnerabilities. “There probably is in all likelihood some genotype that predisposes you to this,” Paul theorized. “If you could go in and figure out a genetic test -- those are the things we should be looking for.”

Anti-vaccine activists became nervous earlier this year after the White House reportedly put the kibosh on speaking about vaccines before the US midterm elections. “February, March, April, May, vaccine reform got a little quiet, a little scary. You know, behind the scenes, we’re like, “Yeah, what happened?” said Michael Kane, CHD’s director of advocacy. He praised Rand Paul for bringing the anti-vaccine conversation back to the national stage.

Kane urged attendees to comment on the proposed HHS changes to the way it recommends vaccines, which experts say could sow confusion and chaos. “As we’re turning the corner and looking at the midterm elections, and this is now top priority -- I mean, sometimes I gotta pinch myself,” Kane said. “Just a few years ago, we were the, you know, hated stepchild in the corner... Now we are a national movement that isn’t going anywhere.”

Trump administration business allies look to pick off scraps of Cuba’s starved economy

Even as the Trump administration seeks to force regime change in Cuba through withering economic isolation, allies of Trump and Marco Rubio, his secretary of state, are angling to get rich off the island’s collapse.

Reporting for The Guardian, Lee Schlenker and Ed Augustin found that business interests connected with Trump and Rubio, the architect of current US designs on Cuba, are jostling to fill the void left by companies squeezed out of the island by the latest barrage of US sanctions.

A pair of Trump-friendly Texas business magnates are fighting to take over a Canadian company’s stake of Cuba’s mineral wealth.

Sources close to the matter say that Trump Organization leaders, operating under a company closely tied to the president’s family, have traveled to Cuba to explore hotel deals after a pair of Spanish hotel chains fled the island.

And Franklin Graham, a close ally of Trump on the evangelical right and head of the evangelical aid organization Samaritan’s Purse, recently won a $40m federal contract to oversee the distribution of humanitarian assistance on the island Trump is pushing toward collapse.

One source with decades of background in Cuba policy put it succinctly: “If I had done during the Biden administration what Trump’s friends have done now, I’d have been put in jail.”

The Trump administration has taken an especially severe stance toward Cuba since the start of Trump’s second term. Rubio, the child of Cuban immigrants, has led that campaign, seeking to bring about his long hoped-for goal of collapsing Cuba’s communist leadership and seeing a new US-friendly government in its place.

The goal of regime change in Cuba has echoes of the Trump administration’s playbook in Venezuela, where Rubio helped orchestrate the toppling of a leftist dictator and the funneling of the country’s oil revenues to US coffers.

Carney welcomes proposed special EU-Canada relationship that drew Trump's ire

Speaking to EU officials in Strasbourg on Thursday, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney seemed open to a proposal to grant his country a new “associate member” status within the bloc – even as details remained thin on what, exactly, that would entail.

“We welcome this ambition,” Carney said of the proposal. “Canada and Europe should secure our strategic autonomy through deep cooperation in the full range of strategic capabilities, including critical minerals, defense industrial capacity, AI and compute, energy security, space, and payments.”

Ursula von der Leyen, president of the European Commission, first raised the possibility of making Canada an associate member in an address on Wednesday. Her surprise suggestion to grant Canada – notably not in Europe – a heretofore unheard-of special status within the union appeared to catch member states off-guard.

The same was true for President Trump, who slammed the proposal as “laughable” and as a “hostile act” and went on to threaten sweeping tariffs against Europe if it follows through.

Carney tried to articulate what exactly deepened ties between the EU and Canada might look like, saying he was “not proposing a third bloc in order to become a great-power rival – only with better manners.”

“We do not seek power to dominate others,” Carney continued. “On the contrary, we are pursuing resilience so that no one can control our open markets, impair our sovereignty, threaten our territorial integrity, or undermine our freedoms, our democracies, our rule of law.”

Ilhan Omar brings bill to make ICE ‘pay the price’ for terrorizing communities

The US congresswoman Ilhan Omar is set to introduce a bill that would make federal immigration agencies “pay the price” for terrorising vulnerable communities, blocking nearly $140bn in funding and redirecting it to local recovery efforts, the Guardian has learned.

The Make ICE Pay Act, due to be introduced on Thursday, targets funding previously designated for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and Customs and Border Protection (CBP) under Donald Trump’s “One Big Beautiful Bill”.

It would send the money instead to states, local government authorities, nonprofit organisations and small businesses left reeling by Trump’s immigration enforcement crackdowns.

“For over a year, ICE has been tearing through our nation, leading racist terror campaigns that have caused devastating damage to our cities, to our businesses, and to the lives of our constituents,” said Omar, who represents Minnesota’s fifth congressional district, covering the city of Minneapolis and surrounding suburbs.

Beginning last December, thousands of federal immigration agents descended on Minneapolis and surrounding areas, resulting in nearly 4,000 total arrests and the fatal shooting of two US citizens, Renée Good and Alex Pretti. The mood of fear and isolation also had a profound effect on commerce.

Omar added: “In Minneapolis, Operation Metro Surge cost our community nearly $700m. We have a moral responsibility to end this abuse of power. Our bill will force ICE to pay the price for the damage they’ve left in their wake and ensure that these devastating operations can never be repeated.”

A UN fact-finding mission on Iran said it had reasonable grounds to believe the United States was behind two military strikes on a school and sports facility in Iran in February, and that these constituted war crimes.

It also found that Iranian authorities committed crimes against humanity during their deadly crackdown on anti-government protests.

The findings in a new report by the Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Iran, to be submitted to the UN Human Rights Council in Geneva, examined the US’s conduct during the Iran war that began in February and the Iranian government’s response to nationwide unrest that begun in late December.

The Iranian and US permanent missions in Geneva did not immediately respond to Reuters requests for comment.

US officials have previously said military operations are conducted in accordance with the law of armed conflict, while Iran has consistently rejected allegations of systematic rights violations.

The unprecedented spate of mid-decade redistricting for the US House has changed the congressional districts for one-tenth of Americans and significantly altered the makeup of at least one-fifth of districts, according to a new study.

A report by Protect Democracy found that 34.8m people - about one in 10 Americans - now live in a different district than they did during the 2024 election cycle.

And about one-fifth of all Americans now live in a district where more than a quarter of voters are new to the district compared with the last election cycle, AP reported.

The findings display the stark overhaul that partisan redistricting fights kicked off by president Donald Trump last year have caused for Americans across the country.

“If this trend continues, it really brings into question whether or not elected officials are really representing their constituents anymore, or if the voters are being randomly picked by mapmakers to squeeze out partisan advantage,” said Ben Raderstorf, a policy advocate at Protect Democracy and one of the paper’s co-authors.
