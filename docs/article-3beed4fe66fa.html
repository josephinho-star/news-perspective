‘Friday Night Lights,’ ‘Mosquito Bowl’ author says America has lost the Greatest Generation's sense of duty

2026-11-25 16:00

"Friday Night Lights" author Buzz Bissinger believes modern America has lost touch with the selflessness that once defined the "Greatest Generation."

The 72-year-old Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist penned the 2022 nonfiction book "The Mosquito Bowl," which was adapted into Peter Berg's new Netflix war movie of the same name. "The Mosquito Bowl" tells the true story of elite college football stars who walked away from fame and future wealth to enlist in the Marines and fight in the Pacific Theater during World War II.

While speaking with Fox News Digital at the film's premiere during the AFI Film Festival, Bissinger argued that kind of sacrifice has largely disappeared today.

"I think we've forgotten," Bissinger said. "I know they're called the Greatest Generation, but we have forgotten what it is like to serve, to have a sense of duty, to do something regardless of whether you're going to come back or not. And these are things we have to remember."

"We were together," he continued. "These men fought. They were never heroes. They never thought of themselves as heroes. They were ordinary men in extraordinary circumstances who felt it was really, really important to serve their country. And many of them didn't come back."

"And they need to be memorialized," he added. "It helps remind me of the greatness of this country, just how great this country was. There was no division. We were in unison, and these men personified that. And they had great lives ahead of them, but they didn't make it."

Both the book and the movie depict the Mosquito Bowl, a tackle football game played on Christmas Eve in 1944 on Guadalcanal between two Marine regiments whose ranks included some of the country's top former college football players. Months later, 15 of the 65 players were killed fighting in the Battle of Okinawa.

During his interview with Fox News Digital, Bissinger drew a comparison between modern celebrity sports culture and the dozens of elite college athletes who put their lives on the line in World War II, many of whom had already been drafted by NFL teams.

"I was actually thinking about how many pro football players have served in the military. The answer is virtually none," he said. "To have 65 football players who went to college and are now in the Marine Corps serving, forget it."

"It was a whole different era," Bissinger continued. "You gave up your dreams. You had no choice. You had to serve. It was in your heart."

He cited Dave Schreiner, a celebrated University of Wisconsin two-time All-American football star who turned down an NFL career and non-combat military posts to enlist in the U.S. Marine Corps and serve on the front lines.

Schreiner was mortally wounded during the Battle of Okinawa in June 1945 and died the following day at age 24.

"All of them could have gotten out, I think, because they were athletes, and athletes are coveted," Bissinger said. "But they didn't. They didn't. It was offered, and they said, ‘No, I have to go. I have to go.’ And that's pretty rare. You will not see that happen today."

Bissinger pointed out that many of the football players who fought in WWII could have used their status to get safer stateside opportunities but refused.

Berg's adaptation of "The Mosquito Bowl" focuses on four real-life college football stars who enlisted in the Marines after Pearl Harbor, including Schreiner, who was played by Broadway star Tom Francis, John McLaughry (Nicholas Galitzine), Bob Baumann (Bill Skarsgård) and Tony Butkovich (Ray Nicholson). The screenplay was co-written by Berg and Mark L Smith.

Bissinger spent five years researching and writing "The Mosquito Bowl" including digging through military records and family archives. While speaking with Fox News Digital, the author compared the process to a "detective story" since many of the first-hand witnesses and people who participated in the real-life Mosquito Bowl had died.

During his research, Bissinger made a personal discovery when he unexpectedly uncovered his own late father's service in the 4th Marine Regiment.

"He never talked about it. I didn't know, was he on the line? Was he a rifleman? Was he, you know, a waiter?" Bissinger recalled.

Bissinger, who was born Harry Gerard Bissinger III, explained that he was searching for his own name in the records since he was named after his father.

"So I looked up the roster records, and his name is my name," Bissinger said. "I'm the third, he's the second. And there's his name. But not only that, he is a rifleman, so he's on the lines. And he's in the 4th Regiment, which is one of the regiments that I was writing about, so I'm seeing all these names from the book and, for all I know, he could have been there. He could have been at the game."

"It was mind-blowing, and it put me over the top," he continued. "And what I learned — I'm so proud of him. And I stop because I get worked up. I just wish I could have said that 'I'm really proud of you. I'm really, really proud of you.' But he's been gone for a long time."

However, Bissinger told Fox News Digital that learning about his father's history established a connection that they didn't have when he was alive.

"I've never felt closer to him," Bissinger said.

Berg's late father, Laurence Berg, was also a Marine who served in the Korean War. Bissinger recalled he and the director, who are also second cousins, recently shared a moment in which they wondered how their fathers would have reacted to "The Mosquito Bowl."

"Last week, we were walking out of the Marine Corps Museum, and we both said, ‘What would it be like if our fathers were here?,'" Bissinger said.

"Now, they were kind of critical guys, so the first thing they would do is criticize the film, but then they would hug us, and they would feel such incredible pride, not just for us, but for the family," he continued. "It would have been a great moment, but we'll have to imagine it."

Bissinger said that while audiences remain hungry for stories about real-life heroes like his father and the men of the Mosquito Bowl, he believes Hollywood too often waters down the true cost of combat.

"I think what happens is, particularly in war movies, they ultimately get sanitized," he said. "Filmmakers, directors, studios are afraid of showing war as it is because they think people can't handle it. I think that's ridiculous, and I think it's wrong."

"In order for us to see just how tragic and brutal war is, we have to see it," Bissinger continued. "We have to see it. I tried to do that in the book and Pete has certainly done that in the film. People may flinch, people may not be able to handle it, but to me, it doesn't matter. If you want to know what war is like, you have to watch this film and read other books, and maybe, maybe collectively we'll say, ‘Enough.’"

Bissinger told Fox News Digital that he keeps a picture of Schreiner hanging on his wall to remind him of the harsh realities of war and the sacrifices made by the fallen players.

"I still can't quite comprehend that these guys are no longer with us," he said. "Dave Schreiner, beautiful guy, 6'2", perfect, the perfect two-time All-American, great son, great family, and his whole life ahead of him. And he died 12 hours before officials surrendered."

"And I'm just haunted by that," Bissinger continued. "Because he should have — they should have had a life. They didn't waste their lives, but it's still, in a sense, is the waste of a good human being. And that's just hard for me to overcome. So he reminds me, he keeps me going."

Bissinger admitted that ahead of watching "The Mosquito Bowl," he was worried about whether the film and its young cast would preserve the integrity of the men they were portraying. However, he told Fox News Digital that he was blown away by their performances.

"They did it to a T," he said. "They're all great. Ray Nicholson will knock your socks off. Bill Skarsgård, Tom Francis, Nick Galitzine — they were phenomenal. And so at a certain point, I'm not looking at them as people, they're making a movie about, I am seeing them now as the characters they were, and that was great."

"The Mosquito Bowl" marks a creative reunion for Bissinger and Berg. The director previously adapted Bissinger's 1990 bestselling nonfiction book "Friday Night Lights" into the acclaimed 2004 movie of the same name and later the hit TV series that ran on NBC from 2006 to 2011.

Bissinger also credited Berg, who is known for helming gritty, realistic filmmaking after having helmed "Lone Survivor," "Deepwater Horizon" and "Patriots Day," with refusing to compromise the book's spirit.

"He, to a T, wants to retain authenticity and the integrity of the book and he did that in ‘Friday Night Lights’ and since then we've really become brothers," Bissinger said. "We talk all the time. I pitch projects to him. He says, 'You're out of your mind. I'm not doing that.' But then something hits and it took a while. It took a little while."

"But once he got the thread, which is really about the loss of innocence, he got it," he said. "He's fun to work with. He is a hoot on the set. He has a great character. Hollywood needs more of them, I'll tell you."
