Trump’s tariff vow helped win the White House — now it may cost him Congress

2026-09-26 00:04

In 2016, Donald Trump’s support for tariffs helped propel him to a shocking electoral victory.

In 2026, his tariff policy may help tank Republicans in the midterms.

This turnabout counts as a surprise of Trump’s second term, and an unwelcome one for the president and his party.

During his political ascent in 2015 and 2016, Trump exploited issues that revealed a disconnect between popular and elite opinion, and trade was prominent among them.

His protectionism — expressed in pungent and excoriating terms — poached traditional Democratic ideological territory and appealed to the working-class voters, allowing him to crash the traditional Democratic “Blue Wall” states.

In sheer political terms, what could go wrong?

Well, nothing — until he got his way.

It turns out that Liberation Day was the beginning of the end for any appreciable political support for his tariff policy.

The assumption has usually been that the politics of tariffs favor protectionism — the benefits are very visible and focused for specific sectors and businesses that are highly committed to maintaining them, while the costs are widely dispersed among people who won’t care so much.

In this case, though, the logic has been turned on its head: The costs felt by people with no direct stake in protectionism are tanking support for the tariffs overall.

On top of this, there’s the fact that Trump won his second White House term promising to reduce prices — and import duties increase them.

The Yale Budget Lab estimates the costs to households at about $1,000 a year.

The shambolic and arbitrary rollout of the tariffs, as well as the dubious legal basis for many of them, hasn’t helped, either.

According to a poll that the libertarian Cato Institute conducted with Morning Consult, 74% of Americans say the tariffs increased the prices they pay, including 66% of Republicans.

About half of those polled, 53%, say the tariffs have weakened the economy, and 49% say they’ve hurt their quality of life.

After the latest trade spat with Canada, a Politico/Public First poll found that 56% of Americans think the tariffs on our northern neighbor were “wrong.”

According to a CBS/YouGov survey, people prefer a candidate who opposes Trump’s tariffs over one who supports them by 26 percentage points.

Democratic Senate candidates are promising to reverse Trump’s tariffs; even the card-carrying protectionist Sherrod Brown is critical.

And some Republican candidates in states with lots of trade with Canada have dissented from the trade war with Our Friendly Neighbor to the North.

The Midwest is a trouble spot for the GOP in the midterms, and it’s not hard to understand why.

China cut its purchases of US farm products in retaliation for Trump’s tariffs, and the farm sector has been slammed.

Exports of wheat, cotton, beef and pork to China plummeted last year, and overall agricultural exports to China fell by 66%.

Layer on top of this the price of diesel fuel jumping 80% and fertilizer increasing 15% due to the Iran war, and it’s a formula for outright revolt.

The tariffs have led to a tangle a little more than a month before Election Day.

Trying to address complaints about high grocery prices, the administration wants to relent on beef tariffs to help consumers, but farmers — hurt by other tariffs — are balking at a measure that will reduce prices at a time when they are struggling.

One way or other, someone is going to be unhappy.

Even if Trump’s theory of the tariffs is correct — that higher import duties will lead to re-industrialization — the policy involves immediate disruption and increased costs in exchange for a protectionism-driven build-out that will take years to be fully achieved.

Long before then — indeed, by January — an opposition congressional majority hostile to the president and his tariffs may be entrenched in Washington.

In politics, it’s often “careful what you wish for.”

Especially if that involves imposing higher prices on an inflation-fatigued public.
