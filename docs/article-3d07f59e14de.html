Trump’s ‘perverse’ Ukraine stance twists the path to peace

2026-11-18 00:08

There’s a place for realpolitik in American foreign policy, but it needs to be based on a correct appraisal of our interests.

President Donald Trump’s policy toward the Ukraine war has the trappings of realism — yet is too personal, and too hostile to an embattled ally, to constitute a true exercise in hard-headedness.

Trump has been a chameleon on plaid regarding the conflict.

At times tough on Ukraine, at times tough on Russia, at times sick of the whole thing, he’s lately settled on an unsympathetic line against Kyiv and an accommodating one toward Moscow.

Morally, this is perverse: Vladimir Putin is the one who launched a brutal war of territorial aggrandizement against Ukraine.

He has done everything he can to wreck the country, and to freeze and starve out its civilians during the coming winter.

Through pluck and technological ingenuity, Ukraine has found ways to hit back, targeting Russia’s energy infrastructure.

Concerned with bringing down energy prices ahead of the midterms, Trump tried to broker an “energy cease-fire” and inveighed against Ukraine for its continued attacks on Russian refineries.

These strikes, though, are Ukraine’s chief means of exacting pain from its invader.

Positing an equivalence between Russia and Ukraine regarding infrastructure attacks is preposterous; it brings to mind the old William F. Buckley line about the insanity of condemning both a man who pushes an old woman into the path of an oncoming bus, and a man who pushes her out of its way, for shoving old ladies around.

Ukraine has done everything it reasonably can to accommodate Trump, and Europe has stepped up in providing aid, just as we wanted.

Still, we’ve yanked Ukraine’s chain by slowing down Starshield — the SpaceX-developed satellite service that Ukraine uses for its drone warfare — and reportedly ceased intelligence-sharing for a time.

Why?

Trump considers Putin a big-time player, and is meeting together with him and China’s President Xi during this week’s Asia trip.

He clearly likes the idea of being part of a Big Three, the most exclusive club on the planet, strong leaders of powerful countries.

If he had his druthers, he’d probably enjoy breaking out his red pencil like Winston Churchill did in his 1944 meeting with Joseph Stalin, where they allotted postwar Soviet and Western influence over Eastern European countries based on their percentages of interest (in what’s known as the Percentages Agreement).

Zelensky, on the other hand, is surely a weak supplicant in Trump’s view.

Besides, he played an (unwanted) role in Trump’s first impeachment and he’s liked by all the wrong people.

Admired by the Western media and European leaders, Zelensky might as well be a less-fashionable, Eastern European version of Justin Trudeau.

Trump must also think of the Ukraine war as an annoyance, an inherited source of nothing but frustration, despite his hopes of forging a quick peace deal.

And Trump’s administration has a vision of detaching Russia from China, in a Nixon-Kissinger-type play from the Cold War.

This probably underestimates Russia’s and China’s common anti-Western animus, and their intertwined economic interests.

Russia gets goods from China that it once acquired from Europe, while China gets energy, with neither caring about promoting democracy or advancing human rights.

Perhaps above all, Trump believes in his capacity to make deals.

He’s always valued personal relationships, and imagines he has one with Vladimir Putin.

But he’s ignoring his own lessons about how to establish leverage.

As long as Russia thinks Trump might dump Ukraine — or at the very least, that it can string him along — it has every reason to keep trying to grind down its beleaguered neighbor.

The Ukraine war is unlikely to end soon, certainly not on terms that should be acceptable to the West.

But there’s no need to over-complicate what’s going on based on geopolitical bank shots or personal animosities.

As Churchill once said, “I decline utterly to be impartial as between the fire brigade and the fire.”
