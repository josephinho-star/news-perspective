Pentagon steps back from recently posted clinical guidelines on testosterone testing policy

The Pentagon has stepped back from its recently posted clinical guidelines for its new, mandatory testosterone deficiency testing policy for US armed service members age 30 and above, Reuters reported Thursday.

The Pentagon published a “Clinical Guidance for Health and Human Performance Optimization” Wednesday on its website. But by Thursday, Reuters said, this memorandum and a press statement were taken down.

“The draft (document) published on September 2, 2026, has been temporarily rescinded to allow for updates,” an official told Reuters, saying temporary guidance was still in effect.

The testosterone deficiency testing guidelines set forth different assessment paths for men and women that are supposed to take place during yearly health checks.

This guidance was poised to start right away. The Pentagon has claimed this testing was intended on strengthening military readiness by mitigating potential hormone problems and associated symptoms such as low energy.

Defense secretary Pete Hegseth announced the new testosterone deficiency screening program in July. In a video to X, Hegseth said he wanted to make sure service members had “the right testosterone levels to operate at your absolute best.”

“As we know, the modern battlefield is brutal and unrelenting,” Hegseth also said. “It requires and demands maximum psychological and mental readiness, and by addressing these health markers early, we’re keeping you on the leading edge of lethality, and giving you the same level of support that you give this nation – the absolute best.”

Those with low testosterone--a condition which has become a frequent topic of conversation in conservative circles--can get treatment for “restoring and optimizing”, Hegseth said. This treatment would be voluntary.

The US House of Representatives passed a Republican-backed bill that would penalize universities that participate in boycotts of Israel, or attempt to prevent students from taking part in exchange programs with the country.

The Protect Economic and Academic Freedom Act passed by a 237-169 vote, with 33 Democrats breaking with the rest of their party to vote in favor, and only two Republicans opposed. It now goes to the Senate for consideration.

The measure would require that colleges and universities who participate in federal student aid programs not participate in boycotts of Israel and also mandates that institutions receiving federal funding for international study and foreign language programs certify annually that they do not impose restrictions on their students participating in programs in Israel, or on students from Israel participating in programs on their campuses.

A federal judge in Boston is considering whether to continue her ban on Donald Trump’s proposed federal oversight of mail-in ballots.

Boston federal court Judge Indira Talwani said Thursday that the US Postal Service had not provided her information about how it would carry out a plan to regulate mail-in ballots for midterms, per the Associated Press.

“We are 70 days from the election and I have nothing from the USPS about how this will happen,” Talwani reportedly told Justice Department lawyer Michael Velchik.

Talwani’s comment comes several days after a whistleblower alleged that the US Postal System’s mail-in ballot screening was tossed together so quickly that just one scanning glitch could disqualify batches of ballots.

Trump signed an executive order in March tightening federal control over mail-in voting, ordering the postal service to ballots against a federally approved voter roll before sending them. This order marks a shift from the postal service’s traditional role of delivering ballots provided by the state.

Talwani last week set a 14-day temporary restraining order to prevent this system from being implemented. In June, Talwani barred the Trump administration from using the system before the midterm election.

The US Supreme Court in late August rejected Talwani’s June order, saying it was premature, AP explained.
