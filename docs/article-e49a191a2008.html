Heart attacks are on the rise under 40 — the hidden drivers and warning signs you might miss

2026-11-23 11:00

When you’re young, it’s easy to feel invincible. But that sense of safety can mask a troubling shift in cardiovascular health.

A disease long associated with older men is increasingly affecting younger adults of all genders.

One of today’s most alarming cardiovascular trends is the steady rise of heart attacks among young adults. Recent data suggests that about 1 in 5 heart attack patients is 40 or younger, while hospitalization rates among adults ages 18 to 44 have climbed significantly.

The data paints a stark picture: A 2026 study in the Journal of the American Heart Association found that, between 2011 and 2022, deaths from severe first-time heart attacks among adults aged 18 to 54 rose 57%. Although men account for more heart attack deaths overall, women may face a higher risk of dying after a first heart attack.

This increase in early mortality and severe complications from first-time heart attacks threatens to reverse decades of medical progress.

A heart attack in someone’s 20s, 30s or 40s can cut short decades of productive life, imposing an enormous emotional, physical and financial burden on patients and their families.

Here’s what you need to know about the forces behind this troubling trend, the commonly overlooked warning signs, how symptoms can differ by sex and why some people should begin screening in their 20s.

The hidden drivers of early heart attacks

The forces behind this shift are conditions that once appeared much later in life but are now taking root in childhood and early adulthood.

Obesity is a major driver. Your 20s, 30s and 40s are critical decades for building lifelong cardiovascular fitness. Staying active during these years can help support longer life and fewer health complications with age. Losing that reserve early can set off a dangerous domino effect.

Obesity can contribute to high blood pressure, physical inactivity and insulin resistance, increasing the risk of Type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome and, ultimately, heart attack.

Researchers are also identifying lifelong cardiovascular risks that can emerge early. Medicine has traditionally focused on LDL, the “bad” cholesterol, but a growing body of evidence points to lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), as another important risk marker that can affect heart health throughout life.

The warning signs you might miss

Early recognition is critical.

Everyone should know the classic warning signs of a heart attack: chest pressure or discomfort, pain in the left arm and jaw pain. But it is just as vital to recognize atypical symptoms.

A dull ache or sharp, burning sensation below the ribs and above the belly button, back pain or feeling unusually faint, uneasy or short of breath during exertion can all signal a serious problem.

New, persistent or exertion-triggered chest symptoms warrant immediate medical attention. The symptoms could indicate a heart attack, so call emergency services rather than driving yourself to the hospital.

Why women can face greater risks

Men experience heart attacks more often overall, reflecting a combination of biological factors and higher rates of certain risk factors, including smoking and substance use.

But women often can have worse outcomes in part because their heart attacks may be recognized later. Although women often experience the same classic symptoms as men, they may be more likely to report symptoms that are considered atypical.

This delay can also reflect bias in medical evaluation, when clinicians assume that a younger woman is unlikely to be having a heart attack and do not fully investigate the possibility.

Women are much more susceptible to spontaneous coronary artery dissection, or SCAD, a tear in the wall of a coronary artery that can occur during or after pregnancy.

It’s easy to assume that someone who looks healthy and fit has a low risk of heart disease, but genetics can play a powerful role in cardiovascular health.

Reducing obesity remains important because of its serious health effects, but body weight alone is not a perfect measure of heart health. Someone who carries extra weight does not automatically have coronary artery disease, or CAD.

Conversely, a person who appears healthy, exercises regularly and eats well can still develop severe CAD. Genetic factors can contribute to elevated cholesterol and other cardiovascular risks, even in people with otherwise healthy lifestyles.

The screening to consider in your 20s

Routine cardiovascular screenings are rarely top of mind for young adults, allowing silent factors to go unnoticed until a major event occurs.

Young adults should discuss cholesterol testing with their primary care clinician. A lipid panel in the early 20s can help identify elevated cholesterol early, particularly for people with a family history of heart disease or multiple cardiovascular risk factors.

When it comes to heart health, early detection and prevention can make all the difference.
