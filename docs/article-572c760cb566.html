Ministers to create new stalking offence and scrap need for proof of fear or distress

2026-11-26 00:01

Ministers will create a new stalking offence so that victims will no longer have to prove fear or distress to seek protection under the law, the Home Office has announced.

Under current laws, the underlying offence of harassment in England and Wales, as well as additional factors such as the victim’s alarm, must be established for it to be considered stalking. The new law, alongside other changes, will focus on the perpetrator’s behaviour instead, officials said.

The move has been welcomed by family members of murdered stalking victims and organisations campaigning on behalf of women who have been the target of sustained harassment.

The parents of 24-year-old Alice Ruggles, who was murdered by her obsessed ex-partner in 2016, welcomed the findings and said they were “heartened” by the government’s commitment to clarify and strengthen the law.

Clive Ruggles and Sue Hills said changes would “bring in due protection for stoic victims like Alice”.

The victims’ commissioner, Claire Waxman, said the plan was welcome but added that the announcement was “an opportunity missed” because the government had not introduced measures to tackle convicted stalkers.

A review commissioned last year by the government and led by the barrister Richard Wright, who acted for the prosecution in Ruggles’ case, said the offence of stalking “needs to be both uncoupled from harassment and redrawn in its ambition and scope”.

Under two stalking offences introduced in England and Wales in 2012, the underlying offence of harassment must be established and additional factors such as fear or distress proved for it to be considered stalking.

In his review, Wright said that despite good intentions “we have arrived at a position where there are dozens of offences, all with very similar foundations, all tackling similar behaviour, using slightly different terminology, and having an array of different maximum penalties”.

He said: “If we add to that landscape the minefield of protective orders, with so many varieties and initialisms, created and amended in so many different pieces of legislation, the legislative position as a whole across the realm of interpersonal abuse offences is cluttered and confused.”

Alongside pledging to change the law to make stalking a standalone offence, the government has said it will strengthen sentencing so stalkers could face up to 10 years in prison.

In an immediate change, the Home Office said courts could now directly impose stalking protection orders (SPOs) to protect victims by restricting the alleged perpetrators’ behaviour, even if they have not yet been convicted.

A so-called “super-complaint’ from anti-stalking organisations in 2022 set out concerns including how there had been flawed investigations, instances where stalking was not being properly identified, and police officers “minimising or trivialising” such behaviour.

Waxman, who has been the victim of a stalker for 20 years, said the government had “missed a crucial opportunity by failing to address stalking that continues after conviction”. Her office wanted the government to give police powers to arrest convicted stalkers who repeat their criminal behaviour on one occasion.

“A conviction should not be a reset button for a stalker. Victims should not be left trapped in a cycle of abuse because the law fails to recognise continuing patterns of behaviour,” she said.

Emma Lingley-Clark, the interim chief executive of the Suzy Lamplugh Trust, said: “We fully support the government’s commitment to introduce a standalone stalking law, which we hope will reduce confusion and increase the shockingly low conviction rate for stalking, which is currently only 1.2% of stalking incidents recorded by police.”
