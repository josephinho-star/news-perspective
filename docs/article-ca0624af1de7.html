US Senate ‘permitting bill’ threatens clean water protections to speed up energy projects

2026-11-26 19:16

An industry-backed “permitting bill” approved by the US Senate would gut key clean water protections with changes that would do little to speed up the development of major energy projects, advocates warn.

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (Baaja) passed the Senate 62-33 and is now being debated in the House. The Baaja is a “permitting reform” bill that aims to reduce the time it takes federal agencies to approve major energy and infrastructure projects, like power lines, fossil fuel pipelines, natural gas plants and datacenters.

But the legislation also includes provisions that would make it more difficult for states to put in place protections against water pollution, double the amount of time between automatic reviews of water pollution permits, and reduce transparency around the types and levels of water pollution.

More than 50 groups in communities impacted by Pfas “forever chemical” water pollution have come out against the bill, warning that they would suffer further if the legislation is approved because it would weaken some of the few mechanisms in US law that regulate the chemicals’ discharge. The law would “put us in more danger”, said Emily Donovan, co-founder of the Clean Cape Fear advocacy group in Wilmington, North Carolina, where drinking, surface, and ground water is polluted by discharge from a nearby Pfas factory.

“We already see how hard it is for the [Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)] to [regulate] Pfas,” Donovan said. “What frustrates us is we don’t need to make it harder.”

Baaja has drawn wide industry support, including among trade groups like the American Chemistry Council and American Petroleum Institute. It is opposed by virtually every major US environmental group because it would eliminate some foundational environmental protections.

The bill’s supporters say the cost is worth it because it will allow energy projects to be built faster, and hasten the transition to clean energy. Opponents say industry is using the need to transition to clean energy as cover to ram through a range of unnecessary changes to water laws that do nothing to improve energy permitting.

“BAAJA is a handout to the oil and gas industry, while doing very little to solve the permitting problems being used to justify those changes,” the Clean Water Action advocacy group, which is lobbying against the law, said in a statement. “Congress is trading away protections for states, tribes, wetlands and streams even though those protections are largely unrelated to the years-long delays at the center of the permitting debate.”

Among other changes, the bill would allow industry to sue to undo the EPA’s guidance for states on water pollution discharge. The EPA currently develops criteria that states use to limit discharge into waterways. The agency does this without issuing a formal rule, and the criteria cannot easily be challenged in court.

The Baaja would require a formal rule-making process, which would slow the clean water criteria issuance by several years, and open it to lawsuits. The criteria are used by states to develop limits on Pfas water pollution, and the change would shift more responsibility onto states that are largely under-resourced and unable to meet the challenge, Donovan said.

Another provision would change the EPA’s mandatory review of water pollution permits from five years to 10 years. The EPA uses this review mechanism to require polluters to track their Pfas and other water pollution.

Donovan pointed to the Pfas pollution in North Carolina to highlight how the provision would prolong the public’s exposure to dangerous chemicals. North Carolina regulators in 2019 put in place a health advisory limit of 140 parts per tn (ppt) for GenX, one of the main Pfas compounds polluting the state’s waterways. By 2022, the EPA established a health advisory of 10 ppt.

Advocates fear that the extended review period for pollution permits from five to ten years would create a scenario in which a company issued a water permit in 2020 may continue to pollute at higher levels for much longer.

“Imagine not being able to make a change for 10 years despite that the science is showing that a chemical is more toxic than originally thought – that’s what terrifies and frustrates us,” Donovan said. The transition to clean energy does not need to sacrifice clean water, she added.

“We can get clean energy without having to cause cancer,” Donovan said.
