Reform suspends two officials over donations sting as Farage admits their comments ‘don’t look good’ – UK politics live

2026-09-04 12:04

Here are some more lines from Nigel Farage’s interview with LBC this morning where he gave his response to the undercover report about an apparent Reform UK plot to evade election donations rules. (See 9.07am.)

Farage insisted Reform UK had not broken any laws – but he admitted aides had said things they “perhaps should not have been said” and conceded he was “not happy” about this. He said:

The party has broken no laws. The party has not taken any dodgy money or anything like that whatsoever.

However, it is a clear case of entrapment, and they worked at it for a very, very long time, and they got a couple of our contractors to say things that perhaps should not have been said.

And as a result of that, you know, they have been removed this morning. And yeah, you know, I’m not happy about it.

He rejected the claim that the footage of him discussing a £500,000 donation with fake father-and-son donors (see 9am) showed that he was aware of a plan to circumvent electoral law. He said:

If you listen to what I said, I said, is this all above board? Is this all legal? I’ve been told it was a global family office based in London. And by the way, I was in the middle of a Clacton by-election. I turned up to see people. I knew nothing about them whatsoever. I meet people all the time.

He claimed the three polls featured in the investigation were not commissioned by Reform UK. He said:

Whilst the poll benefited Reform, it wasn’t commissioned by Reform.

It had nothing to do with Reform. It was an independent third party that did it. So that’s nothing. That’s nothing. Polls are funded by overseas people every single week. Every single week.

In fact, the report contains footage clearly suggesting the polls were carried out at at the behest of Reform UK.

He suggested Labour and the Tories had a worse record on donations because they in effect sold peerages. He said:

The Labour Party and the Conservative Party have been selling peerages for years in return for political donations. Political donations leading to big government contracts.

In the world of political funding, all sorts of things have been done over decades that are wrong.

He said the party had suspended two aides because their remarks “don’t look good”. He said:

I promise you, the polling is not even an issue. Nothing. The party has not done anything wrong, but entrapped conversations don’t look good, and that’s why we’ve taken the action we have.

What former CPS elections law chief says about legal implications of Reform UK investigation

Nick Vamos used to be head of special crime at the Crown Prosecution Service, which meant he was in charge of all prosecutions for electoral offences. He is now a partner at the law firm Peters & Peters. He has issued this statement about the legal implications of the undercover investigation into Reform UK.

UK electoral law can be fiendishly complicated but, on this issue at least, the rules are very clear. Only UK-registered voters, companies or other similar entities are permitted to make donations to political parties. A donation can include incurring expenses on behalf of a party, as is alleged to have happened here.

The criminal net is cast very wide: it is against the law for anyone knowingly to do any act in furtherance of an arrangement likely to facilitate an impermissible donation. This would include engaging in discussions to solicit or encourage an impermissible donation, provided the person knew that was what was being proposed.

It is also an offence to mislead a political party about the source of a donation, so the perpetrators of the sting, whatever their intentions, may themselves have broken the law. Each offence carries a maximum sentence of 12 months’ imprisonment.

It is very common for allegations of electoral offences to be reported to the police, especially by political rivals looking to score points. But the police should disregard the motives behind any report and focus on the conduct itself. They are not obliged to investigate every allegation, but will assess the evidence to determine whether a full investigation is justified.

What journalists and commentators are saying about Reform UK undercover investigation allegations

Here is some comment from journalists on the significance of the investigation into Reform UK.

Tim Shipman, the Spectator’s political editor, says in a long article this morning that the “stench of incompetence” is deadly in politics. Here is an extract.

[Nigel] Farage should also be cognisant that the mood in the bars around Birmingham last night was utterly mutinous. Those who are putting themselves forward as Reform candidates confided their despair. ‘We are giving up our businesses and putting family lives on hold to fight for Reform and this is going on behind the scenes,’ one said …

The overwhelming reaction in my WhatsApp groups from operatives in the other main parties was incredulity at the recklessness of it all. ‘If this is how they talk to people they don’t know, imagine what they are saying to people they trust,’ observed one former Downing Street Tory …

So why did they do it? It is the experience of everyone I have ever known near the summit of politics that political operations take their character and operating logic from the person at the apex of the triangle. Leaders set culture. The worrying thing for Farage admirers is that if [Dan] Jukes and [James] Orr behaved like this it is because they believed it to be okay by the boss. Both boasted on camera of their closeness to the leader. In the meeting at the pub with the fake donor, Farage seemed both au fait with how the money would be transferred to Reform, and grateful for it.

The scrappy, sod the rules, bugger the establishment vibe comes from him. This is another reminder of the timeless rule that every major politician’s greatest strength is also their greatest weakness. Farage’s insurgent image has put him in a place where he might win power but made his operation demonstrably less suitable to exercise it.

And Stephen Bush from the Financial Times makes a very similar argument in his Inside Politics column this morning.

Individually, these accusations [the ones about evading rules in the undercover investigation, and the Zia Yusuf racism claims – see 11.47am] are deeply damaging, but together they are even more so. In addition to the serious evidence that Channel 4 has uncovered, the manner in which Reform’s bigwigs conduct themselves in the video makes them look a bit ridiculous. A political party can survive being seen as mean or soft-hearted or a number of other things. But if it is seen as silly it cannot be a serious prospect for power.

Add that to the allegations of racism — something beyond the pale for many British voters — and the party is fishing in a small pool indeed. There are people who will vote for a ridiculous party. There is a smaller number who will vote for a party facing allegations of racism. But the number who will vote for a party that is both ridiculous and facing allegations of racism is smaller still. Ditto the number who will vote for a party that is ridiculous, facing allegations of racism and committed to the socially conservative agenda Reform has increasingly adopted (and to which the likes of Orr are firmly committed).
