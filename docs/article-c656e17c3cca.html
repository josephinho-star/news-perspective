Trump defends under-fire commerce secretary after allegedly slow-walking export restrictions on advanced AI chips

Donald Trump also defended his commerce secretary Howard Lutnick today, after he came under fire for allegedly slow-walking export restrictions on Nvidia chips to China. This comes after the company’s CEO, Jensen Huang, was ousted from his role following a revelation that he had a close relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), allegedly a Chinese operative.

Lutnick is now set to testify before Congress about delays by the commerce department in adding Singapore-based Megaspeed, which is associated with Alice Huang and has bought billions of dollars of Nvidia chips in recent years, to Washington’s blacklist of companies that the US considers a risk to national security despite reported concerns within the administration.

Today, Trump justified any delayed action by Lutnick, adding that he’s done a “very good” job. “If you didn’t delay it, if you did do something, you could have a blow up with China. We’re having a very good relationship with China right now,” Trump said.

Lutnick is currently traveling with the president and other senior officials and cabinet members to Manila.

Meanwhile, Scott Bessent, Trump’s treasury secretary, said in an intervie with CNBC that he has “for months” been in favor of putting Megaspeed on the blacklist – but Lutnick’s commerce department “has the pen”.

“You will have to ask them about their internal process,” he added on Friday.

Today, Trump said he understood both officials’ perspectives.

“One wants to be friendly with China and one wants to have a blowup with China. They both are acceptable but they are different views,” the president told reporters.

Ted Cruz questioned the necessity of the $50 bn the White House has requested to upgrade air traffic control infrastructure after Friday’s four-hour nationwide flight stoppage.

“A lot of that $50 billion has very little to do with safety,” said Cruz, the senior senator from Texas who chairs the Commerce Committee which oversees the FAA, on his podcast Monday.

“It’s for things like improving airports and basically expanding amenities, which in a time where we’ve got massive national debt, I’m not sure expanding amenities at airports is a critical priority. But safety most definitely is a critical priority.”

Cruz noted that in July 2025, Congress approved $12.5bn to be spent over five years to upgrade air traffic control and hire more controllers.

On Friday, transportation secretary Sean Duffy first asked Congress to approve $30.1 bn in upgrades, before increasing the figure to $50 bn later that day.

Third US aircraft carrier arrives in the Middle East

A third US aircraft carrier has arrived in the Middle East, according to officials. The USS Theodore Roosevelt has now been deployed to the “area of responsibility”, US Central Command (Centcom) said on Monday. The carrier joins the USS George HW Bush and USS George Washington.

Centcom said that move is “part of a scheduled deployment”, even though the US war with Iran enters its ninth month and peace talks have stalled.

Until April this year, the presence of three warships in the region hadn’t been seen since 2003 during the Iraq war.

Last week, Trump has said that the war in Iran is “effectively over” as he played down concerns that the US would escalate its military campaign against Tehran after the midterm elections.

“At this point, taking the military action doesn’t seem to be a very necessary thing – but we’re locked and loaded, ready to go for any reason,” he told reporters during a briefing at the White House.

Here's how Democrats will pick the new Senate majority leader

Democrats may have secured the Senate majority, but one key contest is still ahead. Over the coming weeks, Chuck Schumer and Chris Van Hollen, along with any late challengers, will vie for the party’s top Senate leadership post before lawmakers cast their votes. Here are four things to know about how the election works.

A secret, closed-door election: Unlike the public Speaker of the House vote, where lawmakers cast ballots on the chamber floor, the Senate leadership contest takes place behind closed doors. Senators vote by secret ballot, and individual votes are not disclosed.

The winner only needs a caucus majority: The Democratic leadership race is decided solely by members of the party’s Senate caucus. If Democrats hold 55 seats, a candidate would need 28 votes to win. If Democrat Mary Peltola wins in Alaska and the party reaches 56 seats, the threshold rises to 29 votes.

The vote is scheduled for next month: As things stand, the election is set for 1 December. Both incumbent senators and incoming Democratic members will participate, while retiring and outgoing senators will not vote, despite many having publicly weighed in on the race.

Republicans choose their leaders separately:Republicans hold their own closed-door leadership elections in both chambers. Earlier this month, House Republicans selected hardliner Jim Jordan as their leader after Mike Johnson opted not to run for minority leader. In the Senate, Republicans unanimously backed John Thune to continue leading the conference.

As the leadership battle for Senate majority leader heats up between progressive Democrat Chris Van Hollen and Chuck Schumer, the current minority leader, we’re watching for more on how the two are securing support of the wider caucus.

While several Democrats have said they don’t intend to back Schumer, they haven’t necessarily pledged to support Van Hollen.

Democratic senator-elect Josh Turek, who flipped Iowa’s open seat in the recent midterms, told reporters today that he’d spoken to both lawmakers but is still waiting for “further plans” from them about how they will deal with affordability in the US.

“I like both,” Turek added. “I have not given my allegiance to any leadership or any party, and I told Iowans I was going to sit down with them and say: ‘What are you going to do for Iowa?’”

Nvidia's board not considering bringing back Jensen Huang – reports

As Donald Trump and tech leaders clamor for his return as Nvidia’s CEO, the company’s board of directors is reportedly not considering bringing back Jensen Huang.

The board is focused on an investigation into Huang, and is not in discussions to bring him back, CNBC reports this morning, citing a person familiar with the matter. Reuters hears the same, also citing a person familiar.

Huang resigned on Thursday after reports that he had at least two extramarital affairs, one with a woman accused of spying for China and another with a subordinate.

Elon Musk and other tech bosses have called for Huang’s reinstatement, amid reports the AI figurehead is already plotting a return to the chip company, just days after he was ousted. An open letter that has more than 165,000 signatories demands that the company’s board members “bring Jensen back while an independent investigation establishes the facts”.

The secluded island of Ni‘ihau has long been a Republican stronghold in deep blue Hawai‘i. Ni‘ihauans have an unusual tendency to unanimously support conservative candidates, like in 2020, when all 43 Ni‘ihauan voters cast their ballots for Donald Trump.

This has been the case since as far back as the early 1900s, when Ni‘ihau’s ballots were sometimes relayed to the neighboring island of Kaua‘i via carrier pigeon. The headline of one characteristic 1939 Honolulu Star Bulletin article reads – “Ni‘ihau Casts 59 Votes All For GOP as Usual.” Bush beat Kerry 39-0, Reagan beat Carter 56-1, and Nixon beat Kennedy 99-0.

But this month, for the first time in recent memory, the island backed a Democrat for governor, casting 14 of their 21 votes for governor Josh Green and lieutenant governor Derek Kawakami. Has Ni‘ihau been caught up in the blue tsunami?

Zohran Mamdani, New York City’s mayor, announced on Monday that he was disbanding a controversial city police unit that has faced criticism over the years from civil rights groups who have accused the unit of using excessive force and unduly aggressive tactics against protesters.

In a video released on Monday morning, Mamdani said the move stemmed from a campaign pledge he made to disband the New York City police department’s strategic response group (SRG). “Today, we are keeping that promise,” Mamdani said in the video.

An accompanying joint statement from Mamdani and NYPD commissioner Jessica Tisch said the overhaul would “transform how the city prepares for and responds to protests, placing communication, de-escalation and civilian coordination at the center of our approach, while protecting New Yorkers’ constitutional right to protest”.

Along with eliminating the SRG, which was founded in 2015 ostensibly to focus on counter-terrorism, Mamdani and Tisch’s statement also said that the city planned to establish a “first-of-its-kind, civilian-led Community Dialogue and De-escalation Division (CDDD)” within the mayor’s office of community safety.

That division will not have weapons or arrest powers, unlike the SRG, according to the announcement.

The city will also establish what they described as a “specialized dialogue team” that will be known as the “First Amendment Support Team” (Fast) within the NYPD’s community affairs bureau, Mamdani and Tisch’s statement said. The creation of Fast is meant to “improve the NYPD’s protest response”, and it will consist of 30 uniformed officers trained in communication and de-escalation, the mayor and police commissioner said.

Trump continues to blame Biden administration for nationwide ground stop despite two years in office and Republican control of Congress

Trump also continued to shift responsibility to Joe Biden administration for the national ground stop over the weekend, that saw thousands of flights cancelled.

This is just the second nationwide ground stop of all departures since the 9/11 attacks. The previous one came in January 2023, when the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) paused all domestic departures for less than two hours.

“I hate to blame anybody, but the Biden administration hooked up a lot of copper into glass, and if you know anything about electrical engineering you cannot hook up copper into glass,” Trump claimed. “A lot of money was wasted, but the equipment is very old, in some cases more than 50 years old, and we essentially want to blow it up and put a new one in, and we can do it simultaneously with very little trouble. So that’s what we’re doing, but we need the funding to get it done. But essentially what you saw on Friday was very, very old equipment that had a heart attack.”

Trump’s claims comes after Congress, under Republican control, passed a $12.5bn air traffic control package in 2025, as part of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The president, however, appeared convinced that lawmakers would be now more willing to allocate more funding to upgrade the systems. “I think the one good thing that comes out of it I’m sure Congress now will give us the money,” he told reporters today.

Over the weekend, Sean Duffy, the transportation secretary, said in an interview with ABC News that the existing funding did not provide money for “a new architecture for telecom”.

“I don’t have any money given by Congress to build a new software system to manage the airspace. They’re very specific on how we spend the 12.5 and none of the money goes to this telecom that I have to build,” he said.

However, the top House Democrats on two key transportation and aviation committees said in a statement that, as of now, $8bn remains unobligated and $11bn is unspent.

Today, Trump also scolded a reporter for questioning why he has spent considerable time devoting energy and resources to White House renovation projects, instead of the FAA upgrades.

“You’re so pathetic,” he said. “I have a responsibility to make sure the symbol of our country is in decent shape, and it takes me 10 minutes a day. Very little time … That building, if you looked a year ago when I came back, was in horrible condition.”
