Giant robotic claws will grab dead satellites in space

2026-10-25 12:54

Space is getting crowded, and I'm not talking about astronauts. Thousands of pieces of junk are circling Earth at incredible speeds. Some are dead satellites. Others are leftover rocket parts or fragments from past collisions. Any one of them could cause serious trouble if it hits a working spacecraft.

Now Britain has a pretty wild solution. It's developing a robotic spacecraft with giant claws that can grab dead satellites and move them toward a fiery end in Earth's atmosphere. Think of a garbage truck in orbit, except this one has to track down its trash while both objects are traveling thousands of miles per hour.

The UK is putting roughly $81 million behind the project. And if everything goes according to plan, the mission could change how we deal with the growing mess above our heads.

Britain's $81 million plan to clean up space

The UK Space Agency has selected aerospace company ClearSpace UK to develop a spacecraft specifically designed to remove dead satellites from orbit. The contract is valued at approximately $81 million. The project is part of Britain's Active Debris Removal mission, known as UK ADR. The spacecraft is targeted for launch in 2029 from SaxaVord Spaceport in Scotland's Shetland Islands.

Its first job will be to remove two defunct UK-registered satellites from low Earth orbit. The government wants both satellites removed by 2032. If successful, the mission would demonstrate that one spacecraft can capture and dispose of multiple dead satellites without being sacrificed after its first cleanup. That could make future operations far more practical, especially as the number of satellites in orbit continues to grow.

How giant robotic claws will grab dead satellites

Catching a satellite sounds straightforward until you consider what's happening in orbit. Objects in low Earth orbit typically travel at roughly 17,000 mph or faster. Some dead satellites may also tumble, making them difficult to approach safely. That's where ClearSpace's robotic technology comes in. The spacecraft will use a specialized capture system to approach and secure an inactive satellite. First, it must carefully coordinate its movements with the target. Then its robotic capture mechanism will take hold of the object.

Once the satellite is secured, the spacecraft will move it into a lower orbit and release it. Atmospheric drag will eventually pull the abandoned satellite down, causing it to reenter Earth's atmosphere, where it is expected to burn up. The cleanup spacecraft itself is being designed for potential refueling and reuse. That means it could remain in orbit for future work instead of ending its mission after disposing of one satellite. Of course, engineers still have to prove the technology can handle these demanding operations safely.

Why space junk is becoming a serious problem

You might assume there's plenty of room in space for a few broken satellites. Unfortunately, some of the orbital regions where satellites operate are becoming increasingly crowded. According to the European Space Agency's space debris statistics, updated October 14, 2026, approximately 47,140 objects are regularly tracked in Earth's orbit. That figure includes functioning satellites, inactive spacecraft, rocket bodies and debris.

And those are only the objects large enough to track reliably. ESA estimates that millions of smaller pieces also surround Earth. The danger comes from their enormous speeds. A piece of debris measuring just one centimeter can carry enough energy to disable a spacecraft. ESA has also warned that an impact of that size could penetrate the protective shielding of the International Space Station.

Now consider what happens when two large objects collide. They can break apart and produce many additional fragments, each capable of damaging something else. Scientists call this potential chain reaction the Kessler syndrome. If enough collisions occur, certain orbital regions could become extremely hazardous for future missions. ESA's 2026 Space Environment Report warns that active debris removal will be necessary to prevent long-term debris growth driven by collisions.

Space junk also threatens astronauts

The danger extends beyond expensive satellites. Astronauts aboard the International Space Station face risks from debris traveling through orbit. In November 2021, crew members took shelter inside their spacecraft after a Russian anti-satellite weapons test created a dangerous cloud of debris. NASA confirmed that astronauts and cosmonauts closed hatches and sheltered inside docked Dragon and Soyuz spacecraft while teams evaluated the threat.

Fortunately, the station and crew remained safe. The incident demonstrated how quickly debris from a satellite breakup can disrupt operations in orbit. A serious impact could damage critical systems or puncture a spacecraft's pressurized structure. That's why space agencies continuously monitor debris and sometimes adjust spacecraft orbits to avoid potential collisions. Removing larger abandoned satellites could help prevent future collisions that create even more fragments.

Could space junk fall back to Earth?

Here's another concern that deserves attention. Some space debris eventually returns to Earth, and certain components can survive the intense heat of atmospheric reentry. Most smaller debris burns up. However, larger objects and components made from heat-resistant materials can sometimes reach the ground. As rocket launches increase, researchers are paying closer attention to this danger.

Space environmentalist Ewan Wright, from the University of British Columbia, told Space.com that approximately 124 rocket stages reentered Earth's atmosphere uncontrolled in 2024. He estimated that if that annual level continued for a decade, there would be a 33% probability of at least one person somewhere in the world being killed by falling rocket debris. That estimate describes a cumulative global risk from rocket stages, rather than the danger posed by Britain's two satellite-removal targets.

ESA also reports that the risk of casualties from uncontrolled reentries has increased in recent years, although the overall probability remains low compared with many everyday risks. The takeaway here is that removing debris from orbit requires careful planning. Operators must consider what happens when that material eventually returns to Earth.

A new space cleanup industry could be taking shape

Britain's mission could have implications well beyond the two satellites it plans to remove. For years, many satellites were designed primarily to complete their missions and then be retired. Some remain in orbit long after they stop functioning. Now companies are developing spacecraft capable of approaching other satellites and performing tasks that once seemed incredibly difficult.

ClearSpace's mission could help prove that robotic spacecraft can safely capture objects that were never designed for servicing. Its planned refueling capability is particularly interesting. Instead of launching a new cleanup vehicle for every job, operators could eventually service an existing spacecraft and send it after more debris.

That could open the door to a new commercial industry. Satellite operators might eventually pay specialized companies to remove aging equipment or extend the useful lives of working spacecraft. However, the UK mission remains in development. Its long-term commercial potential will depend on whether the technology performs as expected.

You probably don't spend much time thinking about abandoned satellites while checking the weather or following GPS directions. But the technology operating above Earth plays an enormous role in everyday life. Satellites support communication networks, provide navigation signals and deliver information used for weather forecasting. They also help scientists monitor changes on Earth and support emergency response operations.

GPS satellites operate in medium Earth orbit, much higher than the low Earth orbit debris targeted by Britain's mission. Still, the broader effort to keep orbital regions safer helps protect the infrastructure that supports satellite-based services. A collision that disables an important spacecraft could disrupt operations and force companies or government agencies to rely on backup systems. Replacing a damaged satellite can also be expensive.

Britain's robotic cleanup mission won't eliminate the space junk problem overnight. But successfully removing two abandoned satellites could help establish a practical way to deal with dangerous objects already in orbit. And with thousands of additional satellites being launched, finding a reliable way to retire them safely could become increasingly important.

I have to admit, the idea of a spacecraft chasing down dead satellites with giant robotic claws sounds like something Hollywood dreamed up. But I find the technology fascinating, especially when you consider how much we depend on satellites without giving them a second thought. What really caught my attention is the possibility of reusing the cleanup spacecraft. That could make removing space junk a routine service rather than an occasional experiment. Still, I wonder whether we're moving fast enough. With companies launching more satellites every year, cleaning up two abandoned spacecraft feels like a small start. I'd like to see satellite operators take greater responsibility for what they leave behind. After all, we wouldn't tolerate companies abandoning broken equipment on our highways. Why should Earth's orbit be any different?
