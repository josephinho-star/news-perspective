‘Blade Runner 2099’ Episode 1 recap: Human behaviour

2026-11-25 16:00

In professional wrestling, to run the blade is to hurt yourself. The phrase refers to the practice by which wrestlers surreptitiously cut their own heads open with hidden sharps, creating the illusion that they’ve been bloodily injured during their matches. In one sense, the violence is real: Those are real cuts, bleeding real blood. In another sense, since the pain is all self-inflicted and the extent of the injury controlled and easily healed, it’s not really violence at all. It’s just how business gets done.

Though the practice goes back decades, the etymology for the specific phrase run the blade almost certainly traces back to Blade Runner, Ridley Scott’s titanically influential 1982 science fiction film. (Future icons of the squared circle the Ultimate Warrior and Sting briefly wrestled as a tag team called the Blade Runners, so it’s not like the boys back then were unfamiliar.) In Scott’s film — it and the rest of the franchise are based on characters and concepts from sci-fi/counterculture legend Philip K. Dick’s 1968 novel Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? — blade runners are specialized cops, one of whom, Deckard, is played by Harrison Ford. Blade runners track down and “retire” rogue replicants, manufactured flesh-and-bone humanoids who look just like real humans, bred with enhanced strength and minimal empathy for optimal slave labor.

As an homage, run the blade has little to do with blade runners. Self-injury has nothing to do with it. It’s just how business gets done. But like a wrestler with a razor blade in his wrist tape, blade runners do tend to wind up battered and bloodied. And their business is telling the difference between what’s real and what’s only looks real. So hey, if the blade slits, run it.

When Blade Runner 2099, the astonishing new Prime Video series from creator Silka Luisa, begins, much has changed since our last live-action look at this dystopian world, courtesy of director Denis Villeneuve’s 2017 sequel Blade Runner 2049. Between the events of the first film, set in a somehow even worse 2019 than the one we actually lived through, and the show, set 80 years later, the replicants first created by the Tyrell Corporation have rebelled, and been banned, and been reapproved and remanufactured, and rebelled again. The supposedly docile “Nexus-9” models created in droves by Tyrell’s successor, the Wallace Corporation, led a successful revolution, seizing control of major portions of the human world and its space colonies, including the crucial and vast megalopolis of Los Angeles.

The ruling replicants are hard at work cleansing Earth’s ruined environment, to the point where you can actually see sunlight from the ground for the first time in franchise history. But they have also turned humanity into the same kind of underclass they once served as, doing the disgusting and dangerous jobs humans once made replicants do. This includes both menial labor on Earth and the often deadly work of terraforming planets and moons to serve as off-world colonies for Earth’s elite. The remaining human rebels have been driven underground, and are hunted by specialized replicant cops who, with bitter irony, call themselves blade runners.

One thing that hasn’t changed is how good it all looks. Director Jonathan Van Tullekken, cinematographer Rob Hardy, lead concept designer Paul Inglis, production designers Inglis and Chloe Arbiture, costume designer Helen Huang, and visual effects supervisors Viktor Müller and Aleks Pejic, along with many others, have created one of the most sumptuous sci-fi worlds on television.

Light glows through a haze of dust or flares in a shroud of mist. Buildings tower into the sky at a colossal scale, dwarfing the impoverished people on street level scrambling to make a living thousands of feet below. Massive moving billboards advertise products both familiar and bizarre as flying cars soar past. The image quality itself is grainy, making the world look organic and lived-in. Rays of sunlight add a whole new suite of colors to the setting’s palette. Simply put, this thing looks incredible.

Meanwhile, composer Leopold Ross’s synth-and-sax score hums, pulses, and occasionally screams, like it’s the city’s throbbing heart. A cold open set in 2008, in which a Tyrell Corporation salesman (Freddie Fox) tries to reassure a hesitant potential customer (Michael Smiley) that there’s no possible way replicants could ever rebel, shows just how well the filmmakers can evoke the feeling of the original film, only for them to then move forward confidently with their own vision. The result more in line with what Scott and cinematographer Jordan Cronenweth were doing in crafting the wet, industrial, cyberpunk look of their film than what Villeneuve and the legendary Roger Deakins did with vast, placid vistas of color in the beautiful but somewhat bloated sequel. But it’s a fruitful synthesis of both approaches, combining Scott’s granular grasp with Villeneuve’s eye for near-cosmic spectacle.

Into this more or less perfectly constructed new Blade Runner world step two actors who seem born to be there. Michelle Yeoh stars as Olwen, a replicant who’s the proverbial cop just eight days from retirement. Since she’s a replicant, in her case this means she has just over a week until her manufactured body reaches its expiration date and dies. Working with an oleaginous newer model named Yahya (House of the Dragon’s Matthew Needham), she’s a blade runner, tracking down a pair of couriers who delivered a rebel bomb.

One of them is Cora (Hunter Schafer), a no-nonsense survivor type who, in the words of Robert De Niro, does not let herself get attached to anything she is not willing to walk out on in 30 seconds flat if she feels the heat around the corner. (Anything except her sick brother Elias, that is.) On the run after serving as a courier for a package that turned out to be a bomb (she didn’t ask because she didn’t care), she saves her own skin by tricking her partner Isaac (Sam C. Wilson) into running for the street and creating a diversion. She sees as Olwyn catches up with him and brutally black-bags him, with a suffocation hood that extends an Alien-style breathing tube down your esophagus. (Thank you, executive producer Ridley Scott.) It’s nasty business, and I’m sure Palantir is hard at work developing them for ICE as we speak.

Now Cora needs another fake ID. She goes to Bebeto (Dimitri Abold), a city worker slash underground doctor and veteran of the human-replicant wars who can help get people new identities, but he says that won’t be enough for her this time. She has to get all the way out of Dodge to Mexico City, where humans are still free. But to come up with that kind of paperwork for herself and her brother, he needs her to steal a piece of evidence for him straight from LAPD headquarters. That will require going undercover in the belly of the beast, not just as a replicant, not even just as a cop, but as a blade runner, the kind of person who hunts people like her.

Cora’s preparation and infiltration as “Marian Jessup,” her new replicant identity, takes up the bulk of the episode, and it too is nasty business. First, Bebeto inscribes serial numbers on her eye and hipbone to help her pass for a manufactured being. The hip process is bloody and painful, requiring a dose of a knockout drug that Bebeto administers by breathing it directly into her mouth. The image glows white as she fades into oblivion — a beautiful, sensual moment amid the ugliness.

Cora puts on the anime-ass uniform of a blade runner and, somehow, successfully infiltrates the LAPD’s towering stronghold, its slow quasi-analog technology making every step unbearably tense. Her guide winds up being Yahya, a real creep who keeps her from getting to the evidence locker, though she does acquire a gun. She finally winds up partnered with Olwen as they interrogate a human suspect, immobilized in his hood by a breathing tube that descends all the way from the ceiling.

Surprise! The human is none other than Cora’s spurned partner Isaac. He’s eager to settle his score against her for getting him arrested, but rather than out as an undercover human immediately, he insists on speaking only to the brass. When Olwen goes to get someone, the two humans engage in a brutal brawl, one Cora only survives by hanging Isaac with the breathing tube. Give it up for Hunter Schafer, who joins Harrison Ford and Ryan Gosling in the hallowed tradition of Blade Runner protagonists getting the absolute dog piss kicked out of them.

Of course, you’re not supposed to be able to get beaten up by a human if you’re a much more powerful replicant, so everyone wonders why the hell “Marian” let it happen. They have a human child (Katelyn Rose Downey) run the Voight-Kampff test on her, a quiz made up by Dick in his novel and used in the original film to determine if someone is human or replicant by measuring their empathy levels.

To her shock, Cora aces the test, proving completely immune to empathy — even when the child fake-begs the undercover human to take her with her as one final test. That’s a level of soullessness that impresses even the suspicious Olwen.

The aging (dying, really) blade runner is pursuing a bizarre case of her own. Marek (Johnny Harris), her former partner off-world and coworker on Earth (they had to separate due to LAPD policy on fraternization), has ditched his post and run. He’s the first LAPD replicant ever to do so, and potentially a huge scandal that could upset the delicate human-replicant political equlibirium. Now it’s Olwen’s job to track him down and “retire” him.

As for “Marian,” she is taken away, stripped, tied up, hung upside down, and hosed off. (Look, folks, I just work here.) Medics slap a sci-fi goop on her face that heals her wounds almost instantly. Olwen comes in and asks her why she let something weaker than her beat her up. “I’d never killed anything before,” the human says.

“Could you do it again?” Olwen asks. Humans remain a threat, she says, even as we watch Marek walking through the desert to some vast abandoned structure elsewhere in what’s left of the American West. But unfortunately for them, “It’s our time now.”

I’ll say! This is an extremely impressive debut, presenting a fully constructed world — not just in terms of lore or gadgetry, but in the sense that it looks like a full, complete, replete place. The light hits a certain way. The air has a unique quality to it. The blur of distant headlights illuminates a vast industrial hellscape is not quite like the blur of distant headlights anywhere else. You get the sense that even the smells would be different, the scent of the streets, of people’s clothes, of their hair. The explosions are real fireballs. Even the glitchy opening credits are unique on TV today and feel like an entryway into 2099.

Every now and then, Amazon’s TV division knocks it completely out of the park on an aesthetic level. Shows like Dead Ringers, Them, Too Old to Die Young, The Underground Railroad, and ZeroZeroZero, just to name a few, immediately looked and felt brutal and beautiful like nothing else on television before them ever had. We’re in the early going yet, of course, but Blade Runner 2099 seems poised to join that company of cult classics, but starting from a much more prominent position in pop culture. If that exposes more people to TV this bold and sumptuous in its image-making and sound, long may that blade run.
