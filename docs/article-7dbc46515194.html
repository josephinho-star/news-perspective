US Army says controlled blast of explosives in Iowa was 'huge success'

2026-11-26 00:23

The US military has hailed as a "huge success" the detonation of nearly five tonnes of toxic explosives in Iowa, an operation that sparked fears of health and environmental problems across the region.

Smoke was seen rising into the sky shortly after 09:00 local time (14:00 GMT) following the explosion, as the Army declared the risk "as close to zero as is absolutely humanly possible".

Plans to detonate the lead azide, a volatile compound reportedly at risk of exploding on its own, were paused several times before the blast following public outcry and legal challenges.

On Wednesday evening, the Army said that an "all clear" has been issued around the ammunition plant.

President Donald Trump said shortly after the blast that reports showed mitigation efforts "far exceeded expectations".

"The Army's controlled, mitigated detonation exceeded all expectations and was a HUGE success," Adam Telle, acting secretary of the US Army, wrote on social media.

Despite widespread concern from residents of Middletown, Iowa, ahead of the blast, officials have said that lead readings in the area so far have been promising.

In a Wednesday evening update on X, the Army said that the Federal Emergency Management Agency (Fema) had issued the "all clear" around the plant and that there was no airborne lead detection following the detonation.

"Post-detonation surface sampling is underway, and results will be released once complete," the Army added.

Environmental Protection Agency administrator Lee Zeldin also said on social media that in the hours after the detonation "all EPA air monitors continue to report a ZERO DETECT of airborne lead".

The explosives had been in storage for around 20 years, and degraded as part of a process known as recrystallisation - this made them more responsive to accidental detonation from friction or other movement.

No level of lead exposure is considered safe, with children and pregnant women particularly vulnerable to harmful effects.

Middletown Mayor Rodney Johnson told the BBC he felt "relieved" after the detonation.

He watched the blast from his job at a manufacturing plant eight miles (12km) away from the blast - and monitored a camera at his two-bedroom home just one mile from the detonation site.

"We did not feel anything. We did not see anything," Johnson said.

Also eight miles away from Middletown, Sandy Mabeus closed the windows and the garage at her home, brought their outdoor cat inside and hunkered down.

The family watched three separate live streams on iPads and televisions of the explosion as it happened.

"We did not feel or hear a thing here," she told the BBC, adding that she checked the videos to confirm it had actually happened.

"The waiting and wondering has not been a good thing for anybody in the area," she said. "Hopefully they had a very good result and we can just get on with things."

Iowa farmer David Vandenberg lives about 20 miles from the blast site and said he first learned of the detonation around two weeks ago. The Army Corps of Engineers took samples from his yard and pond on Tuesday.

"I was listening this morning, and I did hear a rumble about nine o'clock," he told the BBC on Wednesday, but didn't see or smell anything.

Vandenberg expects officials to return to take more samples but "was happy to see that the blast was relatively contained. "I think the effects will be minimal"

Illinois Governor JB Pritzker said on X that his state was "seeing little to no impact" from the blast. In a press release, he said he was directing state agencies to remain on standby for any "unanticipated impact".

The Iowa site is only 10 miles (16 km) from the Illinois border.

But there is also caution from some.

Iowa's incoming Governor-elect Rob Sand said there was "more testing to do" and called for "immediate transparency" to explain why the lead-up was "so chaotic and unclear".

Sand said that any Iowans who have been impacted in any way "must be taken care of fully, right away".

Cameron Hamilton, the Fema administrator, said there would be additional resources and testing in the coming days and weeks.

In recent weeks, the US Army scheduled and rescheduled their detonation ahead of colder winter temperatures.

It also warned locals of "potential exposure to toxic lead dust", which led to some panic. It instituted measures including an optional evacuation zone and a shelter-in-place advisory ahead of the detonation.

The Army faced pushback from residents and politicians alike and there were legal challenges to prevent the detonation from going ahead on environmental and health grounds.

But on Monday, the appeals court overturned a decision to halt the blast. It said that - while these concerns were valid - the risk from an unplanned explosion was greater.

Sheila Flynn and Kwasi Gyamfi Asiedu contributed to this report
