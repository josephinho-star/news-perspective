Trump considering pardon for Huang after Nvidia CEO accused of breaking export control laws

Hello and welcome to the US politics live blog.

Donald Trump has said he would consider pardoning Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, if necessary, but added that he did not think it would come to that.

Chuck Schumer, the current Senate minority leader vying to lead the Democratic majority in the next Congress, yesterday accused Huang of shipping “extremely advanced chips to China” and violating US export control laws.

Schumer said the move “amounts to the equivalent of selling rocket engines to the Soviets during the space race”.

But in an interview with the the New York Post, the president – took credit for convincing Nvidia’s board to reinstate Huang - admitted he could intervene again to help the embattled CEO.

“It’s always an option, I guess, but I don’t think it’ll be necessary,” the president told the rightwing tabloid owned by his supporter, Rupert Murdoch.

Asked about Huang’s upcoming testimony to a US Senate banking committee hearing on export controls, Trump said that he sees no reason for the chief executive, who admitted an affair to a Chinese woman suspected of links to Chinese intelligence, to invoke his Fifth amendment right to avoid answering questions that might incriminate him.

“I don’t think he needs to take the Fifth. I think it’s an interesting story for people,” Trump said. “I think I want him to just tell the truth, and I mean, his lawyers are going to have to advise him, but I’ve always found him to be 100% aboveboard, and you know a very smart person.”

Nvidia’s board reinstated Huang on Monday after Donald Trump says he intervened on the executive’s behalf, assuring company leadership that he did not pose a national security risk despite questions surrounding his relationship with Alice Huang, a tech executive of no relation who has been accused of acting as a Chinese asset and routing advanced chips to Beijing via her datacenter company, Megaspeed.

Trump also told the New York Post that he would meet with senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed, the Michigan Democrat he repeatedly lied about on the campaign trail last month, when he referred to the Muslim physician as “a jihadist”. “He’s a smart guy,” Trump told the tabloid’s reporters.

Kilmar Ábrego García, the Salvadoran national whose mistaken deportation turned him into a symbol of Donald Trump’s mass deportation efforts, has landed in Spain.

Ábrego García arrived in Madrid on Wednesday after leaving the United States of his own volition. Prior to his departure, Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents met him at the airport and removed his ankle monitor, according to We Are CASA, a Maryland-based advocacy group.

The interaction, the group noted, was “a powerful symbol that ends a chapter marked by illegal deportation, imprisonment, abuse at Cecot, retaliatory prosecution, and government surveillance.”

As he made his way through the Madrid airport, Ábrego García did not speak to reporters, instead raising his fist in the air as he climbed into a waiting van.

In a statement provided by We Are CASA, he described his departure from the US as proof that justice was still capable of prevailing over the most powerful government in the world.

“In this case, despite everything they tried to say, every obstacle they put in my way, and every attempt to shame me, justice prevailed under this administration in a way that many people never thought was possible,” he said.

US inflation hits highest since May after prices climb 3.8% over the last year

Consumer prices rose an annualized 3.8% in October, according to new inflation data released today, the highest since May and the last inflation reading before a crucial US Federal Reserve board meeting next month.

Prices rose 0.3% from September to October amid soaring energy prices, according to the Personal Consumption Expenditure index, the Fed’s preferred inflation measure from the commerce department’s Bureau of Economic Analysis.

The data comes after the Bureau of Labor Statistics separately reported prices climbed 0.4% from September to October, higher than economists had anticipated.

Energy prices, including gas prices at the pump, have cooled, going from a high of $4.41 a gallon in early October to $3.61 a gallon by mid-November, according to AAA.

In early December, the Fed will hold the last of its interest rate-setting meetings for the year, and officials are widely expected to hike interest rates for the second time this year. The Fed has set a target inflation rate of 2%, a level not seen since February 2021. Higher interest rates make it more expensive to borrow money, slowing price increases and raising the price of loans like mortgages and student debt.

Though officials held rates steady at the last board meeting in late October, minutes from the meeting showed that some expressed concern about inflation’s stickiness.

“A few of them warned that further delay risked allowing high inflation to become entrenched or necessitating a larger and more disruptive tightening later,” the minutes said.

The move would come at a tumultuous time for the US economy. A new survey from the Conference Board released on Tuesday showed consumer confidence had ticked up in November to its highest level since January 2025, probably driven by the shake-up from the midterm elections.

At the same time, US stocks have been fluctuating from a mixture of uncertainty over datacenter regulations, continuing scandals coming out of Nvidia and overall investor concern about the seemingly limitless amount of money being spent on building AI and the infrastructure to support it.

'We regret the lack of joint action': EU hits back at Trump's plans for more tariffs

The European Union has hit back at Donald Trump’s plans for more tariffs which come almost a year after his previous “liberation day” levies were struck down by the US supreme court.

According to a statement by the US trade representative “the EU’s unreasonable cars, policies and practices” include “industrial plans to expand manufacturing”; policies to “promote production an exports”, “sustained market access barriers” and “preferential lending” to target domestic sectors.

These increase the US economic security risks especially in cars, chemicals and pharmaceuticals, it says.

The current tariff plan is to impose a 15% levy on EU imports, pegging it at the same level agreed with the EU and Trump’s course last July.

“We will study the findings. At the same time we strongly reject the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity. To the contrary, Europe is among those most exposed to overcapacity,” the EU said, a possible reference to imports flooding in from China.

The EU and the US share a real concern about global overcapacity, much of it state-driven and concentrated in third countries. We regret the lack of joint action.

Iowa braced for army blast that could spread lead plume over 100-mile radius

The US military is set to detonate 68 barrels containing nearly 9,500lb of lead azide in Iowa at 10am ET, moving ahead after multiple delays and despite an outpouring of public concern about potential health and environmental effects.

Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, a small town in south-eastern Iowa, where the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant is situated. The army has warned that the blast could release a plume of toxic lead dust extending up to 100 miles northeast, possibly reaching cities such as Wapello and New Boston, Illinois.

Trump plans to hike tariffs on Chinese goods by a further 20% and new levies for EU and other major trading partners

The Trump administration is planning to hike tariffs on Chinesegoods by an additional 20% and raise import taxes on products from the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and nine other major trading partners, with DonaldTrump seemingly undeterred by his resounding midterms defeat, in which his handling of the economy was a major factor, and a large cohort of his previous levies being struck down by the supreme court.

The US Trade Representative’s office announced the tariffs after completing an eight-month unfair trade practices probe into 16 trading partners, including Bangladesh, Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Thailand and Vietnam. For now, Mexico and Norway have pending investigations and are excluded from new levies.

The tariffs are still at the proposal stage and the administration has not provided strict timelines on possible implementation, the Associated Press reports.

The new duties will take into account any trade deals agreed with the Trump administration and will not stack on top of some previous tariff rounds, except for China, which faces the 20% tariff on top of the 25% imposed in 2018, during Trump’s first term, over forced technology transfers.

The new levies are being imposed under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows Washington to address “unfair” foreign trade practices, deemed “unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory foreign government practices that burden or restrict U.S. commerce.”

A group of more than 120 climate scientists have urged US lawmakers to not pass contentious legislation to speed up the building of energy projects, datacenters and other infrastructure, amid a growing backlash to the sweeping permitting bill.

In an open letter to members of the House of Representatives, the collection of Earth and social sciences researchers expressed “deep concern” that the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (or Baaja) weakens environmental laws, locks in new fossil fuel projects without recourse and delivers only minor cuts to planet-heating emissions.

“We understand the need for trade-offs and compromise when passing bipartisan legislation,” they wrote. “But as scientists we feel strongly that the costs of this bill to the climate are too high.”

Michael Mann, a climate scientist who helped arrange the open letter, said that he did so because Baaja is a “deeply problematic bill” that is “backed by a veritable who’s who of bad fossil fuel and dark money actors”.

Mann said that Democrats, who take control of Congress in January, should “scrap Baaja, which in the end only takes us further down the dangerous fossil fuel pathway, and put a sensible, popular datacenter bill on Trump’s desk and force him to veto it. And force Republicans to defend sustaining that veto.”
