China, Russia, US Talk UN Reform, Nuclear Risks, and Ukraine at Trilateral Summit

2026-11-19 15:26

On November 19, the presidents of China, Russia, and the United States – respectively, Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin, and Donald Trump – held a trilateral summit in Shenzhen, China, on the sidelines of the APEC summit. Hailed as “historic” and “groundbreaking” by the governments involved, the summit resulted in a joint declaration on U.N. reform, energy supply chains, and nuclear risk management.

According to the readout from China’s Foreign Ministry, Putin, Trump, and Xi “engaged in deep discussions on the theme of ‘Safeguarding World Peace and Promoting Strategic Stability in Major-Country Relations.’” It’s no coincidence that this wording (which was not found in the U.S. or Russian news releases) borrows heavily from existing Chinese formulas. Xi framed China as the driving force behind the summit, pointing to its previous work to develop bilateral ties with both Russia and the United States “with a view to fostering major-country dynamics featuring peaceful coexistence, overall stability, and balanced development to the benefit of each other and the world at large.”

That’s a selective rewriting of the actual events – Putin first raised the idea of such a summit, then Trump endorsed it. Xi seems to have been a reluctant host who decided to turn the event to his own advantage.

China’s goal for the summit was a big-picture look at how “major countries” can prevent conflict among themselves and “find the right way to get along.” That theme allows Beijing to claim a role as a global leader and peacemaker, without getting into the annoying details of actually resolving anything. Xi’s prescriptions were predictably vague and, on the surface, unobjectionable: “mutual respect,” “peaceful coexistence,” “win-win cooperation” and “dialogue and consultation.”

What that means in practice is, essentially, the old idea of “spheres of influence” – major powers “respecting” one another’s “core interest and major concerns.”

“China, Russia and the United States should be partners and friends,” Xi declared.

Only two issues received any concrete mention in the Chinese readout. The first was the global energy crisis, although China tactfully refrained from mentioning the proximate cause (ongoing wars in Ukraine and Iran, sparked by Russia and the United States, respectively). China, Russia, and the United States agreed to “jointly maintain stable and unimpeded energy industrial and supply chains,” Xi said.

On nuclear issues, another topic of much interest ahead of the summit, Xi issued a firm warning that “A nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought; it must be prevented with all efforts.”

Artificial intelligence (AI) received scant attention. China’s readout made a brief mention, attributed to Putin, that the three countries should “strength international cooperation on artificial intelligence governance.” There was no specific mention of the potential military use of AI, including in nuclear command and control, in any of the official readouts following the meeting.

After their summit, the three leaders issued the “China-Russia-U.S. Heads of State Declaration toward Peace.” The document committed the three countries to United Nations reform to allow the global body to “respond more effectively to global challenges.” They also noted join plans “to increase the production and supply of refined petroleum products for the benefit of all nations.” There were no specifics on either. U.N. reform, in particular, is a widely shared goal in theory, but nearly all the U.N. Security Council members disagree on the specifics.

Worryingly, China, Russia, and the United States also found common ground in insisting that international law cannot touch state officials and needs to operate on the “principle of state consent” – effectively allowing a government to veto international accountability. The statement specifically mentioned the International Criminal Court, which has an arrest warrant against Putin for war crimes related to the invasion of Ukraine. Trump has also repeatedly denounced the ICC for its supposedly biased investigations into Israel’s conduct in Gaza, and the U.S. government has slapped sanctions on the ICC and its judges.

It’s telling that, out of only four clauses in the joint statement, one point was dedicated to asserting state immunity from international legal scrutiny. It was especially ironic – and troubling – as on the same day the International Court of Justice found Myanmar guilty of genocide, a ruling that other states will have to enforce if it is to have any impact.

The final point in the declaration reaffirmed that “a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought,” echoing Xi’s statement in the Chinese readout. Ahead of the summit, Trump had repeatedly expressed his desire to use the talks to push for trilateral nuclear arms control, a prospect China rejects due to its far smaller arsenal. According to the declaration, all three did agree to “conduct a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons.”

The leaders’ discussions on specific global issues that the world is interested in barely featured in the public readouts at all. There was a single brief mention in the Chinese summary that “[t]he three heads of state also exchanged views on major regional and international issues, including the Ukraine crisis, preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons, the Middle East situation, and the Korean Peninsula.”

What, exactly, Putin, Trump, and Xi said (or potentially agreed) about the wars in Ukraine and Iran, and North Korea’s nuclear program, remains unknown. But Putin’s assertion that the three leaders shared “many common interests regarding the resolution of regional conflicts – including, of course, the conflict in Ukraine” is bound to alarm other stakeholders. U.S. allies in Europe, as well as Japan, South Korea, and Australia, strongly oppose both Russia’s military action in Ukraine, as well as Moscow’s alliance with North Korea.

Beyond the trilateral summit, Putin said he and Trump had “devoted a great deal of attention” to the war in Ukraine in their bilateral meeting, “at the initiative of the president of the United States.”
