Ankara silent on Azerbaijan hijab restrictions despite Erdoğan’s condemnation of headscarf bans

2026-10-03 19:18

Rabia Hale Seferoğlu, Warsaw

Turkey’s government has not publicly responded to Azerbaijan’s new school dress rules or reports of girls being barred from classes for wearing headscarves, despite President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan’s condemnation of school headscarf bans at the UN General Assembly in New York on September 22.

Erdoğan did not name any country in his speech, which came a day after Azerbaijan’s Cabinet of Ministers amended its student dress code in a way widely interpreted as a ban on the headscarf.

“It is also worth noting that as our world changes rapidly, humanity has begun to face new social threats at the same time,” Erdoğan said. “Foremost among them is Islamophobia, which is spreading like the plague in the West. Muslim women and girls in particular have faced more discrimination in recent years. We view the headscarf bans some countries have put into practice in schools as an attack on women’s most basic rights.”

Erdoğan spoke publicly about Azerbaijan the same week. In a September 27 post on X marking Azerbaijan’s Remembrance Day, he said Karabakh is Azerbaijani land and will remain so. He also called Azerbaijani President Ilham Aliyev that day. Azerbaijan’s presidency published an account of the call that did not mention the school dress rules.

Azerbaijan’s Decision No. 309, signed by Prime Minister Ali Asadov on September 21, bars students at state schools from wearing additional items that disrupt the uniform or are inconsistent with the principle of secularism in general education. The word “hijab,” the Islamic headscarf, does not appear in the text, and the decision does not specify which garments fall under the secularism clause. The pro-government Azerbaijani news agencies APA and Report did not describe the rule as a headscarf ban, while other Azerbaijani outlets did, the Sözcü news website reported.

The decision assigns questions about compliance to each school’s pedagogical council and requires parents to ensure that their children follow the dress rules. It does not specify whether a pupil can be barred from class or what penalties apply. The Cabinet and the Ministry of Science and Education did not respond to questions about the decision, BBC’s Azerbaijani service reported.

The parent of a student who wears a headscarf told Turkish Minute that there was “nothing to talk about” and that their daughter attends school without any problems. The account contrasts with exclusion complaints at other schools.

The decision follows a law signed by Aliyev in July that added national character and secularism to the principles of general education. A presidential decree on July 22 instructed the Cabinet to update the dress rules accordingly.

Girls wearing headscarves were turned away from Sumgait School No. 6, near Baku, on September 28, Operativ TV reported. Mustafa Yeneroğlu, an independent İstanbul lawmaker and former member of Erdoğan’s ruling Justice and Development Party (AKP), cited footage shot outside the school and said the images “wounded all our consciences.”

The same outlet reported on September 29 that the school’s restriction had been reversed following parents’ protests, citing social media accounts. Its report did not include an official statement confirming the reversal.

Yeneroğlu argued in a September 30 statement that Turkey paid a heavy price for its own headscarf ban, which left hundreds of thousands of young women out of school. He described the practice in Azerbaijan as having grown from “individual and arbitrary” acts into “systematic state policy.”

He also argued that Decision No. 309 is not a neutral dress rule because it allows national clothing while the headscarf is treated as banned. The national clothing exception applies to events concerning Azerbaijani history, culture and values, with school council approval. Leaving councils to decide what is prohibited invites arbitrary enforcement, and making parents responsible for compliance forces families to choose between education and faith, Yeneroğlu warned. He cited constitutional protections for equality and freedom of conscience and Azerbaijan’s obligations under the European Convention on Human Rights.

The national dress question had surfaced before the decision. In the spring, reports citing the Azerbaijani Yeni Müsavat outlet suggested that the hijab would be restricted in official settings and replaced by the kelaghayi, a traditional Azerbaijani silk headscarf. The outlet’s director, Rauf Arifoğlu, later denied the claim, acknowledging that the report was based on unverified information.

Yeneroğlu urged Erdoğan, relevant ministers and Parliament Speaker Numan Kurtulmuş to speak out and raise the issue with Azerbaijani officials. He had made a similar call on April 10, urging Erdoğan to take the matter up with Aliyev.

The pro-government Yeni Akit daily ran the story under a headline comparing it to Turkey’s February 28 era. The term refers to the 1997 military-backed campaign against political Islam, which intensified enforcement of headscarf restrictions in universities and the civil service.

The Turkish government dismantled headscarf restrictions between 2010 and 2017. Erdoğan built his image as a champion of religious women through his campaign to end restrictions that had excluded them from education and public employment, winning support among conservative women. He repeatedly invoked the former bans to attack the secular opposition, presenting the freedoms secured under his government as a reason for voters to support his party.

In İstanbul, the Freedom of Thought and Education Rights Association (Özgür-Der) organized a protest outside the Azerbaijani Consulate on October 3. Demonstrators carried a banner reading “No to the hijab ban in Azerbaijan!” Former Özgür-Der chair Hülya Şekerci urged Turkish officials to press Baku to end the restrictions, the Haksöz Haber news website reported. She also referred to unconfirmed claims that Turkish officials had warned Azerbaijan.

Azerbaijan’s Media and Broadcasting Council has threatened measures against those it accuses of conducting a campaign against the country’s alliance with Turkey. In a September 26 statement, the council described posts in the Turkish media and on social media as part of a coordinated campaign by “certain circles.” It raised the possibility of barring the organizers from entering Azerbaijan. A parliamentary commission on foreign interference also investigated the alleged campaign.

The commission accused unnamed groups in Turkey of using real and fake accounts and targeted advertising to turn Azerbaijanis against their government and the alliance, Joseph Epstein wrote in a September 29 analysis published by the Yorktown Institute, a US think tank. Neither the commission’s statement nor the regulator’s statement named the headscarf dispute. Some Turkish posts criticize and threaten Aliyev directly, BBC’s Azerbaijani service reported.

Every child should study under the same conditions regardless of social, ethnic or religious background, Ceyhun Mammadov, a member of parliament’s education committee, told BBC’s Azerbaijani service. The outlet reported that the Cabinet and the education ministry had not answered its questions about the new rules.

The dispute did not start in September. Teachers and parents at School No. 269 in Baku’s Khatai district described demands for pupils and teachers to remove their headscarves after an inspection in February, Yeni Avaz reported. The school responded that its pedagogical council had chosen one of four uniform designs and that teachers must follow its dress rules.

By March, similar cases had occurred in Sumgait, Mehdiabad and Imishli. The head of Mehdiabad School No. 3 reportedly excluded 23 pupils who wore the hijab on March 10. Journalist and religious freedom activist Arzu Abdulla said a school director told her the order came “directly from high ranking officials.” On April 1, she said restrictions had been lifted the day before, but predicted the ban would soon become official.

Complaints also concern the Yolçubəyli village school in Sabirabad and School No. 24 in Ganja, Qəzetçi reported on October 1, citing Abdulla’s accounts. The Sabirabad director denied the allegation. Abdulla described a September 30 ultimatum there, while the pressure in Ganja reportedly began in winter and continued.

The March cases followed a March 5 drone attack on Azerbaijan’s Nakhchivan exclave, which Baku blamed on Iran. Iran denied responsibility. Arrests and restrictions of this kind tend to follow tension between Baku and Tehran, rights defender Arif Yunus told Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty (RFE/RL).

In April a Sumgait parent described being told that her child must remove her headscarf or leave school, JAMnews reported. The outlet noted that the 2022 Cabinet decision on school uniforms did not mention the hijab and cited constitutional protections for education and freedom of conscience.

Azerbaijan’s education ministry had said in 2023 that dress rules were set by each school’s pedagogical council. Education expert Nadir Israfilov warned at the time that leaving the issue to schools would cause confusion. The ministry sought to enforce a school headscarf restriction in late 2010, when then-education minister Misir Mardanov insisted that pupils wear only the prescribed uniform. Police dispersed a protest of hundreds outside the ministry on December 10, 2010. Contemporary reporting in January 2011 described the de facto restriction as continuing.

Theologian Abgül Suleymanov, who criticized the headscarf restrictions on Facebook, was detained near his home on the evening of September 22, the same day as Erdoğan’s speech. His family links the detention to those posts. A court sentenced him to 30 days in administrative detention on a charge of intentionally disobeying a police officer. He was previously imprisoned from 2011 to 2022 following earlier headscarf protests.

Another theologian, Fazil Ahmad, described a potential ban as Islamophobia and warned that it could create a rift between society and the state in comments to Tehsil365 on September 22.

Turkish Minute sought comment from students affected by the restrictions, but they declined to speak even anonymously, citing security concerns.

Speaking at the New Azerbaijan Party’s congress in Baku on October 2, Aliyev declared that Azerbaijan builds its foreign policy so that no one interferes in its affairs and needs no one’s “help or patronage.” He did not identify Turkey or the headscarf dispute. The Turkish Rast Haber outlet interpreted the remarks as a warning to Ankara.

Turkey and Azerbaijan describe their relationship as “One Nation, Two States.” They formalized allied relations in the 2021 Shusha Declaration, which covers security and defense cooperation. The Turkish presidency’s Center for Countering Disinformation (DMM) used the slogan in a March 7 statement warning against what it described as online attempts to damage ties.
