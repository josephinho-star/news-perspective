Erdoğan ally confirms newly built home for jailed PKK founder after official denial

2026-09-14 14:44

Nationalist Movement Party (MHP) leader Devlet Bahçeli, a governing ally of President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, has confirmed that a house was built for imprisoned Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK) founder Abdullah Öcalan at the İmralı island prison, journalist Nagehan Alçı said, months after Turkey’s justice minister denied that a residence was being constructed for him.

Alçı said she asked Bahçeli about reports concerning the building during an on-the-record meeting at MHP headquarters in Ankara.

Bahçeli told her that “a house was built for Abdullah Öcalan on İmralı Island” and gave her permission to publish the information, Alçı said.

He described it as a home with a front garden, living space and conditions that would allow Öcalan to follow an ordinary daily routine.

“A comfortable house was built,” Bahçeli said, according to Alçı.

Bahçeli said Öcalan had refused to move into the building “because he wants status.”

He said Öcalan wants the bodies established under a new law governing the peace initiative to begin working and expects to receive a role in one of them before moving into the house.

The disputed “status” refers to a defined position for Öcalan in talks meant to oversee the PKK’s weapons handover, breakup and transition to political activity. It is separate from his status as a prisoner.

Alçı said unnamed state sources described a two-story building inside the prison. The lower floor was designed as an office and meeting area, while the upper floor contains living quarters.

She said the building has a walled garden with both soil and concrete sections. She also reported that Öcalan wants relatives to assist him and is seeking a secretariat made up of people from Kurdish political groups, women’s organizations and Europe.

Those details came from Alçı’s unnamed sources and were not part of the remarks she attributed directly to Bahçeli.

Former Justice and Development Party (AKP) lawmaker Şamil Tayyar later called the structure a 250-square-meter duplex villa with a garden about twice that size.

Tayyar wrote on X that it was designed for both accommodation and work and has internet access, video communications and other equipment.

He also claimed that Öcalan already uses its office when necessary, although he has not moved into the living area.

Tayyar said Erdoğan had rejected Öcalan’s request for status because the president did not want to approve it before the PKK completed the weapons handover. The presidency has not responded to his account.

The revelations conflict with a denial issued by Justice Minister Akın Gürlek on April 1, when the existence of a home for Öcalan was first raised by Kurdish politicians.

“There is no such thing there. There is an administrative compound,” Gürlek said.

Gürlek said new buildings could be constructed as part of the compound when needed but denied that a residence was being built specifically for Öcalan.

Several news outlets republished Gürlek’s remarks after Alçı disclosed her conversation with Bahçeli, presenting them as a response to the new report. The statement was made more than five months earlier and therefore was not a direct answer to Bahçeli’s reported confirmation.

Neither the Justice Ministry nor the presidency had issued a new statement about the building as of Monday.

Abdulkadir Selvi, a columnist for the pro-government Hürriyet daily, offered another description on Monday. He rejected the term “duplex villa” and described the building as a work and meeting space inside the prison that could be used to coordinate the peace initiative, T24 reported.

Selvi’s account did not deny that a new facility had been built. It differed from Bahçeli’s reported description of a home with ordinary living conditions and Tayyar’s account of a duplex villa.

The dispute may partly concern how the government classifies a building that contains both living and work space. The government has not explained its official purpose or legal status.

Reports about the facility predate Bahçeli’s remarks.

Peoples’ Equality and Democracy Party (DEM Party) Co-chair Tülay Hatimoğulları said in March that her party had received information that a residence had been completed but that Öcalan had not moved into it.

Hatimoğulları later said the question was not whether Öcalan should receive a new home but whether Ankara would recognize him as the chief negotiator and allow him to meet freely with journalists, academics and political representatives.

She quoted Öcalan as saying he did not intend to move “from an old prison to a new prison” without a defined role.

DEM Party Deputy Chairman Tayip Temel said in July that Öcalan would move to the new compound with a secretariat if parliament passed the expected legal framework and the necessary conditions were met.

Parliament passed the Strengthening National Solidarity and Social Integration law on August 10.

The law creates a Follow-up and Evaluation Board chaired by the vice president and made up of cabinet ministers, the head of the National Intelligence Organization (MİT) and other state officials.

It allows the board to form sub-commissions, invite people to meetings and assign individuals to help move the process forward inside the PKK. It does not name Öcalan or grant him a position.

The law also provides no route for Öcalan’s release. It excludes people convicted of intentional killing and those given life or aggravated life sentences for offenses committed before June 1, 2005, from provisions allowing sentences to be suspended.

Öcalan has been imprisoned since 1999 and is serving an aggravated life sentence. His original death sentence was changed to life imprisonment after Turkey abolished the death penalty.

Bahçeli has separately called for Öcalan to receive the “right to hope,” a legal principle requiring life prisoners to have some prospect of a sentence review. It does not provide automatic release, and the August law contains no such measure for Öcalan.

The reported house is inside İmralı Prison and surrounded by walls, according to Alçı. A transfer there would therefore not, by itself, amount to release or house arrest.

The claim prompted objections from opposition politicians and organizations representing military veterans and families of soldiers killed in the conflict.

Nationalist opposition İYİ (Good) Party leader Müsavat Dervişoğlu questioned whether such an arrangement would comply with the rules governing aggravated life sentences.

“If a convict can decide how the state treats him, what remains of law and the state?” Dervişoğlu asked.

Pakize Akbaba, head of the Association of Mothers of Fallen Soldiers, contrasted the alleged villa with the death of her son.

“They built him a two-story villa. My son lies two meters beneath sacred soil,” Akbaba said, according to T24.

Öcalan is one of the most reviled figures in Turkish public life. Nationalist politicians and much of Turkey’s mainstream media routinely label him the “baby killer,” referring to civilians, among them children, killed in PKK attacks.

The PKK began its armed campaign against the Turkish state in 1984. The conflict has killed more than 40,000 people, and Turkey and its Western allies designate the PKK as a terrorist organization.

Bahçeli’s role in the current initiative marks a shift for a politician who spent decades attacking Öcalan and opposing talks with the PKK.

In October 2024 Bahçeli proposed that Öcalan be allowed to speak to pro-Kurdish lawmakers in parliament if he called on the PKK to lay down arms and disband. Öcalan issued that call in February 2025, and the PKK later announced that it was ending its armed campaign and dissolving its organizational structure.

A DEM Party delegation met with Öcalan over the weekend. In a message released Monday, he called for legal and political steps to secure the peace initiative but made no public reference to the house or his reported refusal to move.
